Mothering Men With Unrealized Potential: Over-Functioning in Love

Contributed by Patricia on August 14, 2026

She does not think of herself as his mother. She thinks she is being supportive.

She sees an intelligent, attractive, fundamentally good man whose life would improve enormously if he would only find stable work, take better care of himself, address his mental health, become more intentional about dating, follow through on his plans, communicate more effectively, or stop sabotaging himself. None of these changes seem impossible, and in fact they may appear painfully obvious. So she starts helping.

She reminds him. She encourages him. She researches solutions and recommends books, doctors, therapists, podcasts, clothing, career strategies, organizational systems, and healthier routines. She explains how his behaviour affects her, and when that does not work, she explains it more clearly. When he becomes defensive, she softens the delivery. When he still does not act, she becomes firmer.

Eventually, she is no longer dating the man. She is managing the project of who he might become, quietly taking on responsibility for developing another adult into the partner she needs him to be, well past the point of caring for him through an ordinary difficult season. That shift is what it means to mother a man with unrealized potential.

The Over-Functioning, Under-Functioning Cycle

Relationship dynamics are co-created, even when responsibility is not distributed equally.

When one partner repeatedly fails to initiate, remember, plan, repair, or follow through, the other partner faces a choice: allow something important to remain undone, or pick up the slack.

At first, picking up the slack seems practical. Then it becomes expected.

The more she does, the less urgently he has to do. The less he does, the more convinced she becomes that she cannot rely on him. She starts reminding, monitoring, questioning, correcting, and taking over. He increasingly experiences her as critical or controlling and may become defensive, passive, avoidant, or secretive.

She concludes that he is incapable.

He concludes that nothing he does will ever be good enough.

This begins to resemble the demand-withdraw pattern studied in couples research: one partner pursues change through requests, pressure, complaints, or criticism, while the other avoids, defends, disengages, or withdraws. A meta-analysis of 74 studies involving more than 14,000 participants found a meaningful association between demand-withdraw interactions and poorer individual, relational, and communication outcomes.

The trap is that each person's behaviour makes the other person's behaviour more likely.

Her pursuit intensifies because he is not responding. His withdrawal intensifies because he feels pursued. She over-functions because he under-functions. He under-functions even more because she has taken over.

Why Women Become the Over-Functioning Partner

This dynamic can occur in relationships of any gender. However, women in heterosexual relationships are particularly vulnerable to it because girls and women are often socialized to notice needs, anticipate problems, maintain relationships, and take responsibility for the emotional climate around them.

Research on cognitive labour describes the invisible work of anticipating needs, identifying possible solutions, making decisions, and monitoring whether those solutions were carried out. In heterosexual households, women tend to perform more of this cognitive labour, especially the anticipating and monitoring stages, the very parts that make someone feel as though she must always keep the entire system in her head.

Research on invisible household labour has similarly found that feeling disproportionately responsible for managing family life is associated with greater role overload, poorer personal well-being, and lower relationship satisfaction among women. Although much of this research concerns established households and parenting, the pattern can begin much earlier in dating: she notices what needs to happen, and because she notices it first, she gradually becomes responsible for making it happen.

For some women, over-functioning is also an attempt to create safety.

"If I plan carefully enough, nothing will fall apart."

"If I explain myself perfectly, he will understand."

"If I help him become more stable, the relationship will become stable."

"If I can solve the problem, I will not have to lose the person."

The behaviour may be controlling, but the feeling underneath it is often fear.

What Mothering a Partner Looks Like

Mothering can appear caring from the outside because much of it consists of useful behaviour.

It may look like:

  • Reminding him about appointments, deadlines, chores, or commitments.
  • Researching treatment for problems he has not taken responsibility for addressing.
  • Planning every date because otherwise no plan materializes.
  • Managing difficult conversations while also managing his emotional response to them.
  • Repeatedly explaining basic relationship expectations.
  • Coaching him on grooming, clothing, employment, finances, health, friendships, or communication.
  • Doing tasks herself because asking, reminding, and checking his work feels more exhausting.
  • Protecting him from the consequences of his own inaction.
  • Praising minimal effort because she is afraid criticism will make him withdraw again.
  • Remaining attached to what he could become if he finally followed her advice.

Partners are supposed to help each other, and healthy relationships involve mutual influence, encouragement, practical assistance, and growth. Trouble starts when one person becomes the permanent source of motivation, structure, foresight, emotional regulation, and accountability for the other. At that point, she is no longer beside him. She is carrying him.

When Supporting His Growth Becomes Coaching Him

There is a healthy version of helping a partner grow.

Researchers sometimes describe the Michelangelo phenomenon: close partners can help bring out one another's ideal selves by seeing, encouraging, and responding to qualities the person already wants to develop. In healthy versions of this process, the partner is not imposing a new identity. They are affirming the other person's own values and goals. This kind of partner affirmation has been associated with movement toward the ideal self and better couple well-being.

The crucial question is: whose ideal is she trying to create?

There is a difference between:

"I know starting this business matters to you. How can I support the plan you have chosen?"

and:

"You need to stop working on that project, get a conventional job, dress differently, see a therapist, organize your apartment, and follow the plan I created for you."

The first supports his agency. The second replaces it.

Research on goal support finds that autonomy-supportive encouragement, meaning listening, understanding the person's perspective, and supporting goals they have genuinely chosen, is associated with better goal progress, relationship quality, and well-being. Directive support can be less effective when it makes the recipient feel controlled rather than internally motivated.

A romantic partner can be an ally. She should not have to become his life coach, therapist, career counsellor, stylist, project manager, and motivational speaker.

How Mothering Makes a Man Feel Inadequate

A woman may begin coaching a man because she believes in him.

He may experience the same behaviour as evidence that she does not actually like him.

Every new suggestion can carry an unintended message:

"You are almost acceptable."

"You would be a wonderful partner after several renovations."

"The person you are today is not the person I want."

Research on partner regulation supports this tension. The more people perceive a discrepancy between their partner and their ideal standards, the more strongly they may attempt to change the partner. Those regulation attempts are intertwined with lower perceived consistency between the real partner and the desired one, which can reduce relationship quality.

Some attempts to influence a partner do produce change. Research following couples over a year found that direct communication could predict later improvement in targeted behaviours, particularly when the communication was positive and direct. But the same research also showed that increasing problem severity predicted lower relationship quality. In other words, asking clearly for meaningful change can be appropriate, but living in a permanent campaign to remodel one another is not.

A man who constantly feels assessed may stop experiencing his partner as a loving witness and start experiencing her as a performance review.

He may also begin resisting changes he would otherwise have made simply because making them now feels like surrendering his autonomy.

How Coaching Him Changes the Way She Sees Him

Mothering harms the woman's perception of her partner too.

When she first met him, she may have found his unconventional career charming, his casual clothing endearing, his eccentric habits interesting, or his uncertainty understandable.

Once she begins managing those qualities, they become evidence in a case she is building against him.

She stops noticing his warmth and notices the appointment he forgot. She stops seeing creativity and sees instability. She stops seeing spontaneity and sees inadequate planning. She stops seeing a man she admires and begins seeing a list of deficiencies awaiting correction.

This is one reason dating for potential is so dangerous. The more attention she invests in the imagined future version, the more disappointing the present version becomes.

Research on ideal standards finds that greater consistency between the partner someone perceives and the partner they ideally want is associated with greater acceptance and relationship satisfaction. When the gap becomes central to the relationship, both acceptance and satisfaction suffer.

She may have loved him as he was in the beginning. By repeatedly concentrating on who he should become, she gradually teaches herself that who he is now is not enough.

When the Gap Is Real: How Over-Accommodation Can Become Entitlement

Not every disappointment is a distortion of perception. Sometimes the mismatch is real. One partner arrives with stability, initiative, emotional insight, a developed career, and a demonstrated willingness to commit, while the other has not yet built comparable stability, self-direction, or relational effort, and expects love to fill the gap.

A woman in this position may assume that being chosen by someone capable will inspire her partner to rise to meet her. Sometimes it does. But continued access to her care, without consequences for his low effort, can send a different message: that his current level of effort is already enough to keep her. Rather than crediting her generosity, he may fold her continued affection into his own self-appraisal as proof of his desirability.

The implicit lesson may no longer be:

"I need to rise to meet the opportunity of this relationship."

It may become:

"If a woman like this continues choosing me, I must already be doing enough."

Gratitude research shows that appreciation normally works as a cycle: a partner's caring behaviour is recognized as responsiveness, that recognition produces gratitude, and gratitude motivates further care in return. When one person's contribution becomes invisible or simply expected, that cycle breaks down. A woman's contribution includes her time, especially the years spent building a life with someone during what are often her prime years, and that cost tends to go unrecognized. Research also suggests that men generally benefit more from partnering with an accomplished, high-value woman than women benefit from partnering with a man who is still a project. The partner carrying more of the relationship is therefore likely to experience the imbalance as a crisis long before the partner benefiting from it does. From his position, the arrangement can look entirely functional, since his needs are being met and the relationship continues. From hers, it can become increasingly exhausting and degrading to be dating a man who she feels is taking her for granted.

None of this makes a woman responsible for a man's entitlement. His choices remain his own. But chronic over-accommodation can shield low effort from the consequences that might otherwise prompt him to grow. Being chosen by someone impressive is not proof that a person is equally prepared to sustain what she is offering. Access to her is not an achievement he can continue benefiting from indefinitely while contributing less. Her attraction should not be mistaken for a permanent credential validating his current way of living. Sometimes the answer is not gentler coaching. It is better selection.

Date the Man, Not the Renovation Plan

One of the most important questions in dating is this: would I still choose this person if nothing significant about him changed?

Not if he eventually earns more. Not if he becomes more generous. Not if he goes to therapy. Not if he becomes healthier, more ambitious, better dressed, more emotionally regulated, more organized, or more commitment-minded.

Would you choose the person currently standing in front of you?

People can change, partners can grow together, and nobody arrives in a relationship perfectly developed. But change should be treated as a possibility, not counted on like guaranteed future income in the relationship budget. Dating someone for potential is a bit like buying a house from a beautiful architectural rendering with no completed foundation: the final result may be magnificent, or it may remain a drawing indefinitely. Psychological maturity means experiencing relational reality as it actually is, rather than dating an imagined future and asking the present to keep making the payments on it.

"Training Season" Is Over

There comes a point when a woman has to stop auditioning for the position of Head of Male Development.

Dua Lipa's 2024 song "Training Season" captures this feeling well. The track is about being tired of coaching a partner into readiness and wanting someone who arrives already capable of showing up, rather than someone who needs to be developed into the role. The phrase has since become shorthand in dating culture for exactly the pattern described throughout this article.

She does not need to teach another adult how to plan a date, maintain his health, seek appropriate help, communicate respectfully, become financially responsible, or decide whether he wants a committed relationship.

That does not mean she expects perfection. It means she looks for evidence of self-direction.

A relationship-ready man may still be imperfect, but he notices his own problems. He has goals that belong to him. He seeks information without needing to be assigned it. When feedback is appropriate, he can consider it without collapsing, retaliating, or requiring his partner to tolerate three weeks of him convincing her to take it back.

Research on commitment readiness suggests that people differ meaningfully in whether they feel prepared to enter and maintain a committed relationship. Greater readiness is associated with stronger interest in forming a close relationship, more active pursuit of one, greater later commitment, and better stability-related processes.

The point is not to find a man "trained by another woman." It is to find a man who has participated in his own development.

Why Does He Sometimes Marry the Next Woman?

There is a popular story in dating culture: a woman spends years explaining everything that is wrong. She encourages him, argues with him, gives him resources, and finally leaves after delivering a devastating final review. He then improves his life and marries the next woman, sometimes very quickly.

It can feel as though the first woman completed all the training and the next woman collected the finished product.

Research does support a less dramatic explanation: timing and readiness matter. A person may encounter one partner when he is defensive, avoidant, immature, overwhelmed, or simply not prepared to commit. The breakup may become one of several experiences that eventually contribute to greater readiness. When he later meets someone during a period when commitment feels timely, he may move differently.

That does not mean the earlier woman failed. It does not mean the later woman was better.

Most importantly, it does not create a duty for women to operate as unpaid rehabilitation centres so that future women can receive more functional partners. Adults are responsible for learning from their own consequences.

But What Happens If Women Stop Training Men?

Men will have to develop themselves.

They can learn through fathers, brothers, friends, mentors, work, books, therapy, faith communities, previous relationships, honest feedback, natural consequences, and self-reflection.

Women already do enormous amounts of personal improvement work. They are encouraged to analyze their attachment styles, regulate their reactions, communicate gently, build careers, maintain friendships, improve their health, manage their appearance, and seek therapy.

Men are equally capable of looking at their own lives and asking:

"What am I repeatedly losing because of how I show up?"

"What feedback have several people given me?"

"What kind of partner do I want to become?"

"What help do I need to pursue without a woman arranging it for me?"

Growth that occurs only while a woman is pushing is not yet self-sustaining growth.

How to Stop Mothering a Man You Want to Keep

Sometimes the relationship is worth preserving. Perhaps the man is loving, compatible, and generally responsible, but the couple has slipped into an unhealthy division where she manages too much and he initiates too little. The solution is not to become colder. It is to stop confusing love with over-responsibility.

Say What You Need Clearly

Do not hint, test, lecture, diagnose, or create a ten-step development plan.

Say:

"I need a partner who shares responsibility for planning our time."

"I am no longer willing to remind you repeatedly about commitments."

"I need you to take responsibility for seeking help with this issue."

"I want a relationship where both people initiate repair."

Express the need, the impact, and the boundary. Then stop campaigning.

Step Back and Give Him Room

After communicating, give him room to respond.

Do not immediately send articles, identify providers, draft the email, schedule the appointment, make the spreadsheet, and check whether he followed through.

Research distinguishes autonomy-supportive help from controlling or directive support. People are more likely to sustain goals that feel internally chosen than goals pursued mainly to stop another person's pressure.

His response gives you valuable information. He may step forward imperfectly. He may do nothing. Both are answers.

Let Some Balls Drop

Over-functioners often prevent failure so efficiently that the under-functioning partner never experiences the real consequences of under-functioning.

If she always makes the reservation, the couple always has somewhere to eat. If she always initiates reconciliation, the relationship never remains ruptured long enough for him to decide whether he will repair it. If she always remembers the obligation, he never has to build a system for remembering.

Letting a ball drop is not sabotage. It does not mean neglecting children, endangering finances, or violating responsibilities she explicitly agreed to carry.

It means no longer rescuing another capable adult from predictable consequences that belong to him.

Sometimes he needs to find out the hard way, not because she is punishing him, but because reality teaches lessons that repeated explanations cannot.

Learn to Hold the Discomfort

This is often the hardest part. She may see the forgotten reservation coming from three days away, and every part of her wants to intervene. Stepping back may produce anxiety, disappointment, embarrassment, or inconvenience. But self-trust means knowing:

"If he disappoints me, I can survive being disappointed. If the plan fails, I can cope with the failed plan. If he does not step up, I would rather know than spend years disguising that fact with my own effort."

Holding discomfort is not self-abandonment. Over-functioning to prevent all discomfort is.

Appreciate Real Effort

When he initiates, follows through, repairs, or takes responsibility, acknowledge it.

Not with exaggerated praise for basic adulthood, and not as though he is a child earning a sticker. Simply let the good register:

"Thank you for planning this. It made me feel cared for."

"I noticed that you handled that without being reminded."

"I appreciated how you came back to repair the conversation."

Daily research on gratitude in romantic relationships has found that grateful interactions predict greater connection and relationship satisfaction for both the person expressing gratitude and the person receiving it.

Positive attention helps partners know which behaviours create closeness. But appreciation cannot replace standards.

Watch What He Does Without Management

The goal is not to manipulate him into changing. The goal is to stop interfering with the evidence.

When you stop over-functioning, you discover whether he was waiting for room to contribute, had become accustomed to your management, or simply does not intend to provide what you need.

That answer may hurt. It is still better than maintaining a relationship through invisible life support.

When There Is Nothing Left to Reinforce

Sometimes a woman steps back and the man does not step forward. Plans disappear, repair stops, and problems remain unaddressed, and her resentment grows because she is no longer distracted by the project of fixing him. At that point, the question shifts from how she can motivate him to why she is choosing a relationship that requires her to become someone she does not want to be.

If she cannot find anything to appreciate, trust, respect, or rely upon, the relationship may not need better coaching. It may need an ending.

Do not keep reminding him to text you first, to check on you, or to make time for you. Do not ask him to bring you flowers, plan dates, or show you off to the world. If you have to beg for effort, is that satisfying love or a disappointment? The right man will not need instructions. He will naturally do the things that make you feel safe, cared for, and valued. He will remember the little details you mention, the things that make you smile, and the moments that matter to you. He will put in effort because he truly wants to, not because you asked him to.

When a man genuinely loves you, his actions will speak louder than any promise. You will not have to remind him how to love you, because he will already be doing it, without you asking twice. And if he shows you again and again that he is incapable or uninterested in being a good man to you, let him go.

It is time to nurture an abundance mindset. This man who is not realizing his potential is not the last man on earth with whom you could find your happily ever after. Trust that being alone is survivable, disappointment can be contained, and compatibility cannot be manufactured. Let people show you who they are without over-functioning to help them succeed, and if that person is not compatible with you, someone out there already is.

How Therapy Helps Women Stop Over-Functioning

Therapy can help a woman identify what she is afraid will happen if she stops managing everything.

Will she be abandoned? Will she feel irresponsible? Will the relationship reveal itself to be less secure than she hoped? Will she have to grieve the future she imagined?

Therapy can also help distinguish a request from an attempt to control, support from rescue, patience from self-abandonment, and ordinary imperfection from chronic incompatibility.

The goal is not to make women less loving. It is to help them love without disappearing into responsibility for another adult's life.

Common Questions About Mothering Men

Is it wrong to encourage a partner to improve?

No. Healthy partners influence and support one another. It becomes unhealthy when the goal belongs primarily to you, when you are doing more work toward it than your partner is, or when the relationship has become organized around his improvement rather than mutual connection.

Should I accept a man exactly as he is even when his behaviour hurts me?

Acceptance does not mean unlimited tolerance. You can see someone accurately, communicate what you require, and leave when the current reality is incompatible with your needs. Acceptance means giving up the fantasy that enough persuasion can transform him.

What if he says my requests make him feel that he is not good enough?

Take that seriously without abandoning your standards. Consider whether you are offering a clear request about behaviour or continuously communicating that his whole identity needs renovation. He is responsible for managing his own feelings, and you are responsible for deciding whether you genuinely respect the person you are dating.

How long should I wait for him to step up?

Look for movement rather than promises. Meaningful movement includes self-initiated action, consistent follow-through, openness to appropriate help, and improvement that continues without constant supervision. A reasonable timeline depends on the seriousness of the issue, but indefinite potential is not evidence of change.

Am I enabling under-functioning by doing everything myself?

Possibly. When compensating for a partner prevents him from encountering consequences, developing skills, or recognizing how much labour you provide, it can unintentionally maintain the imbalance rather than resolve it.

The work is not about learning how to explain yourself even better. You are allowed to support a man, believe in him, and appreciate his potential. What you do not have to do is build the man, carry the relationship, or absorb every consequence on his behalf while you wait for that potential to become real. Training season is over. Date the man who is here, and once you stop managing him, let him show you who that actually is. If he does not show up for you appropriately, find someone who does.
