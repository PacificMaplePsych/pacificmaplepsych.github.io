What Is PMDD (Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

PMDD can bring severe low mood and thoughts of suicide in the days before a period. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, or PMDD, is a severe mood condition linked to the menstrual cycle. Symptoms appear in the luteal phase, usually one to two weeks before a period, and improve within a few days after bleeding begins. PMDD is much more intense than typical premenstrual syndrome. It involves significant emotional symptoms, such as depression, irritability, anxiety, and mood swings, that can disrupt work, relationships, and daily life. PMDD is recognized as a depressive disorder in diagnostic manuals. It is not a sign of weakness or overreaction, and effective treatments are available.

What PMDD Looks Like

Emotional symptoms may include:

  • Marked mood swings, sudden sadness, or tearfulness.
  • Intense irritability or anger, and conflict with others.
  • Depressed mood, hopelessness, or feelings of worthlessness.
  • Anxiety, tension, or feeling on edge.
  • Sensitivity to rejection.

Other symptoms may include:

  • Loss of interest in usual activities.
  • Difficulty concentrating.
  • Fatigue and low energy.
  • Changes in appetite or food cravings.
  • Sleeping too much or too little.
  • Feeling overwhelmed or out of control.
  • Physical symptoms, such as bloating, breast tenderness, and aches.

Many people describe feeling like a different person before their period, as explored on the page about mood crashes before a period.

Why It Happens

PMDD is not caused by abnormal hormone levels. Instead, research suggests that people with PMDD have an unusual sensitivity in the brain to normal hormonal changes across the menstrual cycle, particularly shifts in estrogen and progesterone and their effects on serotonin and other brain chemicals. Genetics may play a role. Stress and a history of trauma may increase vulnerability. PMDD can begin any time after periods start and may change across life stages, sometimes worsening during perimenopause.

PMDD can also worsen existing conditions. People with depression, anxiety, or ADHD may notice their symptoms intensify before their period, a pattern called premenstrual exacerbation, which differs from PMDD itself. Tracking symptoms helps clarify the pattern.

Because symptoms come and go with the cycle, PMDD can be confusing for both the person and those around them. Relationships may feel strained for part of the month and fine for the rest. Understanding the cyclical pattern can reduce self-blame and help loved ones offer support at the right times.

Planning ahead can make a real difference. Some people schedule demanding tasks for the weeks after their period, build in extra rest before it, and let close people know when a harder phase is approaching.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary PMS. PMDD is more severe and significantly disrupts life.
  • Not caused by abnormal hormones. It reflects sensitivity to normal hormonal changes.
  • Not something to simply tolerate. Effective treatments exist.
  • Not the same as ongoing depression. PMDD symptoms follow the menstrual cycle and ease after a period begins. Ongoing low mood is described on the page about major depressive disorder.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Diagnosis usually involves tracking symptoms daily for at least two menstrual cycles, to confirm the timing and severity. A physician can check for other conditions. Treatment options include certain antidepressants, which can be taken daily or only during the luteal phase, and hormonal treatments that stabilize the cycle. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps manage mood, thinking patterns, and relationship stress during symptomatic days. Regular exercise, sleep, reducing caffeine and alcohol, and planning lighter schedules before a period can help. Communicating with loved ones about the cycle can reduce conflict.

Common Questions About PMDD

What is PMDD?

PMDD is a severe mood condition linked to the menstrual cycle, with depression, irritability, and anxiety before a period.

When do PMDD symptoms occur?

Symptoms usually appear one to two weeks before a period and ease within a few days after bleeding starts.

Is PMDD caused by a hormone imbalance?

No. Research suggests PMDD reflects unusual brain sensitivity to normal hormonal changes, not abnormal hormone levels.

Why do clinicians ask me to track symptoms for two cycles?

Daily tracking over at least two cycles confirms that symptoms follow the premenstrual pattern and shows how severe they are.

Can PMDD cause suicidal thoughts?

Yes. Some people experience severe low mood and suicidal thoughts before their period. Crisis support is always available.

What is premenstrual exacerbation?

It describes worsening of an existing condition, such as depression or ADHD, before a period, which differs from PMDD.

Can antidepressants help PMDD?

Yes. Certain antidepressants can be effective, sometimes taken only during the two weeks before a period.

Does PMDD get worse in perimenopause?

For some people, yes, as hormonal fluctuations become more pronounced.

What helps loved ones understand the monthly pattern?

Sharing a symptom calendar, how symptoms follow your cycle, and what helps on difficult days makes the pattern easier to understand.

What role does CBT play alongside medical treatment for PMDD?

Cognitive behavioural therapy can help manage mood, thoughts, and relationship stress during symptomatic phases.
