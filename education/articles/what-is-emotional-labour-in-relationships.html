What Is Emotional Labour in Relationships

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Emotional labour in relationships refers to the often invisible work of managing emotions, anticipating needs, remembering, planning, and keeping a household and relationship running smoothly. The term originally described workers managing their emotions on the job, but it is now widely used to describe unpaid emotional and mental work at home. When this work falls mostly on one partner, it can lead to exhaustion, resentment, and feeling unseen. Recognizing emotional labour is an important step toward sharing it more fairly.

What Emotional Labour Looks Like

  • Remembering birthdays, appointments, school forms, and social plans.
  • Noticing when supplies are low or tasks need doing, and planning for them.
  • Managing a partner's moods, smoothing over conflict, or keeping the peace.
  • Maintaining family and social relationships, such as sending cards and arranging visits.
  • Anticipating children's emotional needs and coordinating their care.
  • Being the one who initiates conversations about the relationship.

This work is often invisible because it happens in someone's head: tracking, anticipating, and worrying. It is sometimes called the mental load. In families with young children, it can be a major source of resentment toward a partner.

Why It Happens

Emotional labour often falls unevenly because of gender expectations, family roles learned in childhood, and differences in how partners notice or prioritize tasks. People raised to attend to others' feelings may take it on automatically. People who feel responsible for a partner's emotions may carry an especially heavy emotional share. Because the work is invisible, the partner doing less may not realize how much is being done. ADHD, depression, or other challenges in one partner can also shift more of the load onto the other.

Over time, carrying most of the emotional labour can lead to burnout, loneliness, and a sense of being more of a manager than a partner. The partner doing less may feel criticized without understanding why. Naming the invisible work helps both people see it clearly.

The partner carrying less of the load is often surprised by how much there is. A useful exercise is for each person to write down everything they track or manage in a typical week, including the thinking behind tasks. Comparing lists often makes the imbalance obvious without either person needing to argue about it, and it gives a concrete starting point for sharing responsibilities.

What It Is Not

  • Not just chores. Emotional labour includes the planning, noticing, and emotional management behind tasks, not only the tasks themselves.
  • Not always a sign of an uncaring partner. Many partners simply have not been taught to notice or share this work.
  • Not only a gendered issue. Imbalances occur in all kinds of relationships.
  • Not healthy when it is always one-sided. Sharing the load is part of a balanced relationship, as described on the page about always being the one compromising.

How It Is Addressed

Addressing emotional labour usually starts with making it visible. Couples can list all the tasks involved in running their lives, including planning and remembering, and discuss how to divide them so that each person owns tasks fully, from noticing to completion. Shared calendars and regular check-ins help. Talking about emotional labour without blame, and focusing on fairness and teamwork, tends to work better than criticism. Couples therapy can help when conversations become stuck or resentment has built up.

Common Questions About Emotional Labour in Relationships

What does emotional labour mean in a relationship?

It refers to the often invisible work of anticipating needs, planning, remembering, and managing emotions to keep a relationship and household running.

What is the mental load?

The mental load is the cognitive work of tracking and planning household and family tasks, such as remembering appointments and noticing what needs doing.

Why does emotional labour cause resentment?

When one partner carries most of the invisible work, they can feel exhausted and unseen, and the imbalance can build resentment over time.

What is a calm way to raise the mental load with my partner?

Describe specific examples, including the planning and remembering behind tasks, and focus on wanting to share the load as a team rather than blaming.

Is emotional labour a gender issue?

It often falls unevenly along gender lines because of social expectations, but imbalances can occur in any relationship.

How can couples share emotional labour more fairly?

List all tasks, including planning and noticing, divide ownership so each person handles tasks from start to finish, and review the balance regularly.

Can ADHD affect how emotional labour is shared?

Yes. ADHD can make planning and remembering harder, which may shift more of the load to one partner. Shared systems and open discussion can help.

Is managing my partner's moods a form of emotional labour?

Yes. Constantly anticipating or smoothing over a partner's emotions is emotional labour and can become draining if it is one-sided.

Can emotional labour lead to burnout?

Carrying heavy emotional and mental work over time can contribute to exhaustion and burnout, especially alongside paid work or caregiving.

Is couples therapy useful for mental load conflicts?

Yes. Couples therapy can help partners recognize invisible work, communicate without blame, and create a fairer division of responsibilities.
