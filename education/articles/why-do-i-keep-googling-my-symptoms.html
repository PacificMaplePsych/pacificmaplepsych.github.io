Why Do I Keep Googling My Symptoms

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

A twinge in your chest, a new mole, a headache that lasts longer than usual. Within minutes you are searching online, reading forums, clicking through lists of causes, and landing on the most frightening possibility. You feel briefly reassured when a result seems harmless, then anxious again when the next one does not. An hour later, you are more worried than when you started.

Almost everyone looks up symptoms sometimes. When searching becomes frequent, hard to stop, and leaves you more frightened rather than more informed, it is often a sign that anxiety about health has taken over.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

A real, unresolved health concern. If you have symptoms that have not been properly assessed, or you feel dismissed by health providers, searching can be an attempt to get answers. In that case, pushing for a thorough medical assessment is the right step.

A recent scare. After a family member's diagnosis, a friend's illness, or your own health scare, it is natural to become more watchful for a while.

General anxiety. For people who worry about many things, health is often one of the topics the mind returns to, alongside a general sense that something bad is coming.

Anxiety-driven body sensations. Anxiety produces real physical symptoms, such as a racing heart, dizziness, or headaches and stomach aches. Searching these symptoms can make them feel more dangerous.

OCD. Some people search in a ritualistic way to neutralize a specific feared illness, which can be a form of compulsive checking.

When It Looks More Like Health Anxiety

Health anxiety, sometimes called illness anxiety, involves persistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, even when medical assessments are reassuring. It is common and treatable.

Online searching tends to make health anxiety worse. Search results often highlight rare, serious conditions, and each new article introduces new possibilities to worry about. Reassurance from a search, or from a doctor, tends to last only briefly before doubt returns, much like the cycle described in Why Do I Need Constant Reassurance.

Signs of health anxiety include:

  • Frequently checking your body for changes, such as lumps, pulse, or skin marks.
  • Searching symptoms many times, often late at night.
  • Seeking repeated tests or doctor visits, or avoiding doctors entirely out of fear.
  • Reassurance that fades quickly.
  • Worry that takes up significant time and affects work, sleep, or relationships.

What Can Help

  • Get one good assessment. See a trusted doctor for new or persistent symptoms, then agree on a plan for follow-up rather than seeking repeated reassurance.
  • Delay the search. When the urge to search appears, wait thirty minutes. The urge often fades, and you learn that anxiety passes without checking.
  • Limit body checking. Reduce how often you check your pulse, skin, or other areas, since checking keeps attention locked on sensations.
  • Choose reliable sources. If you do look something up, use reputable health organizations rather than forums.
  • Notice the pattern. Keeping a note of how often worries turned out to be harmless can help loosen the grip of catastrophic thinking.

Common Questions About Googling Symptoms

Why does googling my symptoms make me more anxious?

Search results often highlight rare and serious conditions, and each new page offers another possibility to worry about. Any relief from reassuring results tends to fade quickly, leaving you more anxious than before.

What is health anxiety?

Health anxiety is persistent worry about having or developing a serious illness, even when medical assessments are reassuring. It often involves checking the body, searching symptoms, and seeking reassurance.

Does checking my body for lumps or changes make health worries worse?

Frequent body checking keeps attention focused on sensations and small changes that would otherwise go unnoticed, which tends to increase worry. Checking can also irritate the area being checked, creating new sensations to worry about.

How can I stop googling every symptom?

Delaying the urge to search, limiting body checking, using only reputable sources, and agreeing on a follow-up plan with a trusted doctor can all help. Therapy for health anxiety is effective if the habit feels hard to break.

What if my symptoms really are serious?

It is important to have new or persistent symptoms assessed by a doctor. Health anxiety treatment does not mean ignoring your health. It means getting appropriate care and then reducing excessive checking and searching.

Can anxiety cause real physical symptoms?

Yes. Anxiety can cause a racing heart, dizziness, chest tightness, stomach upset, headaches, and tingling. These sensations are real, even when they are not caused by a physical illness.

Why do I search symptoms late at night?

Night is often when worries feel strongest and distractions are fewest. Searching late at night also interferes with sleep, which can make anxiety worse the next day.

Is it health anxiety if I avoid doctors altogether?

It can be. Some people with health anxiety avoid medical appointments because they fear hearing bad news. Both frequent reassurance seeking and complete avoidance can be part of the pattern.

How is health anxiety treated?

Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most researched treatment. It helps people understand the anxiety cycle, reduce checking and searching, and test catastrophic beliefs about symptoms.

Can health anxiety develop after a family member gets sick?

Yes. Watching someone close to you become ill can make you more alert to your own body and more fearful of illness. This is understandable, and support can help if the worry becomes persistent.
