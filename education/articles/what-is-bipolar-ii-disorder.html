What Is Bipolar II Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Depression in bipolar II can be severe. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Bipolar II disorder is a mood condition involving at least one episode of hypomania and at least one episode of major depression. Hypomania is a period of elevated or irritable mood with increased energy, lasting at least four days, that is noticeable to others but not severe enough to cause major impairment or require hospitalization. Unlike bipolar I disorder, people with bipolar II do not experience full mania. Bipolar II is sometimes mistakenly seen as a milder form of bipolar disorder, but depressive episodes are often frequent, long-lasting, and disabling. Accurate diagnosis matters, because treatment differs from that for depression alone.

What Bipolar II Looks Like

Hypomanic episodes may include:

  • Feeling unusually upbeat, confident, or irritable.
  • Needing much less sleep without feeling tired.
  • Talking more, faster thoughts, or jumping between ideas.
  • Increased activity, productivity, or goal-driven behaviour.
  • Riskier decisions, such as spending or impulsive plans.

These features are described further on the page about hypomania.

Depressive episodes may include:

  • Persistent low mood, emptiness, or hopelessness.
  • Loss of interest or pleasure.
  • Fatigue, oversleeping, or insomnia.
  • Difficulty concentrating and making decisions.
  • Feelings of worthlessness or guilt.
  • Thoughts of death or suicide.

Many people with bipolar II spend far more time depressed than hypomanic. Some also experience mixed features, where symptoms of both occur together.

Why It Happens

Bipolar II has strong genetic roots and often runs in families. Differences in how the brain regulates mood, energy, and sleep are involved. Episodes can be triggered by stress, sleep disruption, seasonal changes, substance use, and sometimes antidepressants taken without a mood stabilizer. Bipolar II often begins in the late teens or twenties.

Because hypomania can feel good or productive, many people do not recognize it as part of an illness and seek help only during depression. As a result, bipolar II is often misdiagnosed as depression for years, as explained on the page about bipolar disorder and depression. Milder, long-lasting mood swings may instead reflect cyclothymia. Bipolar II also commonly occurs alongside anxiety disorders, ADHD, and substance use.

Living with bipolar II can affect relationships and work, especially during long depressive periods. Loved ones may notice hypomania before the person does and may feel confused by shifts in energy and behaviour. Education for families can help everyone recognize early warning signs and respond supportively.

What It Is Not

  • Not a mild form of bipolar disorder. Depression in bipolar II can be severe and long-lasting.
  • Not the same as bipolar I. Bipolar I involves full mania, described on the page about bipolar I disorder.
  • Not just moodiness. It involves distinct episodes lasting days to weeks.
  • Not a character flaw. It is a medical condition that responds to treatment.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a detailed history of mood episodes, sleep, and energy, often with input from family and a mood chart. A psychiatrist usually guides medication, which may include mood stabilizers and certain medications effective for bipolar depression. Antidepressants alone are generally used with caution. Psychotherapy is an important part of treatment. Psychoeducation helps people recognize early warning signs. Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy stabilizes routines and sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps manage depression and thinking patterns. Family-focused therapy can improve communication and support. Regular sleep, limiting alcohol and drugs, and a relapse prevention plan all support stability.

Common Questions About Bipolar II Disorder

What is bipolar II disorder?

Bipolar II involves at least one hypomanic episode and at least one major depressive episode, without full mania.

Does bipolar II ever involve full manic episodes?

No. Full mania defines bipolar I. Bipolar II involves hypomania, which is milder, along with major depression.

Is bipolar II less serious than bipolar I?

No. Depressive episodes in bipolar II can be frequent, long-lasting, and disabling.

Why might years pass before bipolar II is recognized?

People usually seek help when depressed and may not recognize or mention past hypomania.

What does hypomania feel like in bipolar II?

It may feel like high energy, confidence, less need for sleep, faster thoughts, and more activity, noticeable to others.

Can antidepressants make bipolar II worse?

Taken alone, antidepressants may trigger hypomania or mood instability in some people, so they are used with caution.

What does a treatment plan for bipolar II usually include?

It usually combines medication guided by a psychiatrist with psychotherapy, psychoeducation, and routine stabilization.

Does bipolar II run in families?

Yes. Bipolar disorders have strong genetic links and often run in families.

What is family-focused therapy for bipolar disorder?

It involves family members in learning about bipolar disorder, improving communication, and supporting relapse prevention.

How can I track my moods with bipolar II?

A daily mood chart recording mood, sleep, energy, and events can help you and your care team spot patterns.
