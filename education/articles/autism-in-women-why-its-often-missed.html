Autism in Women: Why It's Often Missed

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Autism in women and girls is frequently overlooked, identified late, or mistaken for other conditions. For many years, research and diagnostic descriptions were based mostly on boys and men, leading to a narrow picture of what autism looks like. Many autistic women and girls present differently, often with strong language skills, socially typical interests, and a practised ability to blend in. As a result, many reach adulthood without an explanation for why life has felt so effortful. Recognizing how autism can look in women helps more people access understanding, support, and community.

How Autism Can Look in Women and Girls

  • Studying and copying peers to fit in, a process called masking or camouflaging.
  • Feeling like you are performing in social situations and exhausted afterward.
  • One or two close friendships, sometimes intense, rather than a wide group.
  • Deep interests that look typical on the surface, such as animals, books, psychology, or a favourite show, but are pursued with unusual intensity.
  • Strong sensory sensitivities, such as to clothing, sound, or light.
  • Being described as shy, sensitive, or a perfectionist in childhood.
  • Difficulty with unwritten social rules, especially in group or workplace settings.
  • Periods of burnout, anxiety, or depression in adolescence or adulthood.

Some autistic girls are quiet and compliant at school, then fall apart at home once they no longer have to hold it together. Parents may notice meltdowns or exhaustion that teachers never see.

Why It Is Often Missed

Several factors contribute to under-recognition. Diagnostic tools were developed largely using male samples. Social expectations placed on girls, such as being polite, agreeable, and socially attentive, may encourage masking from an early age. Many autistic girls work hard to observe and imitate others, which can hide differences from teachers and clinicians. Their interests may be seen as ordinary rather than intense.

Autistic women are also often diagnosed first with other conditions, such as anxiety, depression, eating disorders, or personality disorders. These conditions may be present too, but without recognizing autism, treatment may miss the underlying picture. Eating concerns, including restrictive eating linked with sensory differences, are more common among autistic women and girls. The page on late diagnosis of autism explores what finding out in adulthood can mean.

Hormonal changes may also affect how autistic women experience sensory and emotional demands, with some reporting that puberty, pregnancy, or perimenopause brought new challenges. These transitions can be times when previously manageable difficulties become harder to mask, sometimes prompting a first assessment.

What It Is Not

  • Not a different condition. Autism in women is the same condition, often presenting in less recognized ways. The core features are described on the page about autism spectrum disorder.
  • Not just shyness or social anxiety, though social anxiety often co-occurs.
  • Not less real because it was hidden. Masking often comes at a high cost to wellbeing.
  • Not limited to women. Anyone who masks well may be overlooked, whatever their sex.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

An assessment with a clinician experienced in adult autism, including less typical presentations, is important. Assessments explore developmental history, social experiences, sensory differences, masking, and co-occurring conditions, and may include input from family. After diagnosis, support may include neurodiversity-affirming therapy, help with anxiety or burnout, learning about sensory needs, and gradually reducing masking where it feels safe. Connecting with other autistic women often brings a strong sense of belonging and relief.

Common Questions About Autism in Women

Why is autism underdiagnosed in women?

Diagnostic tools were built mostly on male samples, and many autistic women mask traits or present in less recognized ways.

What does autistic camouflaging involve?

Camouflaging involves studying and copying others' social behaviour, scripting conversations, and hiding traits to fit in, often at great cost.

How do autistic girls present differently from boys?

Many autistic girls have strong language skills, socially typical interests, and masking skills, so differences are less visible at school.

What conditions are autistic women often misdiagnosed with?

Anxiety, depression, eating disorders, and personality disorders are common initial diagnoses, sometimes alongside autism.

Can an autistic woman have friends and good social skills?

Yes. Many autistic women have close friendships and learned social skills, though socializing may be draining and effortful.

Why do some autistic girls fall apart at home after school?

Holding it together all day through masking is exhausting, so distress and meltdowns often surface in a safe place like home.

Are eating concerns more common in autistic women?

Yes. Restrictive eating, sometimes linked with sensory differences or a need for control, is more common among autistic women and girls.

Is it worth pursuing an autism assessment as an adult woman?

Many women find that assessment brings clarity, self-compassion, access to support, and connection with community.

How can I find a clinician who understands autism in women?

Ask whether the clinician has experience assessing adults and less typical presentations, including masking and camouflaging.

Can hormonal changes affect autistic women?

Some autistic women report that puberty, pregnancy, or perimenopause brought new sensory or emotional challenges.
