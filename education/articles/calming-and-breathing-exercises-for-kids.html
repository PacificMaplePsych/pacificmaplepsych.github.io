Breathing and calming exercises for kids work best when they are simple, playful, and practised regularly when the child is already calm. Techniques such as balloon breathing, smelling the flower and blowing out the candle, and bubble breathing slow children's breathing and help their bodies switch out of the stress response. Using pictures, toys, and stories makes the skills easier to remember. Children learn calming skills by watching adults, so practising together helps. These exercises can ease worry, frustration, and trouble settling at bedtime, but they are tools for riding out big feelings, not a way to make feelings disappear. Children with ongoing anxiety may also need extra support.

Why Calming Exercises Help Children

When children feel anxious, angry, or overwhelmed, their bodies react quickly: faster breathing, a racing heart, tense muscles, and a brain focused on danger. Slow breathing, especially with longer breaths out, activates the body's calming system. Children also benefit from having something concrete to do when feelings feel too big, which builds a sense of control. Over time, practising calming skills supports emotional regulation, as described on the page about what emotional regulation is.

Exercises to Try

1. Smell the Flower, Blow Out the Candle

Breathe in slowly through the nose as if smelling a flower, then breathe out slowly through the mouth as if blowing out a birthday candle. Repeat three to five times.

2. Balloon Breathing

Place hands on the belly. Breathe in and imagine the belly filling like a balloon, then breathe out slowly and let the balloon shrink.

3. Bubble Breathing

Pretend to blow a big bubble with a slow, steady breath out. Real bubbles make this fun for younger children.

4. Teddy Bear Breathing

Lie down with a stuffed animal on the belly and watch it rise and fall. This works well at bedtime.

5. Finger Tracing

Hold up one hand. Trace up each finger while breathing in and down while breathing out, for five slow breaths.

6. Shake It Out or Squeeze and Release

Shake hands and arms like a wet dog, or squeeze fists tight for a few seconds and then let go. This helps release tension, similar to progressive muscle relaxation.

7. Five Senses Check

Name things you can see, hear, and feel. This grounding skill works well for older children and teens, as described on the page about grounding techniques.

Making It Work

  • Practise when calm. Short daily practice, such as at bedtime, makes skills easier to use in hard moments.
  • Model it. Breathe alongside your child, and use the skills yourself when you feel stressed.
  • Keep it playful. Use stories, pictures, or a "calm corner" with cushions and visual reminders.
  • Do not force it. Offer a choice of two skills. Insisting during a meltdown can backfire.
  • Validate first. Name the feeling, such as "You look really frustrated," before suggesting a skill.
  • Adapt for neurodivergent children. Some children prefer movement, deep pressure, or sensory tools to breathing.

When to Seek More Support

If your child's worries, fears, or outbursts are frequent, intense, or interfere with school, friendships, or sleep, they may benefit from professional support. Anxiety in children responds well to cognitive behavioural therapy, often with parents involved, as described on the pages about anxiety in children and teens and how to support an anxious child. Common Questions About Breathing Exercises for Kids

What are good breathing exercises for kids?

Good breathing exercises for kids include smell the flower and blow out the candle, balloon breathing, bubble breathing, and teddy bear breathing.

How do I teach my child to calm down?

Teach your child to calm down by practising simple breathing or calming exercises together when they are already calm, then using them during hard moments.

At what age can children learn breathing exercises?

Children as young as three or four can learn simple, playful breathing exercises, such as bubble or balloon breathing.

Do breathing exercises help children with anxiety?

Yes. Breathing exercises help children with anxiety by calming the body's stress response, though ongoing anxiety may need more support.

What is balloon breathing for kids?

Balloon breathing for kids means breathing in to fill the belly like a balloon and breathing out slowly to let it shrink.

Should I make my child do breathing exercises during a meltdown?

Forcing breathing exercises during a meltdown can backfire. Stay calm, keep them safe, and offer skills once they start to settle.

What calming techniques work for autistic children?

Many autistic children prefer movement, deep pressure, or sensory tools to breathing exercises, so it helps to experiment.

Can breathing exercises help kids fall asleep?

Yes. Teddy bear breathing and other slow breathing exercises can help kids relax and fall asleep at bedtime.

What is a calm corner for kids?

A calm corner is a quiet space with comfortable items and visual reminders of calming skills where kids can go to settle big feelings.

When should my child see a therapist for anxiety?

Consider a therapist if your child's anxiety is frequent, intense, or interferes with school, friendships, sleep, or family life.
