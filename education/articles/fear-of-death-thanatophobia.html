Fear of Death (Thanatophobia)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Fear of death, sometimes called thanatophobia or death anxiety, is worry or dread about dying, being dead, or the death of loved ones. Almost everyone thinks about mortality at times, and some unease is a natural part of being human. For some people, though, the fear becomes persistent and intense, leading to panic, intrusive thoughts, difficulty sleeping, avoiding reminders of death, or constant worry about health. Death anxiety often rises after a loss, a health scare, a birthday milestone, or becoming a parent. Thanatophobia is not a formal diagnosis on its own, but it often overlaps with health anxiety, panic, OCD, or existential distress, and it responds well to therapy.

How Fear of Death Can Show Up

  • Intrusive thoughts about dying or nonexistence, especially at night
  • Panic when thinking about death
  • Health anxiety, such as frequent checking, googling symptoms, or seeking reassurance, as described on the page about worrying that you are seriously ill
  • Avoidance of funerals, hospitals, news, or conversations about death
  • Fear for loved ones, such as constant worry that a partner or child will die
  • Feeling that life is meaningless or overwhelmed by questions about existence

Why It Happens

Life Events

A death in the family, a frightening diagnosis, or a near miss can bring mortality into sharp focus, as described on the page about grief after a sudden or traumatic death.

Life Stages

Milestones such as turning 40 or 50, becoming a parent, or retiring can prompt reflection on mortality.

Anxiety Tendencies

People prone to anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, or a need for control may find death especially hard to think about, since it is the ultimate uncertainty.

Existential Questions

Some people struggle with questions about meaning, purpose, and what happens after death, as described on the page about existential anxiety.

What It Is Not

Thinking about death from time to time, feeling sad about mortality, or grieving is not a phobia. Fear of death also differs from suicidal thoughts, which involve wishing to die. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

What Helps

Therapy

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy helps address catastrophic thoughts, reassurance-seeking, and avoidance.
  • Exposure to death-related reminders, such as reading about mortality or visiting a cemetery, reduces fear over time.
  • Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people make room for difficult thoughts while focusing on what matters, as described on the page about acceptance and commitment therapy.
  • Existential approaches explore meaning, values, and how awareness of mortality can inform how you live, as described on the page about person-centred, humanistic, and existential therapy.

Everyday Strategies

  • Notice when you are seeking reassurance or checking, and gradually reduce it.
  • Limit late-night rumination by writing worries down and returning to them during the day.
  • Connect with values and relationships that make life meaningful.
  • Draw on spiritual, cultural, or philosophical traditions that bring comfort.

Fear of Death in Children

Children often go through periods of worrying about death, especially after a loss or around ages five to ten. Simple, honest answers, reassurance, and keeping routines usually help. Persistent fear that affects sleep or daily life may benefit from support.

When to Get Support

If fear of death causes frequent distress, panic, sleep problems, or avoidance, or if it is linked to health anxiety or OCD, therapy can help. The page about what health anxiety is describes a common related pattern.

Common Questions About Fear of Death

Is thanatophobia the medical term for fear of death?

Thanatophobia is the term often used for an intense, persistent fear of death or dying, also called death anxiety, though it is not a formal diagnosis.

How much fear of dying is a normal part of being human?

Some unease about dying is a normal part of being human. Fear of death becomes a concern when it is persistent, intense, and affects daily life.

Why do some people fear death more than others?

People prone to anxiety, intolerance of uncertainty, or a need for control, or who have faced loss or health scares, may fear death more than others.

Why can fear of dying start after a health scare or loss?

Fear of dying often starts after a health scare, a loss, a milestone birthday, or becoming a parent, which brings mortality into sharp focus.

Is thanatophobia a diagnosis?

Thanatophobia is not a formal diagnosis on its own, but it often overlaps with health anxiety, panic, or OCD.

How is fear of death treated?

Fear of death is treated with therapies such as CBT, exposure, acceptance and commitment therapy, and existential approaches.

Why does fear of death get worse at bedtime?

Fear of death often gets worse at bedtime because there are fewer distractions and the mind turns inward as you try to sleep.

Is fear of death the same as health anxiety?

Fear of death often overlaps with health anxiety, which involves worry about having or developing a serious illness.

Does CBT or ACT work for death anxiety?

Yes. CBT, exposure, and acceptance and commitment therapy can reduce death anxiety and help people live more fully despite awareness of mortality.

Is fear of death the same as suicidal thoughts?

No. Fear of death is dread about dying, while suicidal thoughts involve wishing to die. Call or text 9-8-8 if you have suicidal thoughts.
