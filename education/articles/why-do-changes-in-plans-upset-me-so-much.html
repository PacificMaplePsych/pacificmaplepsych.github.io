Why Do Changes in Plans Upset Me So Much

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

A friend texts to move dinner from seven to seven thirty. A meeting gets shifted to a different room. The restaurant you planned on is closed. To other people these are tiny adjustments, but for you the day suddenly feels thrown off. You might feel a wave of frustration, anxiety, or even panic, and it can take a surprisingly long time to recover. Sometimes the reaction feels embarrassing, because you know the change is "no big deal."

Strong reactions to changes in plans are more common than people think. They usually reflect how your mind and nervous system handle uncertainty and transitions, and the reason can vary a lot from person to person.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Stress and depletion. When you are running on empty, you have less flexibility for anything unexpected. A small change can be the last straw on an already overloaded day.

Anxiety. For anxious people, a plan is a way of managing uncertainty. When it changes, the uncertainty floods back, and the mind may jump to what else could go wrong, much like the feeling described in Why Do I Feel Like Something Bad Is About to Happen.

Past unpredictability. If you grew up in a chaotic or unsafe home, predictability may have been a real source of safety. As an adult, sudden changes can still set off an old sense of alarm, similar to feeling unsafe when nothing is wrong.

ADHD. Many people with ADHD rely on carefully built plans to stay on track. A change can mean losing the mental scaffolding that got them there, and shifting gears between tasks is often harder than it looks from outside.

Genuine disappointment. Sometimes you are simply let down, particularly if the change reflects a pattern of someone being unreliable.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Predictability plays an especially important role for many autistic people. Knowing what will happen, when, and in what order reduces the constant effort of processing an unpredictable world. It frees up energy for everything else, including social interaction and sensory demands.

When a plan changes, an autistic person often has to rebuild their mental picture of the day from scratch. That takes real cognitive effort, and it arrives on top of whatever else they are managing. The distress is not about being inflexible for the sake of it. It is about suddenly losing a structure that made the day manageable.

A few patterns suggest autism may be part of the picture:

  • Distress about changes has been present since childhood, and was maybe described as "rigid" or "difficult" by adults at the time.
  • Even small or positive changes, such as a surprise outing, can feel unsettling.
  • Transitions between activities are hard in general, not only when plans change.
  • Unexpected changes can lead to shutdown or meltdown, especially on days with a lot of sensory input.
  • Other autistic traits are present, such as taking language literally or strong, focused interests.

What Can Help

  • Build in buffer. Leaving extra time and space in your plans makes small changes easier to absorb.
  • Ask for advance notice. Letting friends, family, and coworkers know that early warning of changes helps you is a reasonable request.
  • Have a backup plan ready. Thinking through a plan B in advance turns an unexpected change into a known alternative.
  • Give yourself recovery time. After a disruptive change, a short break to reset can prevent the rest of the day from unravelling.
  • Practise flexibility gently if anxiety is the main driver, starting with small, low-stakes changes you choose yourself.

Common Questions About Changes in Plans

Is getting upset about changed plans a sign of autism?

It can be, particularly when it has been lifelong, happens even with small or positive changes, and comes with other autistic traits. Anxiety, stress, ADHD, and past unpredictability can also make changes hard, so the wider pattern matters.

Why does a small change in plans ruin my whole day?

When a plan changes, you may need to rebuild your mental picture of the day, manage uncertainty, and shift gears, all of which use energy. On days when capacity is already low, that extra demand can tip you into feeling overwhelmed.

Can anxiety make it hard to cope with plans changing?

Yes. Plans often help anxious people feel in control of uncertainty. When they change, that uncertainty returns and the mind may jump to worst case scenarios, which can make even a minor change feel alarming.

Why do even good surprises upset me?

A pleasant surprise still disrupts expectations and requires quick adjustment. For people who rely on predictability, including many autistic adults, the disruption itself can be uncomfortable even when the change is positive.

How can I explain to friends that changes in plans are hard for me?

Keeping it simple usually works best, such as saying that advance notice helps you enjoy plans more. Most people are happy to text earlier or give more detail once they understand it genuinely helps.

Can ADHD make transitions between plans or tasks difficult?

Yes. Many people with ADHD find shifting attention from one task or plan to another harder than it appears. When plans change unexpectedly, the carefully built structure that kept them on track can fall apart.

Is difficulty with change the same as being controlling?

Not usually. Needing predictability to feel regulated is different from wanting to control other people. The distress is typically about losing structure and managing uncertainty, not about getting your way.

What helps after an unexpected change throws me off?

Taking a short break to reset, writing down the new plan, and reducing other demands for a while can all help. It also helps to notice what the change affected most, such as timing, sensory load, or social expectations.

Can growing up in an unpredictable home affect how I handle change now?

It can. When predictability was a source of safety in childhood, sudden changes in adulthood may still trigger an old sense of alarm. Therapy can help the nervous system learn that present changes are usually manageable.

Would an autism assessment ask about routines and changes?

Yes. Adult autism assessments usually explore how you respond to changes in routine, transitions, and unexpected events, along with social communication, sensory experiences, interests, and developmental history.
