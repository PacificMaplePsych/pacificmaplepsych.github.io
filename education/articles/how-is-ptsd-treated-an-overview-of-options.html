How Is PTSD Treated: An Overview of Options

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

PTSD is treated most effectively with trauma-focused psychotherapies, such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, and EMDR, which help people process traumatic memories so they become less overwhelming. Medication prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist can also help some people, particularly with sleep, anxiety, or co-occurring depression. Treatment is usually delivered within a trauma-informed approach that prioritizes safety, choice, and trust. Many people with PTSD experience significant improvement, often within a few months of regular therapy, even after trauma that happened many years ago.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure. These are two of the best-studied treatments for PTSD. CPT focuses on changing unhelpful beliefs about the trauma, such as self-blame, while PE involves gradually approaching trauma memories and avoided situations. Both are explained on the page about cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure.

Trauma-focused CBT. This approach combines education about trauma, coping skills, processing of memories, and work on thoughts and behaviours.

EMDR. Eye movement desensitization and reprocessing is a recognized trauma treatment in which people recall traumatic memories while following bilateral stimulation, such as side-to-side eye movements.

Other approaches. Narrative exposure therapy, written exposure therapy, and somatic approaches are also used. Written exposure therapy is a shorter option with growing evidence.

Medication. Certain antidepressants may be prescribed for PTSD, and other medications may help with nightmares or sleep. Medication decisions are made with a physician or psychiatrist.

Treatment for related difficulties. Many people with PTSD also experience nightmares, as described on the page about nightmare disorder, as well as depression, anxiety, or substance use, which may be treated alongside PTSD.

All of these are best delivered within a trauma-informed framework.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan begins with an assessment of symptoms, the type of trauma, how long ago it happened, current safety, and co-occurring conditions. The features of the condition are explained on the page about PTSD. For single-event trauma, trauma-focused therapy can often begin fairly soon. For people with complex PTSD, involving long-term or repeated trauma, a period of stabilization and skill-building may come first. People who are still in unsafe situations usually need safety planning before trauma processing. Personal preference matters too. Some people prefer to focus on beliefs, others on memories, and others want a shorter approach.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Many trauma-focused therapies run for around eight to sixteen sessions, and people often notice meaningful change within this time. Early signs of progress can include fewer or less intense flashbacks and nightmares, less avoidance, better sleep, and feeling less on edge. Some people notice their symptoms feel temporarily stronger early in treatment, before improving, which is expected and usually passes. Complex trauma often needs longer-term work. Setbacks can occur around anniversaries or new stressors and are part of recovery.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Practising skills and assignments between sessions supports trauma-focused treatment. Grounding and regulation skills help during difficult moments. Regular sleep, meals, and movement support the body. Limiting alcohol and other substances, which can worsen PTSD symptoms and sleep, helps treatment work. Connecting with supportive people, and letting them know what helps, reduces isolation. Being patient and compassionate with yourself matters, since recovery takes courage.

Common Questions About PTSD Treatment

Which talk therapies do PTSD treatment guidelines recommend?

Trauma-focused therapies such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, trauma-focused CBT, and EMDR are the most effective PTSD treatments.

Can PTSD be treated years after the trauma happened?

Yes. PTSD treatment can help even when trauma happened many years ago.

What is EMDR for PTSD?

EMDR is a trauma treatment in which people recall traumatic memories while following bilateral stimulation, such as side-to-side eye movements.

Can a prescription help with PTSD nightmares or sleep?

It can. A physician may prescribe medication to help with PTSD nightmares or sleep, and certain antidepressants are sometimes used for PTSD more broadly.

How many sessions does trauma-focused therapy for PTSD usually involve?

Many trauma-focused PTSD therapies run around eight to sixteen sessions, while complex trauma may need longer-term work.

Why might PTSD treatment start with stabilization?

For complex PTSD or ongoing stress, building safety and coping skills first makes trauma processing more manageable.

Will PTSD therapy make my symptoms worse at first?

Some people notice PTSD symptoms feel temporarily stronger early in trauma-focused therapy, which usually passes as treatment continues.

What is written exposure therapy for PTSD?

Written exposure therapy is a shorter trauma-focused treatment for PTSD that involves writing about the trauma, with growing evidence.

Can PTSD treatment help with nightmares?

Yes. Trauma-focused therapy often reduces PTSD nightmares, and specific treatments for nightmares can be added.

How can I support my own PTSD recovery between sessions?

Practise skills and assignments, use grounding, protect sleep, limit alcohol, and connect with supportive people during PTSD recovery.
