Why Do I Rehearse Conversations Before Having Them

Before a phone call, a meeting, or even ordering coffee, you run through it in your head. What you will say, what they might say back, how you will respond if it goes a different way. Sometimes you rehearse out loud in the car or the shower. For important conversations, you might script whole exchanges days in advance, and still feel unprepared when the moment arrives.

Preparing for a conversation is normal and often useful. When rehearsing becomes something you feel you must do before almost any interaction, it can take up a lot of mental energy, and it is worth understanding what is driving it.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

High-stakes situations. Job interviews, difficult conversations with a partner, or asking for a raise naturally prompt preparation. Rehearsing in these cases is a sensible strategy.

Social anxiety. When you fear saying the wrong thing or being judged, rehearsing feels like protection against embarrassment. Social anxiety often comes with physical signs, such as sweaty palms before talking to people.

Perfectionism and overthinking. Wanting every word to come out right can lead to endless scripting, part of a broader tendency to overthink everything.

Past conflict. If conversations in your past often turned into arguments or criticism, planning every word can feel like a way to prevent things from going badly. This is common in people who avoid conflict at all costs.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD rehearse because they worry about losing their train of thought, forgetting key points, or interrupting.

Language. Speaking in a second language often involves preparing phrases in advance, especially for formal or unfamiliar situations.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Many autistic people describe rehearsing conversations as a central part of how they manage social life. Everyday interactions involve many unwritten rules, such as when to speak, how much detail to give, and how to read tone, which may not come automatically. Scripting in advance is a way of doing consciously what others seem to do on autopilot.

This kind of rehearsal is often less about fear and more about processing. It can reduce the cognitive load of a conversation in the moment, allowing an autistic person to focus on listening and responding. It is also closely linked to masking, the effort of appearing socially typical, which can be exhausting over time.

Signs that autism may be part of the picture include:

  • Rehearsing even simple, low-stakes exchanges, such as phone calls or greetings, and having done so since childhood.
  • Relying on scripts or stock phrases and feeling thrown when a conversation goes off script.
  • Replaying interactions afterward to analyze what was meant.
  • Other social and sensory differences, such as finding eye contact uncomfortable or taking things literally.

What Can Help

  • Prepare key points, not a full script. A few bullet points give structure while leaving room to respond naturally.
  • Plan a flexible exit. Having a phrase ready for when you lose your place, such as "let me think about that for a second," can reduce pressure.
  • Use written communication when it helps. Email or text can be a reasonable alternative for some interactions, especially if phone calls are draining.
  • Notice when rehearsal becomes worry. If you are imagining worst case scenarios rather than preparing, it may be anxiety talking.
  • Budget energy. If rehearsing and masking are tiring, build in recovery time after social demands.

Common Questions About Rehearsing Conversations

Is it normal to practise conversations in my head?

Yes, many people mentally prepare for important conversations. It becomes more noteworthy when rehearsing happens before nearly every interaction, takes a lot of time, or leaves you feeling drained or anxious.

Is scripting conversations a sign of autism?

It can be. Many autistic adults rehearse or script interactions to manage unwritten social rules and reduce in-the-moment processing demands. Scripting alone does not mean someone is autistic, but it is worth noting alongside other traits.

How is rehearsing from social anxiety different from autistic scripting?

Rehearsing driven by social anxiety is usually focused on avoiding embarrassment or judgment. Autistic scripting is often more about making social expectations predictable and manageable. The two can overlap in the same person.

Why do I get thrown off when a conversation doesn't go as I rehearsed?

When you have prepared a specific script, unexpected responses require quick improvising, which can be difficult if social processing takes effort or anxiety is high. Preparing key points rather than full scripts can make conversations more flexible.

Can ADHD make me rehearse what I want to say?

Yes. Some people with ADHD rehearse to avoid losing their train of thought, forgetting important points, or interrupting others. Brief notes can help serve the same purpose with less mental effort.

Is rehearsing phone calls before making them common?

Very common. Phone calls remove visual cues and require quick responses, which many people find stressful. Rehearsing or writing down what you want to say beforehand is a practical strategy.

What is social masking and how does it relate to rehearsing conversations?

Masking is the effort of hiding natural traits to appear socially typical. Rehearsing conversations, practising expressions, and following learned social rules are all forms of masking, which can be tiring when done constantly.

Why do I feel exhausted after conversations I rehearsed for?

Preparing, performing, and monitoring yourself during a conversation all take energy. If you also mask or manage anxiety at the same time, the combined effort can leave you drained afterward.

How can I rely less on rehearsing before talking to people?

Try preparing a few key points instead of a full script, practising in low-stakes situations, and giving yourself permission to pause or ask for time to think during conversations.

Can therapy help if I rehearse conversations constantly?

Yes. If anxiety drives the rehearsing, therapy can help reduce fear of judgment. If autistic processing is involved, neurodiversity-affirming therapy can help you find strategies that reduce effort and support your communication style.
