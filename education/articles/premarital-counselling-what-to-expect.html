Premarital Counselling: What to Expect

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Premarital counselling is a short, focused form of couples therapy for partners who are planning to marry or make a long-term commitment. It gives couples a structured space to talk about communication, conflict, money, family, intimacy, children, and future plans before these topics become sources of strain. It is not only for couples who are struggling. Many couples who feel happy and secure choose it as a way to strengthen their foundation, understand each other more deeply, and build skills they can rely on later. Sessions are usually collaborative and practical, and many couples describe them as surprisingly enjoyable.

How Premarital Counselling Works

Premarital counselling is based on the idea that healthy relationships rely on skills that can be learned, and that many later difficulties grow from expectations that were never spoken aloud. A counsellor helps partners explore these areas openly and practise constructive ways of talking about them. Common topics include:

  • Communication. How each partner expresses needs, listens, and handles difficult conversations.
  • Conflict. Each partner's conflict style, what tends to trigger arguments, and how to repair afterward. Patterns such as the pursue-withdraw cycle are often explored.
  • Family of origin. How each partner's upbringing shapes their expectations about roles, affection, and conflict.
  • Money. Spending habits, debt, saving, and how finances will be shared.
  • Children and parenting. Whether to have children, and values about raising them.
  • Intimacy and affection. Needs and expectations around closeness and sex.
  • Roles and daily life. Household tasks, careers, time together and apart, and relationships with extended family.
  • Values and future goals. Culture, faith, where to live, and what a shared life might look like.

Counsellors may draw on research-informed couples approaches, including the Gottman Method and emotionally focused therapy, as well as attachment ideas explained on the page about attachment styles. Some use structured questionnaires that highlight strengths and areas to discuss.

What Sessions Are Like

Premarital counselling is usually shorter than ongoing couples therapy, often a handful of sessions, though some couples continue longer. The first session often involves getting to know both partners, hearing the story of the relationship, and identifying goals. Some counsellors ask each partner to complete a questionnaire beforehand or meet with each partner briefly on their own.

Later sessions focus on specific topics, with the counsellor guiding conversations so that both partners feel heard. Sessions often include skills practice, such as taking turns speaking and reflecting back, making requests instead of complaints, and taking breaks during heated discussions. Couples may receive exercises to try at home, like having a planned conversation about finances or sharing memories from their families growing up.

It is common for couples to discover differences they had not discussed. This is not a sign that the relationship is in trouble. The aim is not to agree on everything, but to understand each other and find ways to handle differences with respect. Some couples find that one or two topics need more time, and the counsellor can adjust the focus.

Couples blending families, bringing children from earlier relationships, or coming from different cultural or faith backgrounds may use sessions to talk through the specific questions these situations raise. Couples who have been together a long time before marrying also benefit, since long-standing habits can be examined with fresh eyes.

Who It Can Help

Premarital counselling can help engaged couples, couples considering moving in together, and those making any long-term commitment, whether or not they plan a wedding. It is useful for couples who feel strong and want to stay that way, as well as those who notice recurring arguments or unspoken worries. Research on relationship education programs suggests they can improve communication and relationship satisfaction for many couples, though results vary.

Premarital counselling is not designed for relationships involving abuse or coercive control. In these situations, joint sessions can be unsafe, and individual support and safety planning come first. Resources are available on the page about abuse resources. When one partner is struggling with a significant mental health concern, individual therapy alongside couples work may help.

Common Questions About Premarital Counselling

Do we need to be having problems to book premarital counselling?

No. Many couples who feel happy and secure choose it to strengthen their foundation and learn skills for the future.

How many sessions does premarital counselling usually take?

It is often a handful of sessions, though some couples continue longer to explore specific topics.

What topics come up in sessions before marriage?

Communication, conflict, money, family, children, intimacy, roles, and future goals are common topics.

What happens if premarital counselling shows we disagree about something big, like children or money?

Differences are normal. Counselling helps you understand each other and decide together how to handle them.

Will the counsellor take sides between us?

No. The counsellor supports both partners equally and helps each person feel heard.

Can we do premarital counselling if we are not planning a wedding?

Yes. It suits any couple making a long-term commitment, including moving in together.

Do we have to share our upbringing in premarital sessions?

Family background often comes up because it shapes expectations, but you decide how much to share.

Is premarital counselling helpful for blended families?

Yes. Sessions can explore parenting roles, relationships with children, and expectations in a blended family.

Is there homework between premarital sessions?

Many counsellors suggest short exercises, such as planned conversations about money or family traditions.

When is couples counselling not recommended before marriage?

Joint sessions may be unsafe when there is abuse or coercive control, and individual support and safety planning come first.
