ADHD Coaching vs. Therapy: What's the Difference

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

ADHD coaching focuses on practical, forward-looking goals such as organization, time management, routines, and accountability, while ADHD-informed therapy addresses both practical skills and the emotional and mental health side of ADHD, such as anxiety, depression, shame, relationships, and past experiences. Coaches help you build systems and follow through. Therapists are regulated mental health professionals who can assess and treat mental health conditions. Both can be valuable, and many adults with ADHD benefit from one, the other, or both at different times. The right choice depends on what you need most right now.

Why They Are Easy to Confuse

ADHD coaching and ADHD-informed therapy often cover similar ground. Both may help with planning, procrastination, routines, and goal setting. Both are usually collaborative and practical. Some therapists use coaching-style methods, and some coaches have training in mental health. Both work well alongside medication prescribed by a physician. Because ADHD affects daily functioning and emotional wellbeing at the same time, the line between practical and emotional support can blur. The broader treatment picture is described on the page about how ADHD is treated in adults.

Key Differences

  • Focus. Coaching focuses on present and future goals, action steps, and accountability. Therapy also explores emotions, thought patterns, relationships, and how past experiences affect the present.
  • Mental health. Therapists can assess and treat anxiety, depression, trauma, and other conditions that often occur with ADHD. Coaches do not diagnose or treat mental health conditions.
  • Regulation. In BC, psychologists, clinical counsellors, and other therapists belong to regulated professions or professional bodies with standards of practice. ADHD coaching is not regulated in the same way, so training and quality vary widely.
  • Methods. Coaching often uses goal setting, planning tools, check-ins, and accountability. Therapy may use CBT adapted for ADHD, emotion-focused work, and other approaches.
  • Session style. Coaching sessions may be shorter or more frequent, including brief check-ins between sessions. Therapy sessions are often weekly or biweekly and longer.
  • Coverage. Some extended health plans cover therapy with certain regulated professionals. Coaching is less often covered.

When Both Are Helpful

Many adults with ADHD find coaching and therapy complement each other. Therapy might address the shame, rejection sensitivity, or emotional dysregulation that makes change feel hard, while coaching supports daily follow-through. Some people start with therapy to address anxiety or low mood, then add coaching for practical goals. Others start with coaching and discover emotional barriers that therapy can help with. When both are involved, it helps if the coach and therapist are aware of each other's roles, with your consent.

Why the Distinction Matters

Choosing the right support saves time, money, and frustration. If your main struggles are practical, such as organizing tasks, meeting deadlines, or building routines, and your mood is generally stable, coaching may be a good fit. Strategies such as those on the page about non-medication strategies for managing ADHD are often a coaching focus. If you are also struggling with anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, relationship difficulties, trauma, substance use, or thoughts of self-harm, therapy is more appropriate, since these need a mental health professional.

When choosing a coach, ask about their training, experience with ADHD, and approach. When choosing a therapist, look for ADHD experience, as described on the page about finding an ADHD-informed therapist. If you are unsure whether ADHD is the cause of your difficulties, an assessment can help clarify.

Common Questions About ADHD Coaching and Therapy

Does an ADHD coach work on my feelings, or only on my to-do list?

Mostly the to-do list. ADHD coaching focuses on practical goals and accountability, while ADHD-informed therapy also addresses emotions, mental health, and past experiences.

Can an ADHD coach treat anxiety or depression?

No. ADHD coaches do not diagnose or treat mental health conditions. A therapist can address anxiety or depression alongside ADHD.

Is ADHD coaching regulated in BC?

ADHD coaching is not regulated in the same way as therapy professions in BC, so training and quality vary widely.

Should I see an ADHD coach or a therapist first?

If your ADHD struggles are mainly practical and your mood is stable, coaching may fit. If you also feel anxious, low, or overwhelmed, start with therapy.

Can I work with an ADHD coach and a therapist at the same time?

Yes. Many adults with ADHD use both, with therapy addressing emotional barriers and coaching supporting daily follow-through.

Does extended health insurance cover ADHD coaching?

Some extended health plans cover therapy with certain regulated professionals, but ADHD coaching is less often covered. Check your plan.

What questions should I ask an ADHD coach before hiring them?

Ask about their training, experience with ADHD, approach, session format, and how they handle mental health concerns that come up.

How often do people usually meet with an ADHD coach?

ADHD coaching is often weekly or every other week, sometimes with brief check-ins between sessions to support accountability.

Why might ADHD therapy help when coaching has not worked?

Shame, anxiety, or rejection sensitivity linked with ADHD can block change, and therapy can address these emotional barriers.

Can ADHD coaching work alongside medication?

Yes. ADHD coaching often complements medication prescribed by a physician by helping turn improved focus into lasting habits.
