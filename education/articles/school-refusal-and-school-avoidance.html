School Refusal and School Avoidance

School refusal, also called school avoidance or emotionally based school avoidance, is when a child or teen has persistent difficulty going to school or staying there because of emotional distress. It is not the same as truancy. Children with school refusal usually want to do well but feel overwhelmed by anxiety, low mood, or other difficulties. Signs include stomach aches or headaches on school mornings, tearful pleading to stay home, panic at drop-off, or frequent calls to come home. Common causes include separation anxiety, social anxiety, bullying, learning difficulties, and depression. The longer a child is away, the harder returning can feel, so early, coordinated support between home and school is important.

Signs of School Refusal

  • Physical complaints on school mornings that ease once the child is allowed to stay home
  • Tearfulness, tantrums, or panic before school
  • Pleading or bargaining to stay home
  • Frequent absences, late arrivals, or early pick-ups
  • Spending time in the school office or nurse's room
  • Feeling fine on weekends and holidays

Common Causes

  • Separation anxiety. Worry about being apart from parents, more common in younger children.
  • Social anxiety. Fear of being judged, speaking in class, or peer situations, as described on the page about social anxiety disorder.
  • Bullying, as described on the page about bullying and cyberbullying.
  • Learning difficulties that make school feel frustrating, as described on the page about signs your child may have a learning disability.
  • Test anxiety, as described on the page about test anxiety.
  • Depression or low mood.
  • Sensory overwhelm, particularly for autistic children.
  • Family stress or changes, such as illness or separation.

What It Is Not

School refusal is not laziness or simple defiance, and it is not caused by bad parenting. It is also different from truancy, where a young person skips school without parents knowing, usually without significant anxiety.

What Helps

Work With the School

Meet with teachers, counsellors, or administrators to understand what is happening and create a plan. Schools may offer adjusted schedules, a safe space, a trusted adult, or check-ins.

Plan a Gradual Return

Rather than all or nothing, a step-by-step return, such as attending for part of the day or specific classes, builds confidence.

Keep Routines

Keep waking times and school-day structure even when the child is home, and limit screen time and fun activities during school hours so home does not become more rewarding than school.

Validate and Encourage

Acknowledge feelings while expressing confidence that your child can cope, as described on the page about how to support an anxious child.

Address Underlying Causes

Bullying, learning needs, or mental health concerns need direct attention.

Get Medical Input

A family doctor can rule out physical causes of symptoms.

Mornings

Mornings are often the hardest part. A predictable routine, preparing the night before, calm encouragement, and a clear plan for getting to school can reduce stress for everyone.

When to Get Support

Seek help early if school refusal lasts more than a few days, is increasing, or is linked to strong anxiety or low mood. Cognitive behavioural therapy, often with parent involvement, is effective for school refusal related to anxiety. If learning difficulties may be involved, a psychoeducational assessment can clarify needs, as described on the page about school supports in BC after a psychoeducational assessment.

Common Questions About School Refusal

How common is school refusal in children and teens?

School refusal is fairly common, especially around transitions such as starting school or moving to middle or high school.

Why do children with school refusal often feel fine on weekends?

Children with school refusal often feel fine on weekends because the anxiety is tied to school itself, such as separation, social fears, or academic stress.

Is school refusal the same as truancy?

No. School refusal is driven by emotional distress, while truancy usually involves skipping school without significant anxiety.

What are signs of school refusal?

Signs of school refusal include stomach aches on school mornings, tearfulness, pleading to stay home, panic at drop-off, and frequent absences.

Should I let my child stay home when they refuse school?

Staying home often makes school refusal worse over time. A gradual return plan with school support usually helps more.

How can schools help with school refusal?

Schools can help with adjusted schedules, a safe space, a trusted adult, check-ins, and a gradual return plan.

Can anxiety cause school refusal?

Yes. Anxiety is one of the most common causes of school refusal.

Does therapy help school refusal?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy, often with parent involvement, is effective for anxiety-related school refusal.

Can learning difficulties cause school avoidance?

Yes. Learning difficulties can make school frustrating and lead to avoidance, so an assessment may help.

How soon should parents seek help for school refusal?

Seek help for school refusal early, ideally if it lasts more than a few days, is increasing, or comes with strong anxiety or low mood.
