Treatment Approaches for Prolonged Grief

Prolonged grief is treated most effectively with therapies designed specifically for it, such as prolonged grief disorder therapy (sometimes called complicated grief treatment) and grief-focused cognitive behavioural therapy. These approaches help people accept the reality of the loss, process painful memories, reduce avoidance, and rebuild a meaningful life while keeping a connection to the person who died. General supportive counselling helps many grieving people, but research suggests grief-specific approaches work better when grief remains intense and disabling for a long time. Treatment does not ask anyone to forget or stop loving the person. It helps grief become something that can be carried.

The Main Treatment Options

Prolonged grief disorder therapy. This structured approach, often around sixteen sessions, combines elements of cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal therapy, and motivational interviewing. It works on two tracks: coming to terms with the loss, including revisiting the story of the death and memories of the person, and restoring life, including personal goals, relationships, and enjoyable activities. It has good research support. The condition itself is described on the page about complicated grief and prolonged grief disorder.

Grief-focused CBT. CBT adapted for grief addresses unhelpful beliefs, such as self-blame or thinking one should have prevented the death, and reduces avoidance of reminders. It also includes behavioural activation to rebuild daily life.

Meaning-centred and narrative approaches. These help people make sense of the loss and integrate it into their life story.

Trauma-focused therapy. When the death was sudden, violent, or traumatic, trauma-focused approaches may be used alongside grief treatment.

Group treatment. Structured grief groups, described on the page about group therapy for grief, can complement individual work.

Medication. There is no medication specifically for prolonged grief, but a physician may treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, or sleep problems.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A clinician begins by understanding the loss, the relationship, how grief has changed over time, and how it affects daily life. They also screen for depression, PTSD, substance use, and thoughts of suicide, which often occur alongside prolonged grief. The circumstances of the death matter. Losses to suicide, as explored on the page about grief after a suicide loss, overdose, violence, or the death of a child may call for specialized support. Personal readiness and preferences also shape the plan. Some people want structured work, while others need a period of supportive counselling first. The general experience of counselling is described on the page about grief counselling.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress in prolonged grief treatment often begins with small shifts, such as being able to look at photos, visit meaningful places, or talk about the person without being overwhelmed. Many people notice that longing and preoccupation ease, guilt softens, and interest in life slowly returns. Structured approaches often show meaningful change over several months. Grief does not disappear, and sadness around anniversaries or reminders is normal. The goal is for grief to take a more manageable place in life. Some sessions may feel very emotional, especially when revisiting the death, but therapists pace this work carefully.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Between sessions, gently approaching reminders you have been avoiding, as agreed with your therapist, helps treatment work. Keeping some structure, such as regular meals, sleep, and movement, supports energy. Reconnecting with people, even briefly, reduces isolation. Small steps toward activities that once mattered can rebuild a sense of purpose. It can also help to find ways to honour the person, such as rituals or projects, that let the bond continue in a healthier form. Be patient with yourself, since healing from prolonged grief takes time.

Common Questions About Treatment for Prolonged Grief

Which therapies work best when grief stays intense for years after a death?

For prolonged grief, grief-specific therapies such as prolonged grief disorder therapy and grief-focused CBT have the strongest research support.

How is prolonged grief disorder therapy structured?

Prolonged grief disorder therapy often runs about sixteen sessions and works on accepting the loss and restoring personal goals and relationships.

Will treatment for prolonged grief make me forget the person I lost?

No. Prolonged grief treatment helps you carry the loss while keeping a healthy connection to the person who died.

Why does prolonged grief treatment involve revisiting the death?

Gently revisiting the story of the death helps reduce avoidance and allows the reality of the loss to be processed, which eases prolonged grief.

Can a doctor prescribe something for prolonged grief itself?

There is no medication specifically for prolonged grief, but a physician may treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, or sleep problems.

How is grief-focused CBT different from regular grief counselling?

Grief-focused CBT targets specific beliefs such as self-blame and reduces avoidance, while general grief counselling is more open and supportive.

How long does treatment for prolonged grief usually take?

Structured prolonged grief treatment often shows meaningful change over several months, though timelines vary.

How will I know my prolonged grief is starting to ease in therapy?

Being able to look at photos or talk about the person without being overwhelmed, and a slow return of interest in life, are common early signs.

Does prolonged grief after a traumatic death need different treatment?

Often trauma-focused therapy is added when a death was sudden or violent, alongside prolonged grief treatment.

What can I do between sessions to support prolonged grief treatment?

Gently approach avoided reminders as agreed with your therapist, keep basic routines, reconnect with people, and take small steps toward meaningful activities.
