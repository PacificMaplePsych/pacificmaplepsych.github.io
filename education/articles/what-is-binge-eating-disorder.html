What Is Binge Eating Disorder

If eating concerns have led to thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Speak with a doctor about any physical symptoms, such as chest pain or significant changes in health.

Binge eating disorder is the most common eating disorder. It involves recurring episodes of eating an unusually large amount of food in a short period, together with a feeling of loss of control. Binges are followed by distress, such as guilt, shame, or disgust. Unlike bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder does not involve regular compensatory behaviours such as vomiting or excessive exercise. It affects people of all body sizes, ages, and backgrounds. Binge eating disorder is a real mental health condition, not a lack of willpower, and it responds well to treatment.

What Binge Eating Disorder Looks Like

  • Eating much more than most people would in a similar time, often within two hours.
  • Feeling out of control around food during episodes.
  • Eating very quickly or until uncomfortably full.
  • Eating large amounts when not physically hungry.
  • Eating alone or in secret because of embarrassment.
  • Guilt, shame, or low mood afterward.
  • Episodes occurring at least once a week for three months.

Binges often follow periods of restriction, stress, or difficult emotions. People may describe feeling numb or zoned out during a binge, and then very distressed afterward.

Why It Happens

Binge eating disorder develops from a mix of biological, psychological, and social factors. It often runs in families. Dieting and restriction are strong triggers: when the body is deprived, hunger and preoccupation with food increase, making binges more likely. This is known as the restrict and binge cycle. Emotional factors also matter. Many people use food to cope with stress, sadness, loneliness, boredom, or trauma, because eating can bring temporary comfort or numbness.

Weight stigma and diet culture can deepen shame, which in turn fuels more binges. People with depression, anxiety, ADHD, or a history of trauma are at higher risk. Binge eating disorder often begins in the late teens or early adulthood, though it can start at any age.

Shame plays a powerful role in keeping binge eating going. Many people hide episodes for years and feel alone with the problem, believing that others would judge them. Recognizing binge eating disorder as a health condition, rather than a personal failure, is often an important first step toward seeking help and loosening the grip of the cycle.

Physical health can also be affected over time. Binge eating disorder is associated with digestive discomfort, sleep problems, and higher risk of conditions such as type 2 diabetes and high blood pressure. These risks are best addressed by treating the eating disorder itself and by weight-neutral medical care, rather than by more dieting.

What It Is Not

  • Not overeating on occasion. Most people eat more than usual sometimes. The disorder involves regular episodes with loss of control and distress.
  • Not a lack of willpower. Restriction and willpower-based dieting often make it worse.
  • Not defined by body size. People of any weight can have binge eating disorder.
  • Not the same as bulimia. Bulimia involves regular compensatory behaviours such as vomiting.
  • Not something to solve with a stricter diet. Regular, adequate eating is part of recovery.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about eating patterns, binge frequency, triggers, emotions, and any history of dieting or other eating disorders, alongside mood and trauma. A physician may check physical health. Cognitive behavioural therapy for eating disorders is the most studied treatment and helps people establish regular eating, understand triggers, and reduce binges. Dialectical behaviour therapy skills can help with emotional triggers. Interpersonal therapy addresses relationship stress that may drive binges. Working with a dietitian experienced in eating disorders can support a flexible, non-restrictive approach. Some medications may be considered. Many people see significant improvement with treatment.

Common Questions About Binge Eating Disorder

What is binge eating disorder?

It is an eating disorder involving recurring episodes of eating large amounts of food with a sense of loss of control, followed by distress.

How is binge eating disorder different from overeating?

Occasional overeating is common. Binge eating disorder involves regular episodes with loss of control and significant distress.

Do people with binge eating disorder make themselves sick afterward?

Not as a regular pattern. Regular vomiting, fasting, or excessive exercise after binges points toward bulimia nervosa instead.

Why does dieting make binge eating worse?

Restriction increases hunger and preoccupation with food, making binges more likely. This is known as the restrict and binge cycle.

Can stress or emotions trigger binge eating?

Yes. Many people binge in response to stress, sadness, loneliness, boredom, or other difficult emotions.

Does binge eating disorder only affect people in larger bodies?

No. Binge eating disorder affects people of all body sizes.

Why do I eat in secret?

Shame and fear of judgment often lead people with binge eating to eat alone or hide episodes.

What therapy works best for binge eating disorder?

Cognitive behavioural therapy for eating disorders has the strongest evidence, with DBT and interpersonal therapy also helpful.

How many people live with binge eating?

Yes. It is the most common eating disorder and affects people of all ages and backgrounds.

Can I recover from binge eating disorder?

Yes. Many people significantly reduce or stop binges with treatment and develop a healthier relationship with food.
