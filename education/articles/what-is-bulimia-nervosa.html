What Is Bulimia Nervosa

Bulimia nervosa is an eating disorder that involves repeated episodes of binge eating, where a person eats a large amount of food with a sense of loss of control, followed by behaviours intended to prevent weight gain. These compensatory behaviours can include self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives or diuretics, fasting, or excessive exercise. Bulimia is driven by intense concern about body weight and shape, which strongly influences self-worth. People with bulimia are often within or above an average weight range, so the condition can go unnoticed. Bulimia is a serious condition with real medical risks, and it is treatable.

What Bulimia Nervosa Looks Like

  • Recurring episodes of eating large amounts of food with a sense of loss of control around food.
  • Regular compensatory behaviours after binges, such as vomiting, fasting, laxative use, or excessive exercise.
  • Self-worth heavily tied to body shape and weight, as described on the page about body image distress.
  • Secrecy around eating, such as eating alone or hiding food.
  • Going to the bathroom right after meals.
  • Swollen cheeks or jaw, dental problems, or sores on the knuckles.
  • Fluctuations in weight.
  • Shame, guilt, and distress after binges and purges.
  • Low mood, anxiety, or irritability.

Why It Happens

Bulimia develops from a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors. Genetics play a role, and eating disorders often run in families. Dieting and restriction are strong triggers: restricting food increases hunger and preoccupation, which makes binges more likely, and binges then lead to guilt and compensatory behaviours. This creates a cycle that is hard to break.

Emotional factors are also central. Many people use binge eating to cope with stress, sadness, loneliness, or difficult feelings, and purging may bring temporary relief from guilt or anxiety. Perfectionism, low self-esteem, a history of trauma, and weight-related teasing increase risk. Cultural pressures around thinness and diet culture contribute. Bulimia often begins in adolescence or early adulthood and commonly occurs alongside depression, anxiety, and substance use.

Bulimia shares features with other eating disorders. It differs from binge eating disorder because of the regular compensatory behaviours, and from anorexia nervosa, which involves significantly low weight. Some people move between eating disorder diagnoses over time.

Shame is a powerful force in bulimia. Many people hide their symptoms for years, fearing judgment. Recognizing bulimia as a health condition, rather than a personal failing, can make it easier to seek help and begin recovery.

What It Is Not

  • Not a lifestyle choice. Bulimia is a serious mental health condition.
  • Not defined by weight. Many people with bulimia are at an average weight.
  • Not harmless because it is hidden. Purging carries significant medical risks.
  • Not something willpower alone can fix. Treatment is highly effective.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a mental health professional and a physician, who may check vital signs, electrolytes, and heart function. Cognitive behavioural therapy for eating disorders is the most effective treatment for bulimia. It helps people establish regular eating, break the binge and purge cycle, address beliefs about weight and shape, and manage triggers. Family-based treatment is recommended for adolescents. Dialectical behaviour therapy skills can help with emotional triggers. A dietitian experienced in eating disorders supports regular, flexible eating. Some medications may help reduce binge and purge episodes. Many people recover fully with treatment.

Common Questions About Bulimia Nervosa

What is bulimia nervosa?

Bulimia is an eating disorder involving binge eating followed by compensatory behaviours such as vomiting, fasting, or excessive exercise.

Can someone have bulimia at an average weight?

Yes. Many people with bulimia are within or above an average weight range, which can make it harder to notice.

What are the medical risks of purging?

Purging can cause electrolyte imbalances, heart rhythm problems, dental damage, and digestive problems.

Why does dieting lead to bingeing?

Restriction increases hunger and preoccupation with food, making binges more likely and fuelling the cycle.

What are signs that someone might have bulimia?

Signs include secretive eating, frequent bathroom visits after meals, swollen cheeks, dental problems, and weight changes.

Which therapy has the strongest record for stopping binge and purge cycles?

Cognitive behavioural therapy for eating disorders has the strongest evidence, with family-based treatment for adolescents.

Can bulimia be cured?

Many people recover fully with treatment, and early support improves outcomes.

How can I help a friend who might have bulimia?

Express concern gently, avoid comments about weight or food, listen without judgment, and encourage professional help.

Do low mood and anxiety often travel with bulimia?

Yes. Bulimia often occurs alongside depression, anxiety, and sometimes substance use.

Why do I feel so ashamed after a binge?

Shame is common in bulimia, often tied to beliefs about weight and control. Therapy can help reduce shame and break the cycle.
