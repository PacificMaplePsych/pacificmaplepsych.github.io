Depression in Older Adults: What to Watch For

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you or an older adult you know is having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Depression in older adults is a common and treatable condition, not a normal part of aging. Yet it is frequently overlooked, because it can look different from depression earlier in life. Older adults may talk less about sadness and more about tiredness, aches, poor sleep, or trouble concentrating. Symptoms can be mistaken for physical illness, medication side effects, grief, or memory decline. Recognizing the signs matters, because untreated depression affects health, independence, and quality of life, while treatment helps at any age.

What Depression in Older Adults Looks Like

  • Loss of interest in hobbies, social activities, or family visits.
  • Fatigue, slowed movement, or low energy that is not fully explained by health conditions.
  • Aches, pains, or digestive complaints that increase or persist.
  • Sleep changes, including early waking or sleeping much more.
  • Changes in appetite or unintended weight loss.
  • Memory or concentration complaints, sometimes called pseudodementia when caused by depression.
  • Irritability, worry, or feeling like a burden to family.
  • Neglecting self-care, medications, or the home.
  • Withdrawing and spending more time alone, sometimes alongside ongoing loneliness.

Some older adults describe feeling flat or empty rather than sad. Family members may notice the change before the person does.

Why It Happens

Later life often brings several losses at once: the death of a spouse, siblings, or friends, reduced mobility, the end of work, changes in role, and sometimes moving from a long-time home. Chronic health conditions, such as heart disease, stroke, diabetes, Parkinson's disease, and chronic pain, are linked with higher rates of depression. Some medications can affect mood. Changes in the brain's blood vessels may contribute to depression that begins later in life. Loneliness and social isolation are strong risk factors, especially for those living alone or with limited transportation. A history of earlier depression also raises risk.

The adjustment to retirement can play a role too, especially when work provided structure, identity, and social connection. For some, the loss of purpose feels as significant as any physical change.

Older adults may also be less likely to seek help. Some grew up at a time when mental health was rarely discussed and may see depression as a personal weakness. Others worry about being a burden or assume low mood is simply what getting older feels like. Gentle encouragement from family, friends, or a family doctor can make a real difference in whether someone reaches out.

What It Is Not

  • Not a normal part of aging. Most older adults are not depressed, and depression deserves treatment at any age.
  • Not the same as grief, though bereavement can trigger depression. Grief usually comes in waves and still allows moments of connection and meaning.
  • Not dementia, though the two can overlap and affect memory. A thorough assessment helps tell them apart.
  • Not something older adults are too set in their ways to treat. Therapy and medication work well in later life.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor can check for medical causes, review medications, and screen for depression and memory concerns. Treatment may include psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, problem-solving therapy, or interpersonal therapy, which can address grief, role changes, and isolation. Antidepressant medication may be recommended, with attention to interactions and side effects. Social connection, meaningful activity, and physical movement matched to ability all support recovery. Family can help by noticing changes, encouraging assessment, and staying in regular contact.

Common Questions About Depression in Older Adults

Why is depression in later life often missed?

Older adults may report tiredness, pain, poor sleep, or memory problems more than sadness, which can make depression harder to recognize.

Is depression a normal part of getting older?

No. Most older adults are not depressed. Depression is a treatable condition at any age, not an expected part of aging.

Can depression cause memory problems in older adults?

Yes. Depression can affect concentration and memory. These problems often improve with treatment, which is why assessment matters.

How can I tell whether my parent has depression or dementia?

The two can look similar and can overlap. A doctor can assess mood, memory, and medical factors to clarify what is happening.

What health conditions increase depression risk in later life?

Heart disease, stroke, diabetes, Parkinson's disease, chronic pain, and hearing or vision loss are all linked with higher rates of depression.

Can medications cause low mood in older adults?

Some medications can affect mood or energy. A doctor or pharmacist can review medications if mood changes appear.

How does loneliness affect older adults' mental health?

Loneliness and isolation are strong risk factors for depression in later life. Regular social contact and community involvement help protect mood.

Does therapy work for depression in older adults?

Yes. Therapies such as cognitive behavioural, problem-solving, and interpersonal therapy are effective for older adults.

How can I encourage an older relative to get help for low mood?

Share specific changes you have noticed, express care rather than criticism, and offer to help book or attend a doctor's appointment.

Are older adults at higher risk of suicide?

Some groups of older adults, especially older men living alone or with serious illness, have higher suicide risk. Warning signs should be taken seriously.
