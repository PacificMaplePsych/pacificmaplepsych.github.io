Stress Management Techniques That Work

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Stress management techniques that work tend to do one of three things: calm the body's stress response, change how you think about or approach the stressor, or reduce the stressor itself. Evidence-supported techniques include slow breathing, regular physical activity, relaxation skills, mindfulness, problem-solving, good sleep, and social support. No single technique works for everyone, and most people benefit from combining several. Stress is a normal part of life, and some stress can even help with motivation. The goal is not to eliminate stress but to recover from it well, so it does not build into ongoing exhaustion or health problems.

Why These Techniques Help

When people face a challenge, the body releases stress hormones that raise heart rate, sharpen focus, and prepare for action. This is described on the page about the stress response. The response is useful in short bursts. Problems arise when stress is frequent or ongoing and the body does not get a chance to recover, which can lead to chronic stress, with effects on sleep, mood, digestion, immunity, and concentration.

Effective techniques activate the body's calming system, reduce the mental load of worry, and help people take action on problems they can change. Practising them regularly, not only in moments of crisis, builds resilience over time.

Strategies to Try

1. Slow Breathing

Breathing slowly, around five or six breaths per minute, with longer exhales, can calm the nervous system. Try breathing in for four counts and out for six, for a few minutes.

2. Regular Physical Activity

Movement, such as walking, cycling, swimming, or dancing, helps the body process stress hormones and improves mood and sleep. Even short bouts help.

3. Relaxation Skills

Techniques such as progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, and body scans reduce physical tension.

4. Mindfulness

Mindfulness means paying attention to the present moment with curiosity rather than judgment. Research suggests mindfulness-based programs can reduce stress for many people. Start with a few minutes a day.

5. Structured Problem-Solving

For problems you can change, write down the problem, brainstorm possible solutions, choose one, plan the steps, then review how it went. This reduces the sense of being stuck.

6. Scheduled Worry Time

Set aside fifteen minutes a day to write down worries. When worries come up at other times, note them and return to them later. This can reduce constant worry.

7. Protect Sleep

Keep consistent sleep and wake times, limit screens before bed, and create a calming wind-down routine.

8. Connect With Others

Talking with supportive people eases stress and offers perspective.

9. Prioritize and Set Boundaries

Reduce stress at the source by prioritizing tasks, saying no when needed, and delegating where possible.

10. Spend Time in Nature

Time outdoors, even a short walk in a park, is linked with lower stress for many people.

Common Obstacles

When stressed, people often have the least time and energy for stress management. Starting small, such as two minutes of breathing or a ten-minute walk, is more realistic than long routines. Some techniques may not suit everyone. For example, some people find stillness uncomfortable and prefer movement-based approaches. It can help to experiment and notice what works.

Coping habits such as alcohol, excessive screen time, or skipping meals can bring short-term relief but make stress worse over time. Replacing them gradually with healthier options helps. Some sources of stress, such as financial hardship, discrimination, caregiving, or unsafe work, are outside individual control. Techniques can help people cope, but these situations may also need practical support or broader change. When stress is mostly work-related, the page about nervous system regulation and burnout may help.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If stress feels constant or overwhelming, affects your sleep, health, work, or relationships, or comes with ongoing anxiety, low mood, or panic attacks, professional support can help. Therapy, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, can help you understand your stress patterns and build coping skills. A doctor can check for physical effects of stress. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or feel very unwell, seek medical care or call 911. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Managing Stress

Which stress management techniques have the best evidence?

Slow breathing, regular physical activity, relaxation skills, mindfulness, problem-solving, sleep, and social support are well supported.

How quickly does slow breathing reduce stress?

Many people notice calming effects within a few minutes of slow breathing with longer exhales.

Why does exercise help with stress?

Movement helps the body process stress hormones and improves mood and sleep.

Would writing my worries down at a set time each day stop them following me around?

It involves setting aside a short daily period to write down worries, which can reduce constant worrying.

How can I manage stress when I have no time?

Start small, such as two minutes of breathing or a short walk, and build from there.

Can a deadline that stresses me out also help me perform better?

Yes. Short-term stress can improve focus and motivation. Problems arise when stress is ongoing without recovery.

Why do I reach for alcohol or screens when I am stressed?

They bring quick relief, but over time they can make stress and sleep worse.

Can mindfulness really help with stress?

Research suggests mindfulness-based programs can reduce stress for many people, especially with regular practice.

What if my stress comes from things I cannot control?

Techniques can help you cope, and practical support or advocacy may be needed to address the source.

When is stress a reason to see a professional?

If stress feels constant or overwhelming, or affects your health, sleep, work, or relationships, support can help.
