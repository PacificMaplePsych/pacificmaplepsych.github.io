Depression and Physical Symptoms You Might Not Expect

Depression is often thought of as sadness or low mood, but it affects the whole body. Many people with depression first notice physical changes, such as constant tiredness, aches, stomach problems, or changes in sleep and appetite. In some cultures and communities, physical symptoms are the main way depression is expressed. Because these symptoms can seem unrelated to mood, depression is sometimes missed, and people may undergo many medical tests before the connection is made. Recognizing the physical side of depression can help people get the right support sooner.

Physical Symptoms of Depression

  • Fatigue. Deep tiredness and feeling exhausted all the time, even after rest.
  • Aches and pains. Headaches, back pain, joint or muscle aches.
  • Digestive problems. Nausea, stomach pain, constipation, or changes in bowel habits, similar to headaches or stomach aches with stress.
  • Sleep changes. Insomnia, early waking, or sleeping much more than usual.
  • Appetite and weight changes. Eating much less or much more.
  • Slowed movement or speech, or feeling physically restless.
  • Heaviness in the limbs, as if weighed down.
  • Reduced interest in sex.
  • Difficulty concentrating, sometimes described as brain fog.
  • Lower pain tolerance and more sensitivity to discomfort.

Why It Happens

Depression involves changes in brain systems that regulate mood, energy, sleep, appetite, and pain. Chemical messengers such as serotonin and norepinephrine influence both mood and how the body processes pain signals. Stress hormones, which are often elevated in depression, affect digestion, immune function, and sleep. Research also links inflammation with depression, which may contribute to fatigue and aches. Disrupted sleep, a common part of depression, amplifies fatigue, pain, and poor concentration, as explained on the page about depression and sleep.

The relationship runs both ways. Chronic pain and illness increase the risk of depression, and depression can worsen the experience of physical illness. Reduced activity during depression can lead to deconditioning, making tiredness and aches worse. Some people describe physical symptoms more easily than emotional ones, especially if emotions were not discussed growing up or if mental health carries stigma.

Physical symptoms can also make depression harder to treat if they are not addressed. Fatigue and pain may make it difficult to engage in activities that help mood, such as exercise or socializing. Treating both the emotional and physical aspects together tends to produce better results.

Many people with depression visit doctors several times for physical complaints before depression is discussed. Mentioning changes in mood, interest, or hopelessness alongside physical symptoms helps health professionals see the full picture and offer appropriate care.

What It Is Not

  • Not imagined. Physical symptoms of depression are real and have biological explanations.
  • Not a reason to skip medical checks. A doctor should rule out other causes, such as thyroid problems or anemia.
  • Not always accompanied by obvious sadness. Some people with depression mainly notice physical changes.
  • Not untreatable. Physical symptoms often improve as depression is treated.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor can check for medical causes of physical symptoms and screen for depression. Once depression is identified, treatment usually improves both emotional and physical symptoms. Psychotherapies such as cognitive behavioural therapy and behavioural activation help with mood, energy, and activity. Some antidepressants also help with certain types of pain. Gentle, regular physical activity improves mood, sleep, and energy. Protecting sleep, eating regular meals, and gradually increasing activity support recovery. Common Questions About the Physical Side of Depression

Can depression cause body aches?

Yes. Depression can cause headaches, back pain, and muscle or joint aches, partly through changes in pain processing.

Why am I so tired when I am depressed?

Depression affects brain systems that regulate energy and sleep, leading to deep fatigue that rest does not relieve.

Can depression affect digestion?

Yes. Nausea, stomach pain, and changes in bowel habits are common physical symptoms of depression.

Is it possible to be depressed without feeling sad?

Yes. Some people mainly notice fatigue, aches, or loss of interest rather than obvious sadness.

What is brain fog in depression?

Brain fog describes difficulty concentrating, remembering, and thinking clearly, which is common in depression.

Why does depression make pain feel worse?

Depression and pain share brain pathways, so low mood can lower pain tolerance and increase discomfort.

Should I see a doctor for physical symptoms if I think I am depressed?

Yes. A doctor can rule out other causes, such as thyroid problems or anemia, and check for depression.

Can antidepressants help with physical symptoms?

Some antidepressants help with fatigue and certain types of pain as depression improves.

How is inflammation linked to depression?

Research links inflammation with depression, which may contribute to fatigue and aches in some people.

Does exercise help physical symptoms of depression?

Gentle, regular physical activity can improve mood, energy, and sleep, and reduce some aches over time.
