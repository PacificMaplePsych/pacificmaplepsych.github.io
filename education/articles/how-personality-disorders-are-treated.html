How Personality Disorders Are Treated

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Personality disorders are treated mainly with longer-term psychotherapy designed to change deep, long-standing patterns in how people think, feel, relate to others, and see themselves. Approaches include dialectical behaviour therapy, schema therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, and psychodynamic therapy. Medication is not a primary treatment for personality disorders themselves, though a physician may prescribe it for co-occurring conditions such as depression or anxiety. With consistent, well-matched therapy, many people experience meaningful change. Personality disorders are not character flaws. They often develop from a mix of temperament and difficult early experiences, and people can grow and change throughout life.

The Main Treatment Options

The broad group of conditions is explained on the page about personality disorders. Treatment is tailored to the specific patterns involved.

Dialectical behaviour therapy. DBT is especially well studied for borderline personality disorder and for intense emotions and self-harm.

Schema therapy. Schema therapy targets deep patterns formed in childhood and has support for several personality disorders.

Mentalization-based treatment. This approach strengthens the ability to understand one's own and others' thoughts and feelings, which can reduce misunderstandings and impulsive reactions.

Transference-focused and psychodynamic therapies. These explore how early relationships shape current patterns, using the therapy relationship as a place to understand and change them.

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT can address specific patterns, such as perfectionism and rigid rules in obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, which is distinct from OCD, as explained on the page about OCPD versus OCD.

Group therapy. Groups can help people practise new ways of relating in a supportive setting.

Medication. No medication treats personality disorders directly, but medication may help co-occurring depression, anxiety, or sleep problems.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A clinician begins with a careful assessment of patterns, history, current difficulties, safety, and co-occurring conditions such as depression, trauma, substance use, or eating disorders. The specific personality disorder, or traits involved, shape the approach. For example, borderline personality disorder often begins with DBT or schema therapy, as described on the page about how BPD is treated, while avoidant patterns may respond well to CBT and schema-based work. When safety is a concern, stabilization and skills come first. The therapy relationship is central, so finding a therapist with relevant experience and a good fit is especially important. Access, cost, and readiness also play a role.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Change in personality-related patterns is usually gradual. Early progress may include fewer crises, more awareness of patterns, and slightly calmer responses in difficult situations. Over months, many people notice steadier relationships, less intense emotional swings, more flexible thinking, and a clearer sense of self. Treatment often lasts a year or more. Setbacks are common, particularly during stress or relationship changes, and are part of the process. Research suggests many people with personality disorders improve over time, especially with specialized treatment.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Practising skills learned in therapy, such as emotion regulation or communication skills, between sessions is important. Noticing patterns in daily life, and reflecting on them with curiosity rather than shame, helps. Looking after sleep, limiting alcohol and drugs, and building supportive relationships make change easier. Being honest with your therapist, including about difficult feelings toward therapy itself, strengthens the work.

Common Questions About Personality Disorder Treatment

Is real change possible with a personality disorder?

Yes. With consistent, well-matched therapy, many people with personality disorders experience meaningful change over time.

What therapies are used to treat personality disorders?

DBT, schema therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused and psychodynamic therapy, CBT, and group therapy are used for personality disorders.

What role do prescriptions play in personality disorder care?

No medication treats personality disorders directly, though a physician may treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, or sleep problems.

How long does treatment for a personality disorder take?

Treatment for personality disorders is usually longer-term, often a year or more, because it addresses deep patterns.

How does the bond with a therapist help people with personality disorders?

The therapy relationship is central in personality disorder treatment, offering a safe place to understand and change relationship patterns.

Do OCPD and BPD call for different therapy plans?

OCPD is often treated with CBT and schema-based work on rigid rules, while BPD often starts with DBT or schema therapy.

Are personality disorders caused by bad character?

No. Personality disorders often develop from temperament combined with difficult early experiences, not character flaws.

What are the benefits of group sessions for personality disorders?

Yes. Group therapy can help people with personality disorders practise new ways of relating in a supportive setting.

What does progress look like in personality disorder treatment?

Progress in personality disorder treatment often includes fewer crises, steadier relationships, calmer reactions, and a clearer sense of self.

How can I support my own progress in therapy for a personality disorder?

Practise skills between sessions, notice patterns with curiosity, protect sleep, limit substances, and be honest with your therapist.
