What Is ADHD? The Three Presentations Explained

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or ADHD, is a neurodevelopmental condition that affects attention, activity level, and impulse control. It begins in childhood and often continues into adulthood. ADHD is not a lack of intelligence or effort. It reflects differences in how the brain manages executive functions such as focus, planning, working memory, and self-regulation. Clinicians describe three presentations of ADHD, based on which symptoms are most prominent: predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined. A person's presentation can change over time, especially as hyperactivity often becomes less visible in adulthood.

The Three Presentations

Predominantly inattentive presentation. Mainly difficulties with attention and organization, such as:

  • Trouble sustaining focus, especially on tasks that are not interesting.
  • Careless mistakes and overlooking details.
  • Forgetting appointments, tasks, and belongings.
  • Difficulty organizing and finishing tasks.
  • Seeming not to listen or drifting off in conversations.

This presentation is often missed, as described on the page about inattentive ADHD.

Predominantly hyperactive-impulsive presentation. Mainly restlessness and impulsivity, such as:

  • Fidgeting, squirming, or finding it hard to sit still.
  • Talking excessively.
  • Interrupting others without meaning to.
  • Difficulty waiting for a turn.
  • Acting or making decisions quickly without thinking them through.

Combined presentation. Significant symptoms of both inattention and hyperactivity-impulsivity. This is the most commonly diagnosed presentation.

Why It Happens

ADHD is highly heritable and often runs in families. Research shows differences in brain development and in how networks involved in attention, reward, and self-control function. Chemical messengers such as dopamine and norepinephrine play a role. Environmental factors, such as premature birth or exposure to certain substances during pregnancy, can increase risk. ADHD is not caused by parenting, screen time, or sugar, although environments can influence how symptoms show up.

Presentations can look different across ages and people. Children with hyperactive symptoms are often noticed early, while those with inattentive symptoms may be overlooked. In adults, hyperactivity may show up as inner restlessness rather than obvious movement. Girls and women are more likely to have inattentive presentations and be diagnosed later, as explored on the page about ADHD in women.

ADHD often occurs alongside other conditions, including anxiety, depression, learning differences, autism, and sleep problems. These can complicate diagnosis and should be considered in assessment.

Each presentation brings its own strengths as well as challenges. People with hyperactive traits may bring energy, enthusiasm, and a willingness to act quickly in a crisis. People with inattentive traits may be imaginative, curious, and able to see connections others miss. Understanding your own profile can help you design routines, work, and relationships that fit.

Symptoms also depend on context. Many people with ADHD focus well on tasks that are urgent, novel, or deeply interesting, and struggle with tasks that are routine or delayed in reward. This variability can lead others to assume the person could concentrate if they tried harder, when in fact attention is regulated differently.

What It Is Not

  • Not laziness or lack of willpower. ADHD reflects brain-based differences.
  • Not only a childhood condition. Many adults continue to experience symptoms.
  • Not always obvious hyperactivity. Inattentive ADHD can be quiet and hidden.
  • Not caused by poor parenting.
  • Not a fixed category. Presentations can shift as people grow and circumstances change.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a detailed history of symptoms from childhood onward, questionnaires, and input from others such as parents, teachers, or partners. Clinicians also consider other conditions that can look like ADHD or occur alongside it. Treatment may include medication, which many people find helpful, along with psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for ADHD, coaching, and practical strategies such as routines, reminders, and environmental changes. For children, parent training and school supports are important. Treatment focuses on the person's specific challenges and strengths.

Common Questions About ADHD Presentations

What are the three presentations of ADHD?

Predominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive-impulsive, and combined presentation.

Which ADHD presentation is most common?

The combined presentation is the most commonly diagnosed, though inattentive ADHD is often underdiagnosed.

Can an ADHD presentation change over time?

Yes. Hyperactivity often becomes less visible with age, so presentations can shift in adolescence and adulthood.

Why do some people still say ADD?

ADD is an older term that stuck in everyday language. Today, what was called ADD is usually described as the predominantly inattentive presentation of ADHD.

Where does ADHD come from?

ADHD is highly heritable and involves differences in brain development. It is not caused by parenting or sugar.

Can adults be diagnosed with ADHD?

Yes. Many adults are diagnosed later in life, especially those with inattentive symptoms.

What does hyperactivity look like in adults?

It often shows up as inner restlessness, fidgeting, talking a lot, or difficulty relaxing rather than running around.

What does an ADHD evaluation look at?

It involves a detailed history, questionnaires, and input from others, along with ruling out other explanations.

What conditions often occur with ADHD?

Anxiety, depression, learning differences, autism, and sleep problems commonly occur alongside ADHD.

Which supports make daily life easier with ADHD?

Medication, CBT adapted for ADHD, coaching, psychoeducation, and practical strategies are commonly used.
