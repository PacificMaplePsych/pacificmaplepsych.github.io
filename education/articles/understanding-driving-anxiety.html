Understanding Driving Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Driving anxiety is fear or distress about driving or being a passenger, ranging from mild nervousness on highways to intense fear that leads people to stop driving altogether. It often focuses on specific situations, such as bridges, merging, highways, tunnels, or driving alone. It can develop after an accident, a panic attack while driving, or gradually without a clear cause. Driving anxiety is common and responds well to treatment, especially gradual exposure.

If you are looking for a first-person look at this experience, the page on being scared of driving covers the everyday side. This page explains driving anxiety as a condition: its forms, causes, and treatment.

What Driving Anxiety Looks Like

  • Racing heart, sweating, gripping the wheel, or dizziness while driving or before a trip.
  • Avoiding highways, bridges, left turns, night driving, or unfamiliar routes.
  • Taking long detours to avoid feared roads.
  • Only driving with a passenger, or only as a passenger.
  • Worrying for days before a necessary drive.
  • Fear of losing control, crashing, or having a panic attack at the wheel.

At work, it can limit job options or commuting. At home, it can shift driving duties to a partner. Socially, it can make people decline invitations that involve travel.

Why It Happens

Driving anxiety can begin in several ways. A collision or near miss can create a strong link between driving and danger. A panic attack in the car can lead to fear of another one, especially in places that are hard to exit, such as bridges or highways. For others, general anxiety or a tendency to fear losing control attaches to driving. Avoidance then maintains the fear: each skipped drive brings relief, which teaches the brain that driving was dangerous. Over time, the list of avoided situations often grows.

What It Is Not

  • Not always a phobia. Some nervousness about driving, especially when new or in bad weather, is normal and sensible.
  • Not necessarily PTSD. After an accident, fear of driving may be one part of post-traumatic stress, which also involves intrusive memories, nightmares, and hypervigilance. The page on feeling unsafe when nothing is wrong describes that pattern.
  • Sometimes panic disorder or agoraphobia. When fear centres on having a panic attack in places that are hard to escape, driving anxiety may be part of a broader panic or agoraphobia pattern.
  • Not usually medical, but worth checking when symptoms are unusual. Dizziness, vision changes, or episodes of confusion while driving should be checked by a doctor, since some medical conditions affect driving safety.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A psychologist assesses driving anxiety by exploring what situations are feared, what the person expects will happen, how avoidance affects life, and whether trauma or panic are involved. Cognitive behavioural therapy with graded exposure is the main treatment. It usually starts with small steps, such as sitting in a parked car, then quiet streets, and gradually more challenging roads, while learning to tolerate anxiety without escaping. When an accident is involved, trauma-focused therapy may be added. Many people return to confident driving with treatment. The general principles are the same as for other specific fears.

Common Questions About Driving Anxiety

How common is driving anxiety?

Driving anxiety is common, ranging from mild nervousness to fear that stops people from driving. Many people experience it at some point, especially after a frightening event on the road.

Is driving anxiety considered a phobia?

When the fear is intense, persistent, and leads to avoidance, clinicians may consider it a specific phobia. Milder nervousness is common and not a disorder.

Why do bridges and highways feel scariest?

Bridges and highways are harder to exit quickly and involve speed, so they often trigger fears of losing control or having a panic attack with nowhere to stop.

Can a panic attack while driving cause lasting fear?

Yes. One panic attack behind the wheel can lead to fear of another, and avoiding driving can then make the fear stronger over time.

How does graded exposure work for driving anxiety?

It involves facing driving situations in small steps, from easy to harder, and staying with the anxiety until it settles, which builds confidence gradually.

Should I stop driving if I have driving anxiety?

Avoiding driving usually strengthens the fear. Unless there is a medical or safety reason, gradual practice with support is generally more helpful.

Can being a passenger trigger driving anxiety too?

Yes. Some people feel more anxious as passengers because they lack control. This can also be addressed with graded exposure.

How long does it take to overcome fear of driving?

Many people see improvement within several months of regular practice and therapy. Progress depends on how long avoidance has lasted and whether trauma is involved.

Is driving anxiety after an accident different from other driving fears?

It may involve trauma responses such as intrusive memories and hypervigilance, so therapy often combines exposure with trauma-focused work.

What does a psychologist ask about in a driving anxiety assessment?

They explore feared situations, expected outcomes, avoidance, any accidents or panic attacks, and how the fear affects daily life, to tailor a treatment plan.
