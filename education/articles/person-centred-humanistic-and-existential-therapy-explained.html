Person-Centred, Humanistic, and Existential Therapy Explained

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Person-centred, humanistic, and existential therapies are related approaches that focus on the whole person rather than only on symptoms. Humanistic therapy is a broad family built on the belief that people have an inherent capacity for growth and self-understanding. Person-centred therapy, one of its best-known forms, emphasizes a warm, accepting, and genuine relationship in which people can explore their feelings and find their own answers. Existential therapy explores the big questions of life, such as meaning, freedom, choice, isolation, and mortality. All three tend to be less structured than skills-based therapies, and they suit people who want space to reflect, understand themselves, and grow.

How These Approaches Work

Humanistic Therapy

Humanistic therapy developed in the mid-twentieth century as an alternative to approaches that focused mainly on pathology. It sees people as whole beings with the ability to make choices, grow, and move toward fulfilment. It values subjective experience, what life feels like from the inside, and emphasizes self-awareness, personal responsibility, and authenticity. Gestalt therapy and emotion-focused therapy are also part of this broader tradition, as described on the page about emotion-focused therapy.

Person-Centred Therapy

Person-centred therapy was developed by psychologist Carl Rogers. It holds that people are the experts on their own lives and will naturally move toward growth when they feel understood and accepted. Therapists offer three core conditions: empathy, a genuine effort to understand the person's experience; unconditional positive regard, acceptance without judgment; and congruence, being genuine and real. In this atmosphere, people can explore feelings they may have hidden, reduce the gap between who they are and who they feel they should be, and develop greater self-acceptance. This can be helpful for people struggling with low self-esteem.

Existential Therapy

Existential therapy draws on philosophy and focuses on the core concerns of human existence: finding meaning, facing freedom and responsibility, living with uncertainty, experiencing isolation, and confronting mortality. It sees anxiety and distress as natural responses to these realities, not simply symptoms. Therapy helps people face these questions honestly, clarify what matters to them, and make authentic choices. It can be especially meaningful during midlife transitions, loss, illness, or times of change.

What Sessions Are Like

Person-Centred and Humanistic Sessions

Sessions are usually led by the client, who chooses what to talk about. The therapist listens closely, reflects back what they hear, and offers understanding rather than advice or interpretation. There is typically no homework or set agenda. Some humanistic therapists, such as Gestalt therapists, use experiential techniques, such as focusing on body sensations or speaking to an empty chair. Therapy length varies from a few months to longer-term work.

Existential Sessions

Existential therapy sessions involve exploring your experience and the deeper questions it raises. A therapist might ask what gives your life meaning, what choices you face, or what you are avoiding. The tone is collaborative and reflective, with the therapist as a fellow human exploring life's challenges alongside you. Sessions are typically unstructured and can be short-term or long-term.

How They Compare

All three share a focus on the whole person, the therapeutic relationship, and personal growth. Person-centred therapy emphasizes acceptance and the client's own direction. Humanistic therapy is the broader umbrella and may include more active or experiential techniques. Existential therapy focuses on meaning, choice, and life's big questions. Compared with structured approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy, these therapies involve less skills training and more open exploration. Research suggests humanistic therapies can help with depression, anxiety, and relationship difficulties, and the therapeutic relationship they emphasize is one of the strongest predictors of outcome across therapies.

These approaches suit people who want to understand themselves, explore identity or meaning, or navigate transitions, including those described on the page about identity loss after a life transition. People who want structured tools for specific symptoms, such as panic attacks or OCD, may benefit more from targeted approaches, sometimes combined with humanistic work. Values-based approaches such as acceptance and commitment therapy share some existential themes while offering more structure.

Common Questions About Person-Centred, Humanistic and Existential Therapy

What is person-centred therapy?

It is a humanistic approach, developed by Carl Rogers, that offers empathy, acceptance, and genuineness so people can explore and grow.

What are the three core conditions in person-centred therapy?

Empathy, unconditional positive regard, and congruence, meaning the therapist is genuine and real.

What does existential therapy focus on?

It explores meaning, freedom, choice, isolation, uncertainty, and mortality, and how these shape our lives.

How is humanistic therapy different from CBT?

Humanistic therapy focuses on open exploration and growth, while CBT is more structured and skills-based.

Will a person-centred therapist give me advice?

Usually not. Person-centred therapists listen, reflect, and support you in finding your own answers.

Is there homework in humanistic therapy?

Typically there is no set homework, though some therapists may suggest reflection between sessions.

Can existential therapy help during a life transition?

Yes. It can be especially meaningful during transitions, loss, illness, or times of change.

Who benefits most from person-centred therapy?

People who want space to explore feelings, build self-acceptance, and find their own direction often benefit.

Is humanistic therapy supported by research?

Research suggests it can help with depression, anxiety, and relationship difficulties, and it emphasizes the therapeutic relationship, a strong predictor of outcomes.

What is Gestalt therapy?

It is a humanistic approach that uses present-moment awareness and experiential techniques, such as the empty chair.
