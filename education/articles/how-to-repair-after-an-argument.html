How to Repair After an Argument

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Repairing after an argument usually involves calming down first, then coming back together to acknowledge what happened, take responsibility for your part, understand each other's feelings, and agree on what might help next time. Every couple argues. Research on relationships suggests the ability to repair, rather than the absence of conflict, is one of the strongest signs of a healthy relationship. Repair does not mean deciding who was right. It means restoring a sense of safety and connection so the argument does not linger as resentment. This page focuses on the repair conversation itself in the hours or days after a fight.

Why Repair Helps

During an argument, the body's stress response can make it hard to think clearly or listen. People may say things they do not mean, withdraw, or become defensive. If the argument ends without repair, hurt can linger and quietly shape future interactions. Over time, unrepaired arguments can lead to distance and cycles of conflict, such as the patterns described on the page about conflict styles.

Repair calms the nervous system, shows that the relationship is still safe, and helps partners understand what really went on underneath the argument. Research-based approaches such as the Gottman Method emphasize repair as a core relationship skill. Repair also makes it easier to rebuild closeness over time, as described on the page about rebuilding intimacy after conflict.

Strategies to Try

A repair conversation often follows these steps:

  1. Calm down first. Take at least twenty minutes apart if either of you feels flooded. Do something soothing, such as walking or breathing slowly, rather than replaying the argument.
  2. Signal you want to reconnect. A simple message such as "I do not like how that went. Can we talk when we are both ready?" opens the door.
  3. Take turns describing your experience. Each person shares what they felt and needed during the argument, using I statements, while the other listens without interrupting or defending.
  4. Reflect back what you heard. Summarize your partner's view, such as "It sounds like you felt dismissed when I kept looking at my phone." Understanding does not require agreement.
  5. Take responsibility for your part. Name specific things you regret, such as raising your voice or bringing up old issues. A sincere apology names the behaviour and its impact without adding "but."
  6. Look for the deeper need. Many arguments are about feeling unimportant, criticized, or unsupported. Naming these needs often shifts the conversation.
  7. Agree on something for next time. This might be a signal to take a break, a different way to raise the topic, or a practical change.
  8. Reconnect. End with a hug, a kind word, or shared time together.

Small repair attempts during an argument also help, such as humour, a softer tone, saying "I am getting overwhelmed," or acknowledging a fair point. Practical ideas for wording difficult topics are on the page about communication skills for difficult conversations.

Common Obstacles

One partner may be ready to repair sooner than the other. Agreeing on a time to come back together helps both feel respected. Some people find apologizing very hard, especially if they grew up in families where apologies were rare or used as weapons. Starting small, such as acknowledging one specific thing, can help. Repair can also stall if the conversation turns into a second argument about who is more to blame. If this happens, pause and try again later.

Repeated arguments about the same issue often point to a deeper difference or need. These may not be fully solvable, but couples can learn to talk about them with more understanding. When one partner consistently avoids repair or refuses to take any responsibility, resentment can build. Counselling can help.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If arguments are frequent, escalate quickly, involve contempt, or rarely get repaired, couples counselling can help partners understand their patterns and practise repair. If arguments involve threats, intimidation, or physical harm, safety comes first, and couples counselling is not recommended until safety is established. Support is available through abuse resources. If you are in danger, call 911.

Common Questions About Repairing After an Argument

How soon should couples try to repair after an argument?

Wait until both partners feel calm, often at least twenty minutes, then come back together within the same day or two if possible.

What are the steps of a repair conversation after a fight?

Calm down, signal you want to reconnect, take turns sharing feelings, reflect back what you heard, take responsibility, name deeper needs, and agree on next steps.

How do I apologize after an argument without making it worse?

Name the specific behaviour and its impact, express regret, and avoid adding excuses or a but after the apology.

What if my partner is not ready to repair after our argument?

Respect their need for time and agree on when you will talk. Pushing for repair too soon can reopen the argument.

What are small repair attempts during an argument?

Using humour, softening your tone, admitting you feel overwhelmed, or acknowledging a fair point can calm an argument as it happens.

Why do we keep having the same argument even after we repair?

Recurring arguments often reflect a deeper difference or need that may not be fully solvable but can be discussed with more understanding.

Does repairing after a fight mean admitting I was completely wrong?

No. Repair means acknowledging your part and understanding your partner, not deciding who was right.

What should I do if our repair conversation turns into another argument?

Pause, take a break to calm down, and try the repair conversation again later with a gentler start.

Why is repair after conflict so important for relationships?

Repair restores safety and connection after conflict, and research links it with healthier, longer-lasting relationships.

When should a couple get counselling for arguments that never get repaired?

If arguments are frequent, escalate quickly, involve contempt, or rarely get repaired, couples counselling can help.
