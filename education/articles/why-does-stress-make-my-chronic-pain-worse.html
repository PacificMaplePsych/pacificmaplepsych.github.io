Why Does Stress Make My Chronic Pain Worse

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Stress makes chronic pain worse because the brain and nervous system are directly involved in how pain is felt. Under stress, muscles tighten, sleep suffers, inflammation can increase, and the nervous system becomes more sensitive, turning up the volume on pain signals. Pain then adds to stress, creating a cycle that can feel impossible to escape. This does not mean the pain is imagined. It means stress is one of the factors influencing a real condition, and addressing it can bring meaningful relief.

People with conditions such as back pain, arthritis, migraines, fibromyalgia, or irritable bowel syndrome often notice flares during difficult weeks at work, family conflict, or periods of poor sleep.

New pain, a sudden change in your usual pain, or pain with fever, weakness, numbness, or loss of bladder or bowel control should be checked by a doctor promptly. Call 911 for chest pain or a sudden, severe headache.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Muscle tension. Stress causes muscles to tighten, particularly in the neck, shoulders, jaw, and back. People often feel tense in their shoulders and jaw without noticing it, adding to existing pain.

Poor sleep. Stress disrupts sleep, and even a few bad nights lower pain tolerance the next day. Pain can also keep people awake, feeding the cycle.

Changes in activity. Stress can lead to overdoing it on good days or stopping activity altogether, both of which can make pain worse.

Mood. Depression and anxiety often accompany chronic pain and affect how intensely it is felt.

The body's stress response. Stress hormones influence inflammation and the immune system, which can affect some pain conditions.

A new medical issue. Not every increase in pain is stress-related. Changes should be discussed with your doctor.

When It Looks More Like a Stress and Pain Cycle

Chronic pain often involves central sensitization, where the nervous system becomes more reactive over time and amplifies pain signals. Stress is one of the strongest influences on this system. Signs that stress and pain are feeding each other include:

  • Pain flares that follow stressful events, conflict, or deadlines.
  • Pain that eases on calmer days or holidays.
  • Worry about pain, which increases attention to it and makes it feel worse.
  • Avoiding activities for fear of pain, leading to deconditioning and more pain.
  • Other stress-related physical symptoms, such as headaches or stomach aches.
  • Low mood, frustration, or hopelessness about the pain.

Understanding this cycle is empowering, because it shows where you can intervene. Reducing stress, improving sleep, and changing how you respond to pain can all lower pain intensity and improve day-to-day function. Research supports psychological approaches as part of chronic pain care, usually alongside medical treatment. For people whose symptoms have no clear medical cause, the page on physical symptoms doctors cannot explain may also help.

What Can Help

  • Track stress and pain together. Noting both for a few weeks often reveals patterns and triggers.
  • Relax the body regularly. Slow breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, or gentle stretching can reduce tension and calm the nervous system.
  • Pace activity. Aim for steady, moderate activity rather than big efforts on good days followed by crashes.
  • Protect sleep. A consistent sleep schedule and wind-down routine can raise pain tolerance.
  • Stay connected. Isolation increases stress and focus on pain. Social contact helps.
  • Work with your care team. Physiotherapists, doctors, and psychologists can each address different parts of the pain experience.

Common Questions About Stress and Chronic Pain

Why does my back or joint pain get worse during stressful weeks?

Yes. Stress increases muscle tension, disrupts sleep, affects inflammation, and makes the nervous system more sensitive, all of which can make existing pain feel stronger.

Does this mean my chronic pain is psychological?

No. Chronic pain is real. Stress is one of several factors that influence how intensely pain is felt. Addressing stress is part of treating the whole condition, not a suggestion that pain is imagined.

What is central sensitization?

Central sensitization is a process where the nervous system becomes more reactive and amplifies pain signals. It is common in many chronic pain conditions and is influenced by stress and sleep.

Why do my pain flares happen after stressful events?

Stress triggers physical changes such as tension and heightened nerve sensitivity, and it often disrupts sleep. These changes can lead to flares hours or days after a stressful event.

Can chronic pain cause anxiety and depression?

Yes. Living with ongoing pain is stressful and can limit activities and relationships, which increases the risk of anxiety and depression. These, in turn, can make pain feel worse.

How does poor sleep affect chronic pain?

Poor sleep lowers pain tolerance and increases sensitivity the next day. Improving sleep is often one of the most effective ways to reduce pain intensity.

Which calming practices can lower chronic pain levels?

Slow diaphragmatic breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, and mindfulness practices can reduce tension and calm the nervous system, which may lower pain levels.

What is pacing for chronic pain?

Pacing means spreading activity evenly across days, with regular breaks, rather than doing too much on good days and crashing afterward. It helps build tolerance and reduce flares.

Is it helpful to avoid activities that might cause pain?

Short-term avoidance can make sense during a flare, but long-term avoidance often leads to stiffness, weakness, and more pain. Gradual, guided activity is usually more helpful.

What psychological approaches are used for chronic pain?

Cognitive behavioural therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy are commonly used. They help people manage stress, change unhelpful responses to pain, and build a fuller life alongside it.
