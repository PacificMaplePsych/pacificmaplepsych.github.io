Why Can't I Stop Checking Things Over and Over

You checked the stove is off. You are already down the street. You check again anyway, or turn back to check in person, even though some part of you is fairly confident it really is off. The relief from checking never quite lasts, and the same doubt tends to resurface the next time, with the next door, the next appliance, the next message you may or may not have sent. If repeated checking has become a significant part of your day, it is worth understanding what is actually driving it.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Genuine forgetfulness. For some people, checking is less about doubt and more about honestly not remembering whether something was done, often connected to attention or working memory difficulties rather than anxiety about a feared outcome.

Situational anxiety. A specific stressful period, a recent break-in nearby, a recent mistake at work, can understandably increase checking behaviour temporarily around a particular concern, without reflecting a broader or ongoing pattern.

General anxiety. Anxiety more broadly can increase a need for reassurance and certainty, which checking behaviour can serve, even without meeting the criteria for OCD specifically.

High responsibility or high stakes contexts. Some degree of checking is entirely reasonable and adaptive in contexts where a real, significant risk exists, for example, double-checking medication dosages in certain jobs, and should not automatically be pathologized.

When It Looks More Like OCD

Checking behaviour connected to OCD tends to follow a specific and often exhausting pattern: an intrusive doubt or feared outcome (did I lock the door, did I leave the stove on, could something bad happen because of this) creates intense anxiety, checking temporarily relieves that anxiety, and then the doubt returns, sometimes within minutes, restarting the cycle. Crucially, the relief from checking is almost always short-lived, and more checking does not produce lasting certainty, since the underlying issue is not actually about the door or the stove, it is about an intolerance of uncertainty and doubt itself.

A distinguishing feature is that the level of doubt often does not track with the actual evidence available. Someone might check a locked door five, ten, or more times, having clearly seen and even felt that it was locked each time, and still experience genuine doubt moments later. This is not about poor memory or carelessness, in fact, people experiencing this kind of checking often have excellent memory for the actual checking itself. It reflects a specific kind of anxious doubt that repeated confirmation does not seem to resolve.

This pattern also tends to expand over time if left unaddressed, sometimes starting with one specific concern, like the stove, and gradually including more items, door locks, whether a text message was sent correctly, whether a work email contained an error, as the same underlying difficulty with uncertainty attaches itself to new situations.

What Can Help

For OCD-related checking specifically, the most effective treatment is a structured form of therapy called exposure and response prevention, which involves deliberately practicing tolerating the doubt without checking, gradually retraining the brain's response to uncertainty rather than trying to eliminate the doubt itself, which checking cannot reliably do anyway. Simple strategies like taking a photo of a locked door or turned-off appliance as visual proof can sometimes help interrupt milder patterns, though for more significant checking, professional treatment tends to be considerably more effective than self-management alone.

Common Questions About Repeated Checking

Is checking something once or twice normal?

Yes, entirely. Checking a lock or an appliance once or even twice, particularly in a genuinely high-stakes situation, is a reasonable and adaptive behaviour. It becomes more clinically relevant when checking is repeated excessively and does not provide lasting relief.

Why doesn't checking make the doubt go away?

In OCD specifically, the underlying issue is not really about the object being checked, it is about an intolerance of uncertainty itself. Since checking cannot fully eliminate uncertainty, the relief it provides tends to be brief, and the doubt returns.

Could this be about forgetfulness rather than OCD?

Yes, this is a genuine possibility. Some checking is driven by honestly not remembering whether something was done, which is more connected to attention or working memory than to the specific anxious doubt-relief cycle seen in OCD.

Why do I doubt myself even when I clearly remember checking?

This is a hallmark feature of OCD-related checking. The doubt is not really about memory accuracy, people experiencing this often have excellent memory for the checking itself, it is about a persistent, disproportionate sense of uncertainty that clear evidence does not resolve.

Does checking behaviour tend to get worse over time?

It can, if left unaddressed. The pattern sometimes starts with one specific concern and gradually expands to include new situations, as the same underlying difficulty with uncertainty attaches itself to additional worries.

Is some checking actually reasonable and not a sign of anything clinical?

Yes. In contexts involving genuine risk or high responsibility, a reasonable level of checking is adaptive and appropriate, and should not automatically be treated as a symptom, particularly if it does not cause significant distress or repeat excessively.

What is exposure and response prevention, and how does it help with checking?

It is a specific, well-researched form of therapy for OCD that involves gradually practicing tolerating the urge to check without acting on it, which helps retrain the brain's response to uncertainty over time rather than trying to eliminate doubt directly.

Can taking a photo as proof actually help reduce checking?

For milder patterns, yes, it can provide a concrete point of reference to return to instead of re-checking in person. For more significant or distressing checking, this strategy alone is usually not enough, and professional treatment tends to be more effective.

Is general anxiety enough to explain frequent checking, or does it need to be OCD?

General anxiety alone can increase checking behaviour without necessarily meeting the criteria for OCD. What distinguishes OCD specifically is the intrusive, doubt-driven quality of the thoughts and the short-lived, cycle-like nature of the relief from checking.

What would an assessment look at regarding checking behaviour?

An assessment would look at how much time checking takes up, whether it provides lasting relief or short-lived relief followed by returning doubt, whether other OCD-related patterns are present, and whether general anxiety or forgetfulness better explains it.
