How Is Anxiety Treated: An Overview of Options

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Anxiety is most often treated with psychotherapy, especially cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and exposure-based approaches, sometimes alongside medication prescribed by a physician and lifestyle changes. Other therapies, such as acceptance and commitment therapy and mindfulness-based approaches, also help many people. Treatment is tailored to the type of anxiety, such as generalized anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, specific phobias, or health anxiety, and to its severity. Anxiety disorders are among the most treatable mental health conditions, and many people see significant improvement within a few months of consistent treatment.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT has the strongest evidence for most anxiety disorders. It helps people identify anxious thinking patterns, test fears, reduce avoidance and safety behaviours, and build coping skills.

Exposure therapy. Gradually facing feared situations, sensations, or thoughts is a core part of treating phobias, panic, social anxiety, and OCD-related anxiety, as explained on the page about how exposure therapy treats phobias.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people make room for anxious thoughts and feelings while acting on their values, rather than fighting anxiety.

Mindfulness-based approaches. Mindfulness programs can reduce stress and worry for many people.

Medication. Certain antidepressants are common first-line medications for anxiety disorders, with other options used in specific situations. These are prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist, as described on the page about medication for anxiety.

Lifestyle supports. Regular exercise, consistent sleep, limiting caffeine and alcohol, and social connection support any treatment, as covered on the page about lifestyle changes that reduce anxiety.

Group and digital options. Group CBT and guided online CBT programs can be effective and more accessible for some people.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan begins with understanding the type and severity of anxiety, as described on the page about how anxiety is diagnosed. Mild to moderate anxiety often responds well to therapy alone. More severe anxiety, or anxiety with depression, may benefit from therapy combined with medication. Co-occurring conditions, such as ADHD, trauma, OCD, or substance use, shape the approach. Personal preferences matter, such as whether you want to try medication and whether you prefer a structured or more exploratory therapy. Practical factors, such as cost, access, and whether virtual sessions suit you, also play a role.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Early progress often includes understanding your anxiety pattern and noticing small changes, such as worrying less often, recovering faster from anxious moments, or doing something you had been avoiding. Many structured anxiety treatments run around eight to twenty sessions, with meaningful change often within a few months. Medication usually takes a few weeks to start helping. Progress is rarely a straight line, and anxiety may temporarily increase during exposure work before it decreases. Setbacks during stressful periods are common and are a chance to practise skills again.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Practising skills and exposures between sessions is one of the most important parts of anxiety treatment. Gradually reducing avoidance, even in small ways, builds confidence. Limiting reassurance-seeking and checking helps break anxiety cycles. Protecting sleep, moving regularly, and reducing caffeine support your body. Being patient and kind with yourself makes it easier to keep going. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or symptoms that feel new or severe, seek medical care or call 911.

Common Questions About Anxiety Treatment

Which approach do anxiety guidelines usually recommend first?

Guidelines usually recommend CBT, including exposure-based work, as a first treatment for most anxiety disorders, sometimes combined with medication.

Do I need medication to treat my anxiety?

Not necessarily. Mild to moderate anxiety often responds well to therapy alone, while more severe anxiety may benefit from both.

How many months of therapy do most people need to see their anxiety ease?

Many structured anxiety treatments run around eight to twenty sessions, with meaningful change often within a few months.

Why does exposure matter so much in anxiety treatment?

Avoidance keeps anxiety going, so gradually facing feared situations helps the brain learn they are manageable.

Would acceptance and commitment therapy suit me if fighting my anxiety has not worked?

It may. ACT helps people make room for anxious thoughts and feelings while acting on their values, rather than struggling against anxiety.

Are online CBT programs effective for anxiety?

Guided online CBT programs can be effective for anxiety and are more accessible for some people.

Is anxiety treatment different for panic disorder and social anxiety?

The core approaches are similar, but anxiety treatment is tailored, such as interoceptive exposure for panic and social exposures for social anxiety.

Will my anxiety get worse before it gets better in treatment?

Anxiety may temporarily increase during exposure work in treatment, which usually eases as you practise.

What can I do between sessions to help anxiety treatment work?

Practise skills and exposures, reduce avoidance and reassurance-seeking, protect sleep, and limit caffeine to support anxiety treatment.

How do I know which anxiety treatment is right for me?

A clinician can assess the type and severity of your anxiety and help you choose a treatment that fits your goals and preferences.
