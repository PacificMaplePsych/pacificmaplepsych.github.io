Family Therapy: What It Involves

Family therapy is a form of psychotherapy that works with two or more family members together to improve communication, resolve conflict, and help the family support one another through a difficulty. Rather than treating one person as "the problem," it looks at the patterns between people: how they talk, solve problems, show care, and respond to stress. Family therapy can involve parents and children, siblings, adult children and ageing parents, blended families, or any group that thinks of itself as family. It is used for many concerns, including parent and teen conflict, a child's mental health difficulties, adjusting to separation or a new blended family, grief, illness, and supporting a family member with an eating disorder or substance use concern.

How Family Therapy Works

Family therapy is based on the idea that families are systems. When one person struggles, everyone is affected, and the way the family responds can either ease or maintain the difficulty. A family therapist helps members notice these patterns and try new ways of relating. The goal is not to assign blame but to build understanding and practical skills.

Common Approaches

  • Structural family therapy. Focuses on roles, boundaries, and hierarchy, such as helping parents work as a team.
  • Systemic and strategic approaches. Explore repeating cycles of interaction and try small, deliberate changes to interrupt them.
  • Emotionally focused family therapy. Builds on attachment ideas to help family members express needs and respond to one another with care, related to the approach described on the page about emotion-focused therapy.
  • Family-based treatment for eating disorders. A well-researched approach for adolescents in which parents take an active role in supporting recovery, as noted on the page about therapy approaches for eating disorders.
  • Narrative and solution-focused approaches. Help families separate people from problems and build on strengths that already exist.

What Family Therapy Sessions Are Like

The first meeting usually involves getting to know each family member, hearing everyone's view of what is happening, and agreeing on goals. Sessions are often 50 to 90 minutes. Depending on the situation, the therapist may meet with the whole family, with parents alone, with a parent and one child, or with individuals for part of the time. The therapist makes sure each person, including younger children, has a chance to be heard, and sets ground rules so that sessions feel safe rather than turning into arguments.

Work in sessions can include practising how to listen and speak without escalating, problem-solving a recurring conflict, clarifying household expectations, and talking through painful events. Families are often asked to try new approaches between sessions. Family therapy is commonly shorter term, often somewhere between a handful and about 20 sessions, though this varies with the concern. Confidentiality is discussed at the start, including how information shared in individual meetings will be handled and the legal limits on confidentiality that apply when someone's safety is at risk.

Who Family Therapy Can Help

  • Families with frequent conflict. Especially between parents and teens, or among siblings.
  • Families adjusting to change. Separation, divorce, remarriage, a move, a new baby, or a parent's illness. Practical ideas for separated parents are covered on the page about co-parenting strategies after separation.
  • Families supporting a member with a mental health concern. Such as anxiety, depression, ADHD, an eating disorder, or substance use.
  • Families coping with grief or trauma. When a shared loss or difficult event affects everyone differently.
  • Adult families. Adult siblings or adult children and parents working through long-standing tension or care decisions for an ageing parent.

Family therapy may not be the right first step when there is ongoing abuse or violence in the home, because joint sessions can feel unsafe or increase risk. In those situations, individual support and safety planning come first. Family therapy is also not a replacement for individual treatment when one person needs specialized care, though the two often work well together. Parents who want guidance without bringing the whole family may find parent coaching a useful alternative.

Family Therapy Compared With Couples and Individual Therapy

Individual therapy focuses on one person's thoughts, feelings, and goals. Couples therapy, described on the page about how couples therapy works, focuses on the relationship between two partners. Family therapy widens the lens to the whole household or family group. Some families use a combination, such as couples sessions for parents and individual therapy for a teen.

Common Questions About Family Therapy

What is family therapy and how does it work?

Family therapy is psychotherapy with two or more family members together. It looks at patterns between people and helps the family communicate, resolve conflict, and support one another.

Does everyone in the family have to attend family therapy?

Not always. A family therapist may meet with the whole family, with parents alone, or with smaller groups, depending on the concern and who is willing to take part.

Is family therapy only for families with young children?

No. Family therapy can also help adult siblings, adult children and their parents, and blended families working through tension or major decisions.

How long does a typical course of family therapy last?

Family therapy is often shorter term, commonly between a handful and about 20 sessions, though the number depends on the family's goals and concerns.

Does family therapy mean someone in the family is to blame?

No. Family therapy avoids blaming any one person and instead focuses on understanding patterns and building new ways for the family to relate.

Can family therapy help a teen with an eating disorder?

Yes. Family-based treatment is a well-researched approach for adolescents with eating disorders in which parents take an active role in supporting recovery.

When might joint family sessions be unsafe?

Family therapy is usually not the first step when there is ongoing abuse or violence at home, because joint sessions can increase risk. Individual support and safety planning come first.

How is family therapy different from couples therapy?

Couples therapy focuses on two partners, while family therapy includes the wider family group, such as parents, children, or siblings, and the patterns among them.

What happens in the first family therapy session?

The first family therapy session usually involves hearing each person's view, agreeing on goals, and discussing ground rules and confidentiality.

Can family therapy be combined with individual therapy?

Yes. Many families combine family therapy with individual therapy for one member or couples sessions for parents, depending on their needs.
