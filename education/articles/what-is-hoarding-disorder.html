What Is Hoarding Disorder

Severe clutter can create fire, fall, and health hazards. If blocked exits, fire risks, or unsafe living conditions are present, contact your local fire department or health authority for guidance. Call 911 in an emergency.

Hoarding disorder is a mental health condition in which a person has persistent difficulty discarding or parting with possessions, regardless of their actual value. This difficulty comes from a perceived need to save items and strong distress at the thought of letting them go. Over time, possessions accumulate and clutter living areas, sometimes to the point that rooms cannot be used for their intended purpose. Hoarding disorder is classified with obsessive-compulsive and related conditions. It is more common than many people realize and often begins in adolescence, becoming more noticeable later in life. With patient, respectful support, people can make meaningful progress.

What Hoarding Disorder Looks Like

  • Great difficulty throwing away or giving away items, as in struggling to get rid of things you do not need.
  • Strong distress, anxiety, or indecision when trying to discard.
  • Clutter that fills counters, floors, beds, or entire rooms.
  • Acquiring more items, such as free things, bargains, or through compulsive buying.
  • Saving items because they might be useful or have sentimental value.
  • Embarrassment about having people visit.
  • Conflict with family, landlords, or neighbours about clutter.
  • Difficulty organizing or finding important items.

Some people recognize the problem, while others have limited insight and may not see the clutter as a concern, even when it affects safety.

Why It Happens

Hoarding disorder develops from a combination of factors. It often runs in families, and genetics appear to play a role. People with hoarding disorder frequently have difficulties with decision-making, categorizing, and attention, which make sorting and discarding very hard. Strong emotional attachment to objects, and beliefs such as I might need this or it would be wasteful to throw this away, drive saving. Objects may also provide comfort, a sense of safety, or a connection to memories and people.

Stressful or traumatic life events, such as loss, can trigger or worsen hoarding. Hoarding disorder often occurs alongside depression, anxiety, ADHD, and sometimes OCD, though it is distinct from OCD. Avoidance keeps it going: putting off decisions brings short-term relief but allows clutter to grow.

Hoarding often affects families deeply. Relatives may feel frustrated, worried about safety, or embarrassed, while the person who hoards may feel criticized and defensive. Forced clean-outs, where others remove items without consent, often cause great distress and rarely lead to lasting change, because the underlying difficulties remain.

What It Is Not

  • Not collecting. Collectors organize and display items with pride. Hoarding involves disorganized clutter and distress.
  • Not laziness or messiness. It involves real difficulties with decisions and letting go, unlike ordinary untidiness described on the page about a room that is always a mess.
  • Not solved by a one-time clean-out. Without treatment, clutter often returns.
  • Not a personal failing. It is a recognized mental health condition.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about acquiring, saving, clutter, distress, and how living spaces and relationships are affected, and may visit the home or review photos. Cognitive behavioural therapy for hoarding is the most studied treatment. It includes building motivation, learning skills for sorting and decision-making, practising discarding with gradual exposure, reducing acquiring, and challenging beliefs about possessions. Sessions may take place in the home. Treatment is usually gradual and collaborative, respecting the person's pace and choices. Support groups, family involvement, and professional organizers trained in hoarding can help. Treating co-occurring depression, anxiety, or ADHD supports progress.

Common Questions About Hoarding Disorder

When does keeping things become a mental health concern?

It becomes hoarding disorder when there is persistent difficulty discarding possessions, leading to clutter that fills living spaces and causes distress.

Is a big collection a sign of hoarding?

Not usually. Collectors organize and display items with pride, while hoarding involves disorganized clutter, distress, and difficulty using living spaces.

Why is hoarding grouped with obsessive-compulsive conditions?

It shares features such as distress and avoidance with those conditions, but it is a distinct diagnosis rather than a form of OCD.

Why is it so hard to throw things away?

People with hoarding disorder often feel strong attachment to items, fear needing them later, and struggle with decision-making.

Does a forced clean-out help someone who hoards?

Usually not. Removing items without consent causes distress and clutter often returns without treatment.

How can I help a family member who hoards?

Focus on safety and the relationship, avoid criticism, encourage treatment, and support gradual, collaborative change.

Is hoarding linked with ADHD?

Yes. Attention and decision-making difficulties associated with ADHD are common among people who hoard.

When does hoarding usually start?

Hoarding often begins in adolescence and becomes more severe and noticeable later in life.

How do people make progress sorting through years of possessions?

Progress usually comes gradually through CBT for hoarding, which involves building motivation, practising sorting and discarding, reducing acquiring, and challenging beliefs about possessions.

Can hoarding be dangerous?

Severe clutter can create fire, fall, and health risks. Safety planning is an important part of support.
