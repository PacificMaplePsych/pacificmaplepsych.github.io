Why Does My Heart Race for No Reason

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You are sitting on the couch, or standing in line at the grocery store, or lying in bed about to fall asleep, and suddenly your heart is pounding. Nothing obvious set it off. You were not running, you were not arguing with anyone, and you might not even have felt particularly worried a moment before. The sudden thumping can be startling, and it often leaves people wondering whether something is wrong with their heart, their nerves, or both.

A racing heart without an obvious trigger is very common, and it has many possible explanations, some physical, some emotional, and many a bit of both. This page walks through them calmly, rather than jumping straight to the most frightening one.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Your heart rate is constantly adjusting to what your body needs, and a lot of everyday factors can push it higher than you expect.

Caffeine, nicotine, and other stimulants. Coffee, energy drinks, pre-workout supplements, some cold and allergy medications, and nicotine can all speed up the heart. Sensitivity varies a lot from person to person, and the effect can show up hours after the last cup.

Dehydration, hunger, and alcohol. When you are low on fluids, your heart has to work a little harder to keep blood pressure steady. Low blood sugar can trigger a burst of adrenaline. Alcohol can raise heart rate that evening and during the night that follows, which is why some people notice pounding at 3 a.m. after a few drinks.

Illness, poor sleep, and hormones. Being run down, fighting off a cold, or shifts across the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and perimenopause can all raise your resting heart rate or make it more noticeable. See also Why Do My Moods Crash Before My Period.

Medical causes. An overactive thyroid, anemia, certain heart rhythm conditions, and some medications can all cause a fast heartbeat. This is why it matters to get new or unexplained heart symptoms checked by a doctor, especially if they come with chest pain, fainting, severe shortness of breath, or a heartbeat that feels chaotic or irregular. Those symptoms warrant prompt medical attention, and in an emergency, call 911. Related reading: Why Do I Feel Dizzy or Lightheaded When Anxious.

Anxiety and stress. Even when you are not consciously worried, a nervous system that has been running on high alert for a while can produce sudden surges of adrenaline. This is one of the most common reasons people experience a racing heart "for no reason," and it is the focus of the next section. We explore this further in Why Do I Feel Like I Can't Breathe When I'm Stressed.

When It Looks More Like Anxiety

Once medical causes have been ruled out, anxiety is often a large part of the picture. The body's alarm system, sometimes called the fight or flight response, is designed to speed up the heart so you can react quickly to danger. In people with anxiety, that system can become sensitive and fire in situations where there is no real threat.

A few patterns tend to suggest anxiety is involved:

  • The racing heart comes with other signs of activation, such as tight breathing, shaky hands, a hot flush, tingling, or a sudden sense of dread.
  • It tends to happen during or after stressful stretches of life, even if the moment itself feels calm. Stress often catches up with the body once you finally slow down.
  • Noticing your heartbeat makes it worse. Checking your pulse and worrying about it releases more adrenaline, which speeds the heart up further.
  • Medical tests have come back normal, yet the episodes keep happening.

That last loop is especially important. Anxiety does not only cause a racing heart, it also changes how you interpret it. When a normal fluctuation in heart rate is read as a sign of danger, the body responds as if the danger were real. Over time, people can develop a fear of the sensation itself and begin avoiding exercise, coffee, or anything else that might make their heart beat faster. That avoidance tends to keep the fear alive rather than easing it.

What Can Help

Start with a medical check if you have not had one, so you can approach the rest with more confidence. From there, a few practical steps tend to help:

  • Track patterns for a couple of weeks: time of day, caffeine, sleep, alcohol, and what was happening beforehand. Patterns often become clearer on paper.
  • Try slow breathing with a longer exhale than inhale. Extended exhales activate the parasympathetic nervous system, which helps bring the heart rate down.
  • Resist the urge to keep checking your pulse. Frequent checking tends to feed the worry loop described above.
  • Reintroduce gentle movement if you have been avoiding it, since regular exercise lowers resting heart rate and teaches your body that a faster heartbeat can be safe.

Common Questions About a Racing Heart

Can anxiety make my heart race even when I don't feel worried?

Yes. Anxiety is not always a conscious feeling of worry. A nervous system that has been under strain for weeks or months can release adrenaline in quiet moments, which is why some people notice a pounding heart while relaxing on the couch or drifting off to sleep.

How fast is too fast for a resting heart rate?

For most adults, a resting heart rate between roughly 60 and 100 beats per minute is considered typical. A resting rate that stays above 100, or episodes that come with chest pain, fainting, or significant shortness of breath, should be assessed by a doctor rather than assumed to be anxiety.

Should I get my heart checked before assuming it is anxiety?

It is a good idea, especially if the symptoms are new, getting worse, or accompanied by other physical changes. A medical check can rule out thyroid problems, anemia, and heart rhythm conditions, and a clear result often makes it easier to work on the anxiety side with confidence.

Why does my heart sometimes pound when I'm lying in bed at night?

Lying still in a quiet room makes you more aware of your heartbeat, and the end of the day is often when stress from earlier catches up with you. Late caffeine, alcohol, or a heavy meal can also play a role. Noticing the pounding and worrying about it can then keep it going.

Can caffeine or energy drinks cause a racing heart that feels like anxiety?

They can. Stimulants trigger many of the same physical changes as the stress response, including a faster heartbeat, jitteriness, and restlessness. For people who are already prone to anxiety, the physical buzz from caffeine can be misread as a warning sign and set off a spiral of worry.

Why does noticing my heartbeat seem to make it beat faster?

Paying close attention to a sensation you are afraid of signals danger to your brain, which releases more adrenaline. That speeds the heart up further, which confirms the fear and adds more adrenaline. Psychologists sometimes describe this as a feedback loop between body sensations and anxious interpretation.

Is a racing heart from anxiety dangerous?

For a heart that has been medically checked and found healthy, a fast heartbeat caused by anxiety is uncomfortable but not harmful. The heart is built to speed up during exercise and stress. That said, any new or unusual heart symptoms deserve a medical opinion before being attributed to anxiety.

Can a panic attack start with a racing heart?

Very often. A sudden pounding heart is one of the most common first signs of a panic attack. If the sensation is interpreted as a heart problem, fear escalates quickly, bringing breathlessness, dizziness, and a sense of losing control within a few minutes.

Could my thyroid be behind a fast heartbeat?

An overactive thyroid can cause a rapid heartbeat along with weight changes, heat intolerance, tremor, and trouble sleeping, and these symptoms overlap with anxiety. A simple blood test ordered by your doctor can check thyroid function.

How does interoceptive exposure help with fear of heart sensations?

Interoceptive exposure is a technique used in cognitive behavioural therapy where a person deliberately brings on mild physical sensations, such as a faster heartbeat from climbing stairs, in a safe and gradual way. Repeated practice teaches the brain that these sensations are uncomfortable but safe, which reduces the fear attached to them.
