Anxiety and Perfectionism: How They Feed Each Other

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Anxiety and perfectionism are closely linked, and each tends to strengthen the other. Perfectionism involves holding extremely high standards and judging yourself harshly when you do not meet them. Anxiety involves persistent worry, often about mistakes, failure, or others' judgment. When perfectionism raises the stakes on every task, anxiety increases. When anxiety makes uncertainty feel dangerous, perfectionism offers a way to feel in control. This cycle can lead to exhaustion, procrastination, and avoidance. Understanding how the two feed each other can help people loosen their grip.

What the Cycle Looks Like

  • Worrying about making mistakes or being judged.
  • Spending far too long on tasks to get them just right.
  • Re-reading emails many times before sending.
  • Procrastinating because starting feels overwhelming.
  • Avoiding new challenges out of fear of failure.
  • Seeking reassurance that work is good enough.
  • Harsh self-criticism after small errors, similar to chronic self-criticism.
  • Difficulty relaxing, even after finishing tasks.
  • Physical tension, trouble sleeping, and fatigue.

How They Feed Each Other

Perfectionism fuels anxiety in several ways. When standards are extremely high, every task feels like a test that could end in failure. Beliefs such as mistakes are unacceptable or I must never let people down create constant pressure. Because perfect outcomes are rarely possible, there is always something to worry about. Feeling like you have to be perfect keeps the threat system switched on.

Anxiety, in turn, drives perfectionism. When uncertainty feels unbearable, checking, over-preparing, and controlling details can feel like ways to prevent bad outcomes. These behaviours reduce anxiety briefly, which reinforces them. Over time, they become habits that are hard to break. Anxiety can also lead to avoidance, such as putting off tasks or not attempting challenges, which can then increase self-criticism and worry.

Many people with this pattern appear highly capable and successful, a presentation sometimes called high-functioning anxiety. The cost is often hidden: exhaustion, difficulty enjoying achievements, and eventually burnout. Perfectionism and anxiety often begin early, shaped by temperament, high expectations, conditional approval, or experiences of criticism.

The cycle can show up in relationships, too. Perfectionists may worry about saying the wrong thing, feel responsible for others' happiness, or struggle to accept help. They may hold partners or children to high standards as well, creating tension. Softening perfectionism often improves relationships as well as personal wellbeing.

Social media and workplace cultures can intensify the cycle by constantly showcasing polished results. Comparing yourself with carefully curated images of others' success can make ordinary effort feel inadequate, raising both standards and anxiety.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as healthy ambition. Healthy striving is flexible and can tolerate mistakes.
  • Not a guarantee of better results. Perfectionism and anxiety often slow progress.
  • Not a character flaw. The cycle is a learned pattern that can change.
  • Not something you must fully eliminate. The goal is flexibility, not lowering all standards.

How It Is Addressed and Treated

Cognitive behavioural therapy is effective for both anxiety and perfectionism. It helps people identify perfectionistic rules and anxious predictions, test them through behavioural experiments, and practise good enough. For example, someone might send an email after one read-through and notice what happens. Reducing reassurance-seeking and checking weakens the cycle. Gradual exposure to uncertainty and imperfection builds tolerance. Self-compassion practices soften harsh self-criticism. Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people act on values rather than fear. Scheduling rest, setting time limits on tasks, and celebrating effort rather than only outcomes also help.

Common Questions About Anxiety and Perfectionism

How are anxiety and perfectionism connected?

Perfectionism raises the stakes on every task, increasing anxiety, while anxiety makes perfectionism feel like a way to stay in control.

Why do high standards leave me constantly on edge?

Rigid standards and fear of mistakes keep the threat system activated.

Why do I re-read emails so many times?

Checking reduces anxiety about mistakes in the moment, but it reinforces perfectionism and worry over time.

Why do I delay tasks until I can do them perfectly?

When a task feels like it must be perfect, starting can feel overwhelming, leading to delay.

Is perfectionism a sign of an anxiety disorder?

Not always, but perfectionism is common in anxiety disorders such as generalized anxiety and social anxiety.

What is a behavioural experiment for perfectionism?

It is a planned test, such as doing a task to a good enough standard, to see whether feared consequences happen.

Do perfectionistic standards spill over onto partners and children?

Often. Perfectionism can bring high expectations of others, worry about disappointing them, and difficulty accepting help.

Can I reduce perfectionism without lowering my standards entirely?

Yes. The goal is flexibility and self-compassion, not giving up on doing good work.

Which approaches break the worry and perfectionism loop?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, exposure to uncertainty, self-compassion work, and acceptance-based approaches help.

Why can I not enjoy my achievements?

Perfectionism often moves the goalposts, so success quickly feels like it was not enough.
