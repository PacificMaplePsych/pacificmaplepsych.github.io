Understanding Seasonal Affective Disorder

Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is a form of depression that follows a seasonal pattern, most commonly beginning in fall and continuing through winter, then lifting in spring. It is described clinically as major depression with a seasonal pattern. SAD is linked with reduced daylight in darker months, which affects the body clock, mood-related brain chemicals, and sleep. It is especially relevant in places with long, dark, and rainy winters, such as coastal British Columbia. SAD is different from the milder "winter blues" many people feel, and it is treatable.

What Seasonal Affective Disorder Looks Like

  • Low mood most of the day, most days, during the fall and winter months.
  • Sleeping much more than usual and still feeling tired.
  • Increased appetite, especially craving carbohydrates, and weight gain.
  • Low energy and difficulty getting going in the morning.
  • Loss of interest in activities and withdrawing from people.
  • Difficulty concentrating.
  • Feelings of hopelessness or worthlessness.

A less common pattern involves summer depression, which may include insomnia, reduced appetite, and agitation. For most people with SAD, symptoms return around the same time each year and lift as days lengthen. At work, productivity may drop in winter. At home, people may pull away from family and social plans.

Why It Happens

Reduced daylight in fall and winter can disrupt the circadian rhythm, the internal clock that regulates sleep and mood. Less light can affect serotonin, a brain chemical linked with mood, and change melatonin production, which influences sleepiness. People who live farther from the equator, have a family history of depression, or have existing mood disorders are at higher risk. SAD often begins in young adulthood and is more common in women.

In rainy climates, overcast days reduce light exposure even further. Spending most daylight hours indoors, as many people do in winter, compounds the effect. This is why light exposure is central to treatment.

Because SAD tends to follow a predictable pattern, planning ahead is one of its advantages. People who know their difficult months can arrange morning light exposure, schedule social plans and activities, protect sleep, and let others know when support might be needed, before the lowest point arrives.

What It Is Not

  • Not just the winter blues. The winter blues are mild and do not significantly affect functioning. SAD is a form of clinical depression.
  • Not holiday stress alone. Holiday-related sadness, explored on the page about feeling sad around the holidays, is linked with events rather than light.
  • Not always seasonal. If depression persists year-round, it may be another form of depression.
  • Not bipolar disorder, though some people with bipolar disorder have seasonal mood patterns. Periods of unusually high energy in spring or summer should be discussed with a doctor.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A physician or psychologist assesses SAD by exploring the timing and pattern of symptoms over several years and ruling out other causes, such as thyroid problems. Treatments include light therapy using a light box, usually each morning, which has good evidence for winter SAD. Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for SAD helps people challenge negative thoughts and plan activities through the darker months. Medication prescribed by a physician may also be used. Getting outside in daylight, regular exercise, and consistent sleep and wake times support mood. Starting strategies before symptoms usually begin can reduce their severity.

Common Questions About Seasonal Affective Disorder

Is seasonal depression a real clinical condition?

Seasonal affective disorder is depression with a seasonal pattern, most often starting in fall and lifting in spring, linked with reduced daylight.

How is SAD different from the winter blues?

The winter blues are mild and do not significantly affect daily life. SAD is a form of clinical depression that affects mood, energy, sleep, and functioning.

Why is SAD common in British Columbia?

Long, dark, and often overcast winters, especially in coastal areas, reduce daylight exposure, which can disrupt mood and sleep.

Does light therapy work for SAD?

Light therapy, using a light box usually each morning, has good evidence for winter SAD. A health professional can advise on how to use it safely.

When should I start light therapy for seasonal depression?

Many people begin in early fall, before symptoms usually start, and continue until spring, following guidance from a health professional.

Can you have seasonal depression in summer?

Yes, though it is less common. Summer SAD may involve insomnia, reduced appetite, and agitation rather than oversleeping.

Why do I crave carbohydrates in winter?

Carbohydrate cravings are a common SAD symptom, possibly linked with changes in serotonin and energy during darker months.

Is CBT effective for seasonal affective disorder?

Yes. CBT adapted for SAD helps people challenge negative thoughts and plan engaging activities through winter, with benefits that may last into future seasons.

Can SAD be a sign of bipolar disorder?

Some people with bipolar disorder have seasonal patterns, with depression in winter and elevated mood in spring or summer. A doctor can help assess this.

What daily habits help with seasonal depression?

Getting outside in daylight, regular exercise, consistent sleep and wake times, social contact, and planning enjoyable activities all help.
