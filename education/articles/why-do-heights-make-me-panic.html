Why Do Heights Make Me Panic

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You step onto a balcony, a glass elevator, or a trail with a steep drop, and your body takes over. Your legs go weak, your heart pounds, and you feel a pull to crouch down, grab a railing, or back away. Some people feel an odd urge to jump, which is frightening in itself. You might avoid ladders, bridges, upper floors, or certain hikes entirely, even when you know logically that you are safe.

Some caution around heights is natural and protective. When the fear is intense, hard to control, and starts shaping where you go, it may be something more specific, and it is very treatable.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Balance or inner ear problems. Vertigo, inner ear conditions, and some medications can cause real dizziness and unsteadiness, which makes heights genuinely harder. New or worsening dizziness is worth checking with a doctor.

Visual height intolerance. Many people feel mildly uneasy or unsteady at heights without a phobia. The brain relies partly on nearby visual reference points to keep balance, and at heights those points are far away.

Panic disorder. If you have panic attacks in many settings, heights may simply be one of several triggers. Physical sensations like dizziness and a racing heart can feel especially alarming when you are up high.

A past fall or frightening experience. A fall, a near miss, or watching someone else get hurt can leave a strong, lasting fear.

Related fears. Fear of heights often overlaps with other fears, such as fear of flying or discomfort in enclosed or crowded spaces.

When It Looks More Like Acrophobia

Acrophobia is a specific phobia of heights, one of the most common types. Like other specific phobias, described in Why Do I Feel Terrified of a Specific Thing Everyone Else Seems Fine With, the fear is out of proportion to the actual danger and is hard to reason away.

Signs of acrophobia include:

  • Intense fear or panic even at moderate heights, such as a second-floor balcony or a step ladder.
  • Avoiding bridges, stairwells with open sides, lookout points, or high-rise buildings.
  • Dread in the days before you expect to face a height.
  • Strong urges to lie down, crawl, or cling to something when up high.
  • Fear that affects work, travel, housing choices, or activities with family and friends.

Many people with a fear of heights experience a sudden, unwanted urge to jump when standing near an edge. This is sometimes called the call of the void. It is a common, fleeting brain reaction that does not mean you want to act on it. If you are having thoughts of ending your life at other times, though, please reach out for support.

Avoidance keeps the fear going. Every time you step back from a height, the relief teaches your brain that the height was dangerous, a pattern explored in Why Do I Avoid Situations That Make Me Nervous.

What Can Help

  • Breathe slowly. Long, slow exhales help settle the physical rush of panic when you are up high.
  • Stay grounded. Focusing on something stable nearby, such as a railing or the floor at your feet, can reduce the unsteady feeling.
  • Take small steps. Gradually spending time at slightly higher places, and staying until anxiety eases, retrains your fear response.
  • Limit safety behaviours. Gripping, crouching, or never looking out can keep the fear strong over time. Reducing them gradually helps.
  • Consider structured therapy. Exposure therapy for acrophobia has strong evidence, and some therapists also use virtual reality to practise safely.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

Common Questions About Fear of Heights

What does the term acrophobia mean for someone afraid of heights?

Acrophobia is a specific phobia of heights. It involves intense fear or panic at heights that is out of proportion to the real danger, often leading to avoidance of balconies, bridges, ladders, and tall buildings.

Why do my legs go weak when I look down from a height?

Weak or shaky legs are part of the body's fear response, which diverts energy and changes muscle tension. At heights, reduced visual reference points can also make balance feel less secure.

Why do I get an urge to jump when I'm up high?

A sudden urge to jump near an edge, sometimes called the call of the void, is common and fleeting. Researchers think it may reflect the brain's quick safety signal being misread. It does not mean you want to act on it.

Is fear of heights normal?

Some unease at heights is very common and protective. It becomes a phobia when the fear is intense, happens at moderate heights, and leads you to avoid places or activities you would otherwise do.

Could my fear of heights be vertigo?

Vertigo is a spinning or moving sensation often caused by inner ear problems, and it can happen anywhere. Fear of heights is anxiety triggered by being up high. If you have dizziness at ground level too, see a doctor.

Can a fear of heights start later in life?

Yes. It can develop after a fall, a frightening experience, a period of high stress, or changes in balance or vision. Fear of heights that appears later in life is just as treatable.

Which treatment works best for acrophobia?

Exposure therapy is the most effective treatment. It involves gradually spending time at increasing heights, with support, until fear decreases. Cognitive techniques and breathing skills are often combined with it.

Does virtual reality therapy work for fear of heights?

Research shows virtual reality exposure can be effective for fear of heights, allowing people to practise facing heights in a controlled setting before moving to real situations.

How can I cope on a bridge or balcony when I feel panic?

Slow your breathing, focus on a stable point nearby, remind yourself the panic will peak and pass, and try to stay rather than rushing away if it is safe to do so.

How can I help my child who is scared of heights?

Fear of heights can begin in childhood. Parents can help by encouraging gradual, supported practice rather than avoidance, and a psychologist can help if the fear is strong or lasting.
