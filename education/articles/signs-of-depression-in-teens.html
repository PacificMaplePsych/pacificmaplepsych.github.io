Signs of Depression in Teens

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If a teen talks about wanting to die or harming themselves, or you are worried about their immediate safety, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Young people can reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Depression in teens often looks different from depression in adults. Instead of obvious sadness, teens may seem irritable, angry, withdrawn, or bored, lose interest in friends and activities, sleep much more or less than usual, struggle at school, or complain of physical symptoms such as headaches. Some moodiness is a normal part of adolescence, but depression lasts most days for two weeks or more and affects daily life. Depression is common in teens and is treatable. Risk factors include family history, stress, bullying, trauma, and chronic illness. Talking openly, taking concerns seriously, and connecting with professional support early can help a teen recover.

Signs of Teen Depression

Mood

  • Irritability, anger, or frequent conflict
  • Sadness, tearfulness, or hopelessness
  • Feeling empty or numb

Behaviour

  • Withdrawing from friends and family
  • Losing interest in activities they used to enjoy
  • Spending more time alone in their room
  • Declining grades or skipping school
  • Increased use of alcohol or drugs

Body

  • Sleeping much more or less, as described on the page about teen sleep
  • Changes in appetite or weight
  • Fatigue and low energy
  • Frequent headaches or stomach aches

Thinking

  • Harsh self-criticism or feeling worthless
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions
  • Talking about death or not wanting to be here

Normal Moodiness or Depression?

Teens often have ups and downs, especially with stress, friendships, or hormones. Signs that it may be depression include:

  • Duration. Low mood or irritability lasting most days for two weeks or more.
  • Intensity. Feelings that seem overwhelming or out of proportion.
  • Impact. Changes affecting school, friendships, or family life.
  • Loss of interest across many areas, not just one activity.

Risk Factors

  • Family history of depression
  • Bullying or social difficulties, as described on the page about bullying and cyberbullying
  • Trauma, loss, or family conflict
  • Chronic illness or pain
  • Anxiety, ADHD, learning difficulties, or autism
  • Being 2SLGBTQ+ in an unsupportive environment
  • Heavy or problematic social media use, as described on the page about problematic technology use

How to Help

  • Talk openly. Ask how they are doing and listen without rushing to fix or criticize.
  • Take it seriously. Avoid dismissing feelings as a phase.
  • Ask directly about suicide if you are worried. Asking does not increase risk, as described on the page about how to support someone who is having suicidal thoughts.
  • Support routines. Sleep, movement, meals, and time outside help.
  • Stay connected. Spend low-pressure time together.
  • Connect with help. A family doctor, school counsellor, or therapist can assess and support your teen.

Talking With a Teen Who Does Not Want to Talk

Many teens find direct conversations about feelings uncomfortable. Talking side by side, such as in the car, on a walk, or while cooking, often feels easier than face-to-face conversations. Short, low-pressure check-ins can be more effective than long talks. If your teen shuts down, let them know you are available whenever they are ready, and keep showing up. Sometimes teens are more willing to talk with another trusted adult, such as a relative, coach, or school counsellor, and that is okay.

How Teen Depression Is Treated

Therapy, such as cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy, is effective for teen depression, often with parent involvement. Medication may be considered for moderate to severe depression, prescribed by a physician who monitors closely. The page about why parenting a teenager is so hard offers support for parents.

Common Questions About Depression in Teens

What does depression look like in a teenager?

Depression in a teenager can look like irritability, withdrawal, loss of interest, sleep and appetite changes, falling grades, and feeling worthless or hopeless.

Why does teen depression often look like anger instead of sadness?

Teen depression often shows up as irritability, anger, or withdrawal because teens may express distress through behaviour more than words.

How long do symptoms need to last to be depression in a teen?

Low mood, irritability, or loss of interest lasting most days for two weeks or more, and affecting daily life, may signal depression in a teen.

What causes depression in teenagers?

Teen depression can be caused by a mix of family history, stress, bullying, trauma, chronic illness, and other mental health conditions.

Should I ask my teen if they are thinking about suicide?

Yes. Asking directly about suicide does not increase risk and can open the door to getting help.

How is depression treated in teens?

Teen depression is treated with therapy such as CBT or interpersonal therapy, and sometimes medication prescribed by a physician.

Can social media cause depression in teens?

Heavy or problematic social media use is linked to depression in some teens, though the relationship is complex.

How can parents help a depressed teen?

Parents can help by talking openly, taking feelings seriously, supporting routines, staying connected, and seeking professional help.

Can teen depression affect grades and school attendance?

Yes. Teen depression often affects concentration, energy, and motivation, which can lead to falling grades and missed school.

Where can a teen get help for depression in BC?

Teens in BC can get help through a family doctor, school counsellor, Foundry centres, Kids Help Phone, or a therapist.
