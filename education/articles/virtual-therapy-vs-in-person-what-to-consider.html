Virtual Therapy vs. In-Person: What to Consider

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Research shows that virtual therapy by secure video can be as effective as in-person therapy for many concerns, including anxiety, depression, and PTSD. Virtual sessions offer convenience, less travel, and easier access from anywhere in the province. In-person sessions can feel more personal for some people, avoid technology problems, provide a private space away from home, and are sometimes preferred for certain assessments or when safety concerns are present. The best choice depends on your concerns, your home environment, your comfort with technology, and your preferences. Many people use a mix, and you can change formats if your needs change.

How Each Works

Virtual therapy. Sessions take place over a secure video platform. You join from a private space using a computer, tablet, or phone. The structure and therapy approaches are generally the same as in person.

In-person therapy. You attend the clinic's office. Being in a dedicated space can help some people focus and leave daily stress behind.

Hybrid. Some people alternate, for example meeting in person at first, then continuing virtually.

Practical Details

Location rules. BC-registered clinicians generally need you to be physically in BC during a virtual session. If you travel or move, ask about this.

Privacy at home. Virtual therapy works best with a private, quiet space. If you share a home, options include headphones, a white noise machine, or a parked car. If you live with someone who is controlling or abusive, in-person sessions or careful safety planning may be needed. See the escape planning page.

Technology. A stable internet connection and a working camera and microphone matter. Test them beforehand.

Assessments. Interviews can often be done virtually, but some standardized tests are best in person, as noted on the page about psychological assessments.

Couples therapy. Couples can attend virtually together, as explained on the page about virtual couples therapy.

Parents of young children. Virtual sessions can be easier to fit around caregiving, as discussed on the page about therapy during parental leave.

Higher levels of need. If you are in frequent crisis or need close monitoring, in-person or more intensive care may be more appropriate, as explained on the page about higher levels of care.

Fees. Fees are often the same for virtual and in-person sessions, but ask the clinic.

How to Decide

  • Choose virtual if you have a private space, value convenience, live far from the clinic, have mobility or health limits, or have a busy schedule.
  • Choose in person if you lack privacy at home, find screens tiring or distracting, prefer a separate space for therapy, or need certain types of testing.
  • Consider your concern. For example, some people with social anxiety start virtually and later practise attending in person.
  • Try it and reassess. If one format is not working, talk with your therapist about switching.

If you are in crisis, do not wait for a session. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Virtual and In-Person Therapy

What does research say about video sessions compared with office visits?

Research shows virtual therapy by secure video can be as effective as in-person therapy for many concerns, including anxiety, depression, and PTSD.

What are the benefits of in-person therapy?

In-person therapy can feel more personal, avoids technology problems, provides privacy away from home, and suits some assessments and situations.

Do I need to be in BC for virtual therapy with a BC clinician?

Generally yes. BC-registered clinicians usually need you to be physically in BC during a virtual therapy session.

What if I do not have privacy at home for virtual sessions?

Try headphones, white noise, or a parked car for virtual sessions, or choose in-person therapy if privacy at home is not possible.

Which parts of testing work over video and which need an office visit?

Interviews can often be done online, but some standardized tests are best completed in person, so assessments may be hybrid.

Is virtual therapy safe if I live with an abusive partner?

Virtual therapy may not be safe if you live with an abusive partner. In-person sessions or careful safety planning may be needed.

Can I switch between virtual and in-person sessions?

Many clinics allow you to switch between virtual and in-person sessions or use a mix, depending on your needs.

Do virtual and in-person sessions cost the same?

Fees for virtual and in-person sessions are often the same, but ask the clinic to confirm.

What technology do I need for video therapy?

For video therapy, you need a stable internet connection, a device with a camera and microphone, and ideally headphones.

Who might prefer in-person over virtual therapy?

People without privacy at home, those who find screens tiring, or those who prefer a separate space may prefer in-person over virtual therapy.
