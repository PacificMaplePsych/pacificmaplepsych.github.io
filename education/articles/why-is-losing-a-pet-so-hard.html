Why Is Losing a Pet So Hard

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If grief has left you having thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Losing a pet is hard because the bond with an animal is a real attachment relationship, built on daily routines, physical closeness, and unconditional companionship. When that relationship ends, the grief can be as intense as grief for a person, and sometimes more complicated, because others may not recognize it as a significant loss. Feeling devastated after a pet dies is a normal response to losing someone you loved, not an overreaction.

People often describe the quiet of the house as the hardest part: no greeting at the door, no morning walk, no weight at the foot of the bed. Many also carry guilt about decisions made at the end of the pet's life.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

The loss of daily structure. Pets shape the rhythm of a day. Walks, feeding times, and play give routine and purpose, and their absence can leave days feeling empty.

Loss of unconditional comfort. A pet offers acceptance without judgment. For people who live alone, or who have found human relationships difficult, that comfort may have been a central source of support.

Guilt about euthanasia. Many people agonize over whether they chose the right time, or whether they missed signs of illness. This guilt is extremely common and usually reflects how much they cared.

Grief that others minimize. Comments such as "it was just a dog" or pressure to get a new pet quickly can make people feel their grief is not allowed, which makes it harder to process.

A pet linked to a life chapter. A pet who was with you through a divorce, a move, or the death of a family member can hold many memories. Losing them can reopen older grief that lingers for years.

Sudden or traumatic loss. An accident or unexpected death can bring shock and intrusive images on top of grief.

When It Looks More Like Complicated Grief

Grief for a pet usually softens over weeks and months, even though sadness can return at anniversaries or unexpected moments. Like other grief, it often comes in waves that hit out of nowhere. Sometimes, though, grief becomes stuck. Signs that it may need more attention include:

  • Intense longing or distress that is not easing after many months.
  • Being unable to accept that the pet has died.
  • Persistent guilt or self-blame that dominates your thoughts.
  • Avoiding anything that reminds you of the pet, or being unable to engage with anything else.
  • Withdrawal from people and activities, and a sense that life has lost meaning.
  • Symptoms of depression, such as low mood most days, sleep changes, or hopelessness.

Grief can become more complicated when a pet was a primary source of emotional support, when the loss was traumatic, or when grief is disenfranchised, meaning it is not socially acknowledged. Some people also feel numb after the loss rather than sad, and worry that this means they did not care. Numbness is a common early response and usually shifts with time.

What Can Help

  • Let the grief be real. Give yourself permission to mourn fully, without comparing your loss to anyone else's.
  • Mark the loss. A small ceremony, planting something, a photo album, or writing a letter to your pet can help you say goodbye.
  • Talk to people who understand. Friends who have loved animals, pet loss support groups, and veterinary grief resources can offer validation.
  • Rebuild routine gently. Replace walking or feeding times with something that still gets you outside or moving.
  • Look at guilt with compassion. Decisions at the end of a pet's life are made with the information available at the time and usually out of love.
  • Take your time with a new pet. There is no right timeline. Some people are ready soon, others need longer.

Common Questions About Grieving a Pet

Is it normal to grieve a pet as much as a person?

Yes. The bond with a pet is a genuine attachment, and many people grieve a pet as deeply as a human loved one. The intensity reflects the relationship, not a lack of perspective.

When does the pain of losing a pet start to ease?

There is no set timeline. The most intense pain often eases over weeks to months, though waves of sadness can return for much longer, especially around anniversaries or reminders.

Why do I feel so guilty about putting my pet down?

Guilt after euthanasia is very common. People question the timing or wonder whether they missed something. This usually reflects deep care. Decisions are made with the best information available at the time.

What is disenfranchised grief after losing a pet?

Disenfranchised grief is grief that others do not fully recognize. When people minimize pet loss, it can leave grievers feeling isolated and less able to mourn openly.

Why is the house so hard to be in after my pet died?

Pets fill the home with sounds, routines, and presence. Their absence is felt in every familiar space, which can make home feel painfully quiet for a while.

Should I get a new pet right away to help with the grief?

Some people find a new pet helps, while others need more time. A new animal cannot replace the one you lost, so it helps to wait until you feel ready rather than using it to avoid grief.

How do I explain the death of a family pet to children?

Use simple, honest language, avoid phrases like "put to sleep" that can confuse young children, and let them ask questions and express feelings. Including them in a small goodbye can help.

Can losing a pet trigger depression?

It can, particularly if the pet was a main source of companionship or the loss came during an already difficult time. If low mood persists most days for weeks, consider speaking with a professional.

Are there support groups for pet loss in Canada?

Yes. Many veterinary colleges, animal hospitals, and community organizations offer pet loss support lines or groups. Your veterinarian can often point you toward local options.

When is grief counselling worth it after a pet dies?

Consider it if grief is not easing after several months, guilt feels overwhelming, you are withdrawing from life, or the loss has brought up other past losses or difficult memories.
