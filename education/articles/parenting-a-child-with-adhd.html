Parenting a Child With ADHD

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Parenting a child with ADHD tends to go best with clear routines, short and specific instructions, frequent positive feedback, consistent and calm consequences, and plenty of patience for both your child and yourself. Children with ADHD often struggle with attention, impulse control, organization, and managing big emotions, which can lead to daily friction around homework, mornings, and bedtime. They are also often creative, energetic, curious, and full of ideas. ADHD is a difference in how the brain develops, not the result of bad parenting. With the right strategies and support, family life can become calmer and children can build confidence.

Why ADHD-Friendly Parenting Helps

ADHD affects executive functions, the skills that help children plan, start tasks, follow multi-step instructions, manage time, and control impulses. These skills develop more slowly in children with ADHD, so they may need more support than peers of the same age. The ways ADHD can show up are explained on the page about the three presentations of ADHD. Many children with ADHD also have intense emotional reactions, as described on the page about ADHD and emotional dysregulation.

Children with ADHD often receive more criticism than praise, which can harm self-esteem. Parenting strategies that add structure, break tasks down, and focus on positive feedback help children succeed more often, reduce conflict, and strengthen the parent-child relationship.

Strategies to Try

1. Build Predictable Routines

Consistent morning, after-school, and bedtime routines reduce arguments. Visual schedules with pictures or checklists help children follow steps.

2. Give Short, Clear Instructions

Get your child's attention first, make eye contact if comfortable, and give one or two steps at a time. Ask them to repeat back what you said.

3. Praise Specific Efforts

Notice and name what goes well, such as "You started your homework right away. Great job." Aim for much more praise than correction.

4. Use Calm, Consistent Consequences

Keep consequences brief, predictable, and connected to the behaviour. Avoid long lectures.

5. Make Tasks Smaller

Break chores and homework into short chunks with breaks. Timers can help.

6. Plan for Transitions

Give warnings before changes, such as "Ten minutes until we leave."

7. Support Big Feelings

Help your child name emotions, stay calm yourself, and teach simple calming skills such as slow breathing. The page about staying calm as a parent offers ideas for you too.

8. Work With the School

Partner with teachers on strategies, such as seating, check-ins, or written instructions. In BC, schools can develop learning plans and supports for students with diverse needs.

9. Make Room for Strengths

Encourage activities where your child shines, such as sports, art, building, or music.

10. Look After Yourself

Parenting a child with ADHD can be tiring. Rest, support, and time for yourself help you stay patient.

Common Obstacles

Consistency is hard when parents are tired, busy, or disagree about approaches. Agreeing on a few key rules and routines helps. Some parents feel judged by others who do not understand ADHD. Remember that ADHD is a brain-based difference. Parents with ADHD themselves may find routines especially challenging, and support for both parent and child can help. Strategies may need adjusting as children grow, especially into the teen years. Practical support for parents is also described on the page about parent coaching.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If your child's difficulties are affecting school, friendships, or family life significantly, or if you notice anxiety, low mood, or low self-esteem, professional support can help. An assessment can clarify whether ADHD or other conditions are present, as described on the page about signs your child may need support. Behavioural parent training has strong evidence for children with ADHD. A physician can discuss whether medication may help. If you feel overwhelmed or burnt out as a parent, support for you matters too.

Common Questions About Parenting a Child With ADHD

What parenting strategies work best for children with ADHD?

Clear routines, short instructions, frequent specific praise, calm consistent consequences, and breaking tasks down work well for children with ADHD.

Is my child's ADHD caused by my parenting?

No. ADHD is a brain-based difference in development, not the result of bad parenting, though parenting strategies can help.

How can I give instructions my child with ADHD will follow?

Get your child's attention first, give one or two steps at a time, and ask them to repeat back what you said, since ADHD affects working memory.

How much praise does a child with ADHD need?

Children with ADHD often receive more criticism than praise, so aim for much more specific praise than correction.

How can I make homework easier for my child with ADHD?

Break ADHD homework into short chunks with breaks, use timers, and keep a consistent time and place for it.

Why does my child with ADHD have such big emotional reactions?

Many children with ADHD struggle with emotional regulation, so feelings can rise quickly and intensely.

How can I work with my child's school on ADHD?

Partner with teachers on ADHD strategies such as seating, check-ins, and written instructions, and ask about learning plans and supports.

What is behavioural parent training for ADHD?

Behavioural parent training teaches parents structured strategies for routines, praise, and consequences, and has strong evidence for children with ADHD.

What if I have ADHD and so does my child?

Parents with ADHD may find routines especially hard, and support for both parent and child can help the whole family.

What signs mean my child with ADHD needs more than home strategies?

If ADHD difficulties significantly affect school, friendships, or family life, or your child seems anxious or low, professional support can help.
