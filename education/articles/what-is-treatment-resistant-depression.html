What Is Treatment-Resistant Depression

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Living with depression that has not lifted can feel exhausting and hopeless. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Treatment-resistant depression is a term used when depression has not improved enough after at least two adequate trials of antidepressant treatment, meaning medications taken at a suitable dose for a sufficient length of time. Some definitions also consider psychotherapy. The term can sound discouraging, but it does not mean depression is untreatable. It means the first approaches have not worked well enough, and a more careful review and different strategies are needed. Many people with treatment-resistant depression go on to improve with further options.

What Treatment-Resistant Depression Looks Like

  • Ongoing symptoms of major depression despite treatment.
  • Partial improvement, where some symptoms ease but others remain.
  • Repeated relapses after initial improvement.
  • Side effects that have made it hard to continue medications.
  • Feeling hopeless about getting better after several attempts.
  • Low energy, loss of pleasure, poor sleep, or difficulty functioning continuing for months or years.

Many people feel discouraged, frustrated, or ashamed after trying several treatments. These feelings are understandable, and they are not a sign that recovery is impossible.

Why It Happens

There are many reasons depression may not respond to initial treatment. Sometimes the diagnosis needs revisiting. For example, bipolar spectrum conditions can look like depression but need different treatment, as explained on the pages about bipolar disorder and depression and hypomania. Medical conditions, such as thyroid problems, anemia, sleep apnea, or chronic pain, can sustain depressive symptoms. Some medications and substance use, including alcohol, can interfere with recovery.

Treatment factors matter too. A medication may not have been taken long enough, at an effective dose, or consistently, sometimes because of side effects. Psychotherapy may not have been tried, or the approach may not have fit. Ongoing stressors, such as trauma, relationship conflict, financial strain, or isolation, can keep depression in place. Co-occurring anxiety, ADHD, or personality patterns may also need attention. Long-standing low mood, as in persistent depressive disorder, can be particularly stubborn.

Biological differences also play a role. People vary in how they metabolize medications and in which brain systems are most involved in their depression. This is part of why one person responds to a treatment that does not help another, and why finding the right approach can take time.

What It Is Not

  • Not untreatable depression. Many people improve with a revised plan.
  • Not your fault. Response to treatment varies widely between people.
  • Not a sign you have not tried hard enough. Depression is a health condition, not a test of effort.
  • Not the end of options. There are many further treatments to consider.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A thorough review is the first step: confirming the diagnosis, checking medical contributors, reviewing past treatments, and identifying stressors and co-occurring conditions. A psychiatrist may recommend adjusting doses, switching medications, combining medications, or adding augmenting agents. Psychotherapy is especially important. Cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation, interpersonal therapy, and approaches designed for chronic depression can help. Other options a psychiatrist may discuss include repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, electroconvulsive therapy, which is effective for severe depression, and newer treatments such as esketamine in specialized settings. Addressing sleep, activity, alcohol use, and social connection supports any treatment plan.

Many people find that improvement comes gradually rather than all at once. Tracking mood and daily functioning over time can help you and your care team notice progress that might otherwise be missed.

Common Questions About Treatment-Resistant Depression

What does treatment-resistant depression mean?

It usually means depression has not improved enough after at least two adequate trials of antidepressant treatment.

Does treatment-resistant depression mean I will never get better?

No. Many people improve with a revised diagnosis, different medications, psychotherapy, or other treatments.

Why have antidepressants not worked for me?

Reasons include dose or duration, side effects, a missed diagnosis such as bipolar disorder, medical conditions, or ongoing stressors.

Can a medical condition keep depression from improving?

Yes. Thyroid problems, anemia, sleep apnea, chronic pain, and other conditions can sustain depressive symptoms.

Is psychotherapy helpful for treatment-resistant depression?

Yes. Therapies such as CBT, behavioural activation, and approaches designed for chronic depression can help, often alongside medication.

What is transcranial magnetic stimulation?

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation uses magnetic pulses to stimulate brain areas involved in mood and is used for depression that has not responded to medication.

Is electroconvulsive therapy still used?

Yes. Modern electroconvulsive therapy is done under anesthesia and is highly effective for severe or treatment-resistant depression.

Could my depression actually be bipolar disorder?

Sometimes. A history of hypomania or mania changes treatment, so clinicians often review this when depression does not respond.

How does alcohol affect depression treatment?

Alcohol can worsen mood, disrupt sleep, and reduce the effectiveness of treatment, making recovery harder.

How can I stay hopeful when treatments have not worked?

Remember that many people improve after several attempts, track small gains, lean on support, and keep working with your care team.
