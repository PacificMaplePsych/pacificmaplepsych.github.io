Why Do I Feel Like a Different Person Depending on Who I'm With

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Everyone adjusts somewhat depending on who they are with, speaking differently to a boss than to a friend, for example. Feeling like a completely different person with different people goes further. It usually means your sense of who you are depends heavily on the people around you, so your opinions, style, and even values seem to shift. This can come from people-pleasing, masking, a history of needing to adapt to stay safe, or an unstable sense of identity, which is one of the features of borderline personality disorder. Understanding the pattern can help you feel more consistently like yourself.

People describe agreeing with whoever they are talking to, taking on the interests of each new partner, feeling unsure what they actually think when alone, or feeling exhausted and fake after moving between groups.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Normal social flexibility. Adapting tone and topics to different settings is healthy. It becomes a concern when your core preferences and values seem to disappear.

People-pleasing. If approval feels essential, you may shape yourself around what each person wants. People who need other people's approval often notice this.

Masking. Many autistic and ADHD adults learn to copy others to fit in. The page on feeling like you are performing in social situations explores this.

Growing up needing to adapt. Children who had to read a parent's moods to stay safe often become skilled at becoming whoever others need, sometimes at the cost of knowing themselves.

Social anxiety. Fear of judgment can lead people to hide their real opinions and blend in.

Cultural code-switching. People moving between cultures often shift language, behaviour, and expectations. This is a skill, though it can feel tiring or confusing.

When It Looks More Like a Borderline Personality Pattern

In borderline personality disorder (BPD), identity disturbance, a markedly unstable sense of self, is a core feature. It goes beyond adapting to others. Signs this may be part of a wider pattern include:

  • Frequent, dramatic shifts in goals, values, career plans, or sense of who you are.
  • Taking on the identity of a new partner or friend, then feeling lost when the relationship ends.
  • Feeling empty without a relationship or goal to organize yourself around.
  • Intense fear of abandonment, explored on the page about being afraid of people leaving.
  • Emotions that feel intense and change quickly.

An unstable sense of self often develops in environments where a child's own feelings and preferences were not noticed or validated, so they learned to look outward for cues about who to be. It is not a character flaw. Many people who shift around others do not have BPD, and only a clinician can assess this. Whatever the cause, a steadier sense of self can be built.

What Can Help

  • Notice your own reactions. Before responding to someone, ask yourself what you actually think or feel.
  • Spend time alone deliberately. Doing activities by yourself helps you learn your own preferences without outside influence.
  • Clarify your values. Write down what matters to you. Values offer a stable core even when behaviour adapts.
  • Practise small disagreements. Sharing a different opinion on low-stakes topics builds confidence in your own voice.
  • Keep a journal. Tracking your thoughts over time can reveal consistent threads in who you are.
  • Choose safe relationships. People who welcome your real self make it easier to stop shape-shifting.

Common Questions About Feeling Like a Different Person Around Others

Why do my opinions shift to match whoever I am talking to?

It can come from people-pleasing, masking, social anxiety, or having learned early to adapt to others to stay safe. Some adapting is normal, but losing your own views suggests a deeper pattern.

Is it normal to act differently around different people?

Yes, to a degree. Most people adjust their tone and topics to the setting. It becomes a concern when your values, preferences, and sense of self shift so much that you feel lost.

What is identity disturbance?

Identity disturbance refers to a markedly unstable sense of self, with frequent shifts in values, goals, or self-image. It is one of the features that clinicians consider when assessing borderline personality disorder.

Is being a chameleon around people a sign of BPD?

Not on its own. Many people adapt to others without having borderline personality disorder. BPD involves a broader pattern that includes emotional intensity and relationship difficulties.

Why do I take on my partner's interests and opinions?

When your sense of self depends on connection, adopting a partner's interests can feel like a way to stay close. Over time it can leave you unsure of your own preferences.

Can childhood experiences make it hard to know who I am?

Yes. If your feelings and preferences were overlooked or you had to focus on others' moods, you may have learned to look outward for cues rather than inward.

How is masking different from having an unstable sense of self?

Masking involves hiding natural traits to fit in, while knowing who you are underneath. An unstable sense of self involves genuine uncertainty about who you are. The two can overlap.

Why do I feel fake after spending time with certain groups?

Feeling fake often means you were presenting a version of yourself that does not match your inner experience. It can come from masking, people-pleasing, or social anxiety.

How can I build a stronger sense of self?

Spend time alone, notice your own reactions, clarify your values, practise sharing opinions, and invest in relationships where you can be yourself. Therapy can support this process.

What does therapy focus on when someone does not know who they are?

Therapy explores values, emotions, and relationship patterns, helps people notice their own preferences, and supports steadier self-understanding. Approaches such as DBT and schema-focused work are often used.
