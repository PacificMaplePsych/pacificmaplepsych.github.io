What a Bipolar Disorder Assessment Involves

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you or someone you care about is having thoughts of suicide, is not sleeping for days with racing energy and risky behaviour, or seems out of touch with reality, seek help right away. Call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline), call 911, or go to the nearest emergency department.

A bipolar disorder assessment is a detailed evaluation of your mood history to determine whether you have experienced episodes of mania or hypomania, as well as depression, and whether bipolar disorder or another condition best explains your symptoms. It usually involves an in-depth interview about past and current mood changes, sleep, energy, behaviour, family history, substance use, and medical health, often supported by questionnaires and, with your consent, input from someone who knows you well. Because depression is often what brings people in, and periods of elevated mood can be missed or seen as positive, a careful history is the heart of the process. Diagnosis is often made by a psychiatrist or physician, and psychologists also assess and diagnose bipolar disorder.

What to Expect

  1. Current concerns. The clinician asks what is happening now, including mood, sleep, energy, concentration, and safety.
  2. Mood history. You will be asked about past periods of low mood and about times of unusually high, irritable, or energized mood, such as needing much less sleep, racing thoughts, talking faster, taking on many projects, or spending or acting impulsively. The page about hypomania describes these signs.
  3. Timing and pattern. How long episodes lasted, how often they occur, and whether they are linked to seasons, stress, sleep loss, childbirth, or substances.
  4. Family history. Bipolar disorder often runs in families, so questions about relatives' mental health are important.
  5. Other explanations. The clinician considers depression, ADHD, borderline personality disorder, anxiety, trauma, thyroid conditions, medications, and substance use, which can all affect mood.
  6. Questionnaires and collateral input. Mood screening tools and, with your permission, input from a partner or family member who has seen your episodes.

Practical Details

Who diagnoses. Psychiatrists, family doctors, and psychologists can all be involved. Medication decisions are made by a physician or nurse practitioner, often a psychiatrist. A family doctor can refer you to psychiatry, which is usually covered by MSP.

Medical checks. A doctor may order blood tests, such as for thyroid function, to rule out physical causes.

More than one appointment. A full picture can take time, and some people are asked to track mood and sleep before a diagnosis is confirmed, as explained on the page about mood tracking for bipolar disorder.

Types of diagnosis. Possible outcomes include bipolar I, bipolar II, cyclothymia, another condition, or a decision to monitor over time.

Private assessments. Private psychological assessments are not covered by MSP. Ask the clinic about fees and what the assessment includes.

How to Prepare and What Happens Next

  • Write a mood timeline. Note major periods of low or high mood, how long they lasted, and what happened.
  • Track mood and sleep. A few weeks of daily ratings can be very useful.
  • Ask someone close to you. They may remember changes you did not notice.
  • Gather family history. Including any relatives with bipolar disorder, depression, or substance use.
  • List medications and substances. Including antidepressants, which can sometimes affect mood in people with bipolar disorder.

If bipolar disorder is diagnosed, treatment usually combines medication with therapy and lifestyle supports, as described on the page about treatment options for bipolar disorder.

Common Questions About Bipolar Disorder Assessments

What happens during a bipolar disorder assessment?

A bipolar disorder assessment involves a detailed interview about mood episodes, sleep, energy, behaviour, family history, and health, often with questionnaires and input from someone close to you.

Which BC professionals take part in a bipolar assessment?

Psychiatrists, family doctors, and psychologists can assess and diagnose bipolar disorder in BC, while medication is prescribed by a physician or nurse practitioner.

Why do highs often go unreported in a bipolar assessment?

Bipolar disorder is sometimes missed because people seek help during depression, while periods of hypomania may feel positive or go unnoticed.

Why does the clinician ask about my family in a bipolar assessment?

Bipolar disorder often runs in families, so knowing whether relatives have had mood disorders helps the clinician assess the likelihood.

Can a partner or family member help with a bipolar assessment?

Yes. With your consent, someone close to you can describe mood changes they noticed, which helps the clinician assess for mania or hypomania.

Do I need blood tests for a bipolar disorder assessment?

A doctor may order blood tests, such as thyroid function, during a bipolar disorder assessment to rule out physical causes of mood changes.

How should I prepare for a bipolar disorder assessment?

Before a bipolar disorder assessment, write a mood timeline, track mood and sleep, gather family history, and list medications and substances.

Can ADHD or BPD be mistaken for bipolar disorder?

Yes. ADHD, borderline personality disorder, and some other conditions can resemble bipolar disorder, so clinicians consider them carefully.

Can bipolar disorder be diagnosed in one appointment?

Sometimes, but a bipolar disorder diagnosis often takes more than one appointment and may involve tracking mood over time.

What happens after a bipolar disorder diagnosis?

After a bipolar disorder diagnosis, treatment usually combines medication prescribed by a physician with therapy, mood tracking, and lifestyle supports.
