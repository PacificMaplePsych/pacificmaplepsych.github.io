What Happens After You Receive an ADHD Diagnosis

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

After an ADHD diagnosis, most people receive a written report and a feedback session that explains the findings and recommends next steps. These often include talking with a family doctor or psychiatrist about medication, learning about ADHD, building practical strategies and routines, considering therapy or coaching, and requesting accommodations at school or work if helpful. Many adults also go through an emotional adjustment as they look back on past struggles with new understanding. A diagnosis does not define you, but it can help explain long-standing patterns and point toward supports that make daily life easier.

What to Expect

The report and feedback. The clinician explains the diagnosis, the presentation type, any co-occurring conditions, and recommendations. You receive a written report to keep and share as you choose.

Mixed emotions. Relief, validation, grief for missed opportunities, or frustration about not being diagnosed earlier are all common, as discussed on the page about late-diagnosed ADHD in adulthood.

Learning about ADHD. Understanding how ADHD affects attention, executive functioning, emotions, and motivation can reduce self-blame.

Practical Details

Medication. Psychologists do not prescribe. If medication is recommended or you want to consider it, a family doctor, nurse practitioner, or psychiatrist can discuss options, as described on the page about ADHD medication. Bringing your report helps.

Therapy and coaching. CBT adapted for ADHD can help with planning, procrastination, and self-esteem. Coaching can help with structure and accountability. The page about how ADHD is treated in adults explains the options.

Accommodations. Your report can support requests for accommodations at work or in post-secondary education, as explained on the page about workplace accommodations for ADHD.

Disclosure. You decide who to tell. Some people share with close family first and consider workplace disclosure later.

Co-occurring conditions. Anxiety, depression, sleep problems, learning differences, and autism often occur alongside ADHD. Recommendations may include further assessment or treatment.

Children and teens. For young people, the report can help schools put supports in place, and parents can learn strategies to support routines and emotions at home.

What Happens Next

  • Pick a starting point. Choose one or two recommendations rather than tackling everything at once.
  • Book a medical appointment if needed. To discuss medication and any health questions.
  • Try practical strategies. External reminders, visual schedules, and simple systems often help. The page about building routines with ADHD offers ideas.
  • Consider therapy. Especially if low self-esteem, anxiety, or relationship strain are part of the picture.
  • Be patient with yourself. Finding the right combination of supports takes time.

If you are struggling with hopelessness or thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Life After an ADHD Diagnosis

What are the first steps once ADHD has been confirmed?

After an ADHD diagnosis, you usually receive a report and feedback session with recommendations, such as medication discussion, therapy, strategies, and accommodations.

Who prescribes ADHD medication after a psychologist's diagnosis?

A family doctor, nurse practitioner, or psychiatrist prescribes ADHD medication. Bringing your psychologist's report to the appointment helps.

Why do some adults feel loss when they learn they have ADHD?

Many adults feel grief for missed opportunities and past struggles, along with relief and validation. This is a common reaction to an adult ADHD diagnosis.

How can an ADHD report support requests for workplace or school adjustments?

An ADHD report documents your needs and recommendations, which can support requests for accommodations at work or in post-secondary education.

Do I have to tell my workplace about my ADHD diagnosis?

No. Sharing an ADHD diagnosis at work is your choice, and many people tell close family first and decide about work later.

Does therapy help after being diagnosed with ADHD?

Yes. Therapy, such as CBT adapted for ADHD, can help with planning, procrastination, self-esteem, and emotional regulation after diagnosis.

What other conditions are common alongside ADHD?

Anxiety, depression, sleep problems, learning differences, and autism often occur alongside ADHD, so further assessment may be recommended.

Where should I start after an ADHD diagnosis?

Choose one or two ADHD recommendations to start with, such as booking a medical appointment or trying a simple routine, rather than everything at once.

How does an ADHD diagnosis help a child at school?

An ADHD diagnosis and report can help schools put supports in place for a child, such as adjustments to instruction, seating, or testing.

Will an ADHD label change how I see myself?

It may, often in a helpful way. An ADHD diagnosis does not define you, but it can explain long-standing patterns and point toward supports that make life easier.
