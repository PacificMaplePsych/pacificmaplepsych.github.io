How to Help a Family Member Who Struggles With Hoarding

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Helping a family member who struggles with hoarding usually works best when you focus on safety and your relationship rather than on getting rid of things, listen without judgment, avoid clearing out their belongings without consent, and gently encourage specialized help. Hoarding disorder involves persistent difficulty discarding possessions, strong urges to save items, and clutter that makes living spaces hard to use. For family members, it can bring worry, embarrassment, conflict, and grief. Many families try to solve the problem by cleaning up, only to see clutter return and relationships suffer. A slower, more respectful approach tends to lead to better outcomes for everyone.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

For someone with hoarding disorder, possessions often carry deep meaning. An item may feel connected to memories or a person, useful in the future, or too wasteful to throw away. Deciding what to keep can feel overwhelming, and discarding can bring intense anxiety, sadness, or a sense of loss. Many people who hoard feel ashamed and may avoid having visitors. Some do not see the clutter as a problem, while others feel stuck and hopeless.

Hoarding often occurs alongside depression, anxiety, ADHD, trauma, or grief, and it can worsen after a loss or major life change. Understanding that hoarding is a mental health condition, not laziness or stubbornness, can shift how families respond.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Lead with care. Express concern for their wellbeing and safety, not just the state of the home.
  • Listen and ask questions. Ask what their items mean to them and how they feel about their home.
  • Focus on safety first. Clear exits, working smoke detectors, safe cooking areas, and pathways are priorities that are easier to agree on.
  • Offer specific help with consent. Offer to help sort one small area at their pace, letting them make decisions.
  • Notice progress. Acknowledge small changes, such as clearing a chair or bringing fewer items home.
  • Learn about hoarding. Understanding the condition reduces frustration and blame.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Forced clean-outs. Removing items without consent often causes severe distress, damages trust, and rarely leads to lasting change.
  • Arguing about specific items. Debating whether something is worth keeping usually increases defensiveness.
  • Shaming or criticizing. This deepens shame and can lead to more secrecy.
  • Taking over decisions. Making choices for them can undermine their sense of control and motivation.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting a family member who hoards can be exhausting and emotionally painful. You may feel angry, helpless, embarrassed, or grieve the home or relationship you once had. Adult children of parents who hoard often carry complicated feelings from growing up in cluttered homes. Setting boundaries, such as how often you help or what you are willing to do, protects your wellbeing. Seeking support for yourself, through counselling or family support groups, can help. Over time, long-term support can lead to strain described on the page about caregiver burnout. Boundaries themselves are explored on the page about boundary issues.

Encouraging Professional Help

Hoarding responds best to specialized treatment, usually CBT designed for hoarding, as explained on the page about treatment for hoarding disorder. Raise the idea gently, focusing on their wellbeing, and offer to help find a therapist or attend a first appointment. If they are not ready, keep the relationship open and revisit the topic later. Motivational approaches, which explore their own reasons for change, often work better than pressure.

If there are serious safety risks, such as fire hazards, blocked exits, unsanitary conditions, or risks to children, older adults, or animals, you may need to involve local services, such as the fire department or health authority. Try to do this in a way that preserves dignity and involves the person as much as possible. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. Low mood and hopelessness can also accompany hoarding. If your family member talks about suicide, call or text 9-8-8 for guidance.

Common Questions About Helping a Relative With Hoarding

How can I help a parent who hoards without damaging our relationship?

Lead with care, focus on safety rather than getting rid of things, listen without judgment, and offer help with sorting only with their consent.

Should I clean out a family member's hoarded home for them?

Forced clean-outs without consent usually cause severe distress and rarely lead to lasting change for someone who hoards.

Why does my relative with hoarding disorder get so upset when anything is thrown away?

For people who hoard, possessions often feel tied to memories or future needs, so discarding can bring intense anxiety or loss.

What should I focus on first when a relative's hoarding is unsafe?

Focus first on safety in the hoarded home, such as clear exits, working smoke detectors, safe cooking areas, and pathways.

How do I talk to my family member about getting help for hoarding?

Raise the topic gently, focus on their wellbeing, offer practical help finding a therapist, and revisit it later if they are not ready.

Is arguing about individual items helpful when someone hoards?

Usually not. Arguing about specific hoarded items tends to increase defensiveness and conflict.

How can I protect my own wellbeing when a family member hoards?

Set boundaries on how much you help, seek counselling or family support, and acknowledge your own feelings about the hoarding.

When should I involve outside services for a relative's hoarding?

If hoarding creates fire hazards, blocked exits, unsanitary conditions, or risks to children, older adults, or animals, local services may need to be involved.

Can hoarding get worse after a loss?

Yes. Hoarding often worsens after a loss or major life change, when possessions may feel even more meaningful.

What if my relative does not think their hoarding is a problem?

Many people who hoard do not see the clutter as a problem. Keep the relationship open, focus on safety, and revisit the conversation over time.
