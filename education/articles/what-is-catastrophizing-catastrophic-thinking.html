What Is Catastrophizing (Catastrophic Thinking)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Catastrophizing is a thinking pattern in which the mind jumps to the worst possible outcome and treats it as likely, or imagines that a difficult outcome would be unbearable. A headache becomes a brain tumour. A delayed reply means a relationship is ending. A mistake at work means being fired. Catastrophic thinking is one of the most common cognitive distortions described in cognitive behavioural therapy. Everyone catastrophizes at times, especially when stressed or tired. It becomes a problem when it happens often, fuels anxiety, and shapes decisions and daily life.

What Catastrophizing Looks Like

  • Chains of what-if thoughts that spiral from small events to disaster.
  • Assuming the worst about ambiguous situations, such as a vague email or a new symptom.
  • Believing you would not be able to cope if something bad happened.
  • Physical anxiety, such as a racing heart or tight chest, as thoughts escalate.
  • Searching for information or reassurance to rule out the worst.
  • Avoiding situations because of imagined disasters.
  • A sense that something bad is about to happen.

Catastrophizing often feels like being prepared or realistic. People may believe that imagining the worst protects them from being caught off guard. In practice, it tends to increase anxiety without making outcomes better.

Why It Happens

The brain is wired to notice threats. From an evolutionary perspective, assuming danger was safer than missing it. When stress or anxiety is high, the brain's threat system becomes more active, and it tends to fill uncertainty with worst-case predictions. Past experiences of sudden loss, trauma, or unpredictability can teach the mind to expect disaster. Temperament, perfectionism, and difficulty tolerating uncertainty also increase the tendency.

Catastrophizing is closely linked with anxiety disorders, health anxiety, panic, depression, and chronic pain. In chronic pain, catastrophic thoughts about pain are associated with greater pain intensity and disability. It often travels with other patterns, such as overthinking and all-or-nothing thinking, described on the page about black-and-white thinking.

Catastrophizing also affects relationships and work. A partner may feel frustrated when reassurance never seems to be enough, or a colleague may not understand why a small change caused so much distress. Recognizing that catastrophic thoughts come from the brain's alarm system, rather than from a lack of trust or effort, can help both the person and those around them respond with more patience.

Sleep and fatigue make catastrophizing worse. Many people notice that worries which feel manageable at noon feel overwhelming at two in the morning. Knowing this can help you treat late-night predictions with extra scepticism and postpone decisions until you are rested.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as realistic planning. Planning for real risks is helpful. Catastrophizing inflates the likelihood and severity of harm.
  • Not a character flaw. It is a learned and common thinking habit.
  • Not something you can stop by being told to calm down. Skills and practice help more than reassurance.
  • Not always a diagnosis, though frequent catastrophizing often accompanies anxiety or depression that can be treated.

How It Is Addressed and Treated

Cognitive behavioural therapy offers practical tools. People learn to notice catastrophic thoughts, name them, and examine the evidence. Useful questions include: what is the most likely outcome, what is the best outcome, and how would I cope if the worst did happen? Decatastrophizing involves thinking the scenario through to its end and recognizing that coping is usually possible. Scheduled worry time can contain spirals. Mindfulness and acceptance-based approaches help people observe thoughts without treating them as facts. Reducing reassurance-seeking and gradually facing avoided situations builds confidence. Treating underlying anxiety or depression often reduces catastrophizing.

Common Questions About Catastrophizing

What does catastrophizing mean?

Catastrophizing is jumping to the worst possible outcome and treating it as likely, or believing a difficult outcome would be unbearable.

Is catastrophizing a symptom of anxiety?

It is common in anxiety disorders, health anxiety, and panic, and can also appear with depression and chronic pain.

Why does my brain always jump to the worst-case scenario?

The brain's threat system is wired to spot danger, and stress, past experiences, and difficulty with uncertainty can strengthen this tendency.

How is catastrophizing linked with chronic pain?

Catastrophic thoughts about pain are associated with higher pain intensity and disability, and addressing them can improve coping.

How does thinking the worst case through to the end help?

This CBT technique, called decatastrophizing, involves thinking a feared scenario through, estimating realistic outcomes, and recognizing how you would cope.

Does imagining the worst help me prepare?

It can feel protective, but catastrophizing usually increases anxiety without improving outcomes. Realistic planning is more helpful.

What questions can I ask myself when I catastrophize?

Ask what the most likely outcome is, what the evidence says, and how you would cope if the worst did happen.

What helps when a partner always expects disaster?

Listen calmly, avoid endless reassurance, gently ask what they think is most likely, and encourage professional support if it persists.

Can mindfulness reduce catastrophic thinking?

Yes. Mindfulness helps people observe thoughts as mental events rather than facts, which can loosen their hold.

Is catastrophizing a cognitive distortion?

Yes. It is one of the common cognitive distortions described in cognitive behavioural therapy.
