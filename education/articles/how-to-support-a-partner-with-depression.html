How to Support a Partner With Depression

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If your partner is talking about suicide or you are worried about their safety, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. You can call 9-8-8 for guidance on supporting someone else. If there is immediate danger, call 911.

Supporting a partner with depression usually involves offering steady, patient presence, listening without trying to fix everything, helping with practical tasks, and gently encouraging professional help, while also looking after your own wellbeing. Depression can change how a person thinks, feels, and behaves, making them withdrawn, irritable, tired, or uninterested in things they once enjoyed, including the relationship. This can be confusing and painful for partners. Depression is a treatable health condition, not a choice or a reflection of how your partner feels about you. Your support can make a real difference, though you cannot cure depression on your own.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

Depression is more than sadness. It often brings heavy fatigue, difficulty concentrating, changes in sleep and appetite, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, and a loss of pleasure in almost everything. Simple tasks can feel exhausting. Your partner may want to connect but feel unable to, or feel numb rather than sad. The condition is explained on the page about major depressive disorder.

Depression can also distort thinking, making people believe they are a burden, that things will never improve, or that their partner would be better off without them. Some people become more irritable or withdrawn rather than visibly sad, which is common in some groups, as described on the page about depression in men. Many people with depression feel guilty about how it affects their relationship, which can add to their distress.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Listen without judgment. Let your partner share how they feel, and acknowledge it, such as "That sounds really hard."
  • Offer specific practical help. Rather than "let me know if you need anything," try "I will make dinner tonight" or "Can I book that appointment with you?"
  • Keep gentle connection. Sit together, watch a show, go for a short walk. Small shared moments matter.
  • Encourage small activities. Invite your partner to do manageable things with you, without pressure.
  • Be patient. Recovery takes time and is rarely a straight line.
  • Remind them it is the depression. Gently note that hopeless thoughts are symptoms, not facts.
  • Learn about depression. Understanding the condition reduces confusion and helps you respond with compassion.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Telling them to snap out of it or to think positively.
  • Taking withdrawal personally. Low interest in sex, conversation, or activities is usually a symptom, not a rejection.
  • Taking over everything. Doing all tasks can reinforce feelings of uselessness. Aim for support, not replacement.
  • Ignoring warning signs. Talk of death, hopelessness, or being a burden should be taken seriously.

Looking After Yourself Too

Living with a depressed partner can be lonely, tiring, and stressful. You may feel helpless, frustrated, sad, or resentful, and these feelings are normal. Keeping your own friendships, activities, sleep, and routines helps you stay steady. It is okay to have limits and to ask for help from others. Some partners find it useful to talk to a counsellor for themselves. Over time, ongoing support without rest can lead to exhaustion, as described on the page about caregiver burnout. Taking care of yourself is not selfish. It allows you to keep offering support.

Encouraging Professional Help

Depression responds well to treatment, which may include therapy, medication prescribed by a physician, and lifestyle changes, as outlined on the page about how depression is treated. Choose a calm time to raise the idea, express care, and share what you have noticed. You might say, "I have noticed you seem really worn down lately, and I care about you. Would you be open to talking to someone?" Offer to help find a therapist, book an appointment, or go with them. If they are already in treatment, encourage them to continue and to tell their care team how they are feeling.

If your partner talks about suicide, has a plan, or you are worried about their safety, act promptly. Ask directly about suicidal thoughts, as explained on the page about supporting someone with suicidal thoughts. Call or text 9-8-8 for guidance, or call 911 if there is immediate danger. Couples counselling can also help partners communicate and cope with depression together.

Common Questions About Supporting a Depressed Partner

What should I say to my partner when they are depressed?

Listen, acknowledge their feelings, and express care. Simple phrases like saying it sounds really hard often help.

Why has my depressed partner lost interest in me?

Depression reduces interest and pleasure in most things. It is usually a symptom, not a reflection of their feelings for you.

How can I help my partner with depression day to day?

Offer specific practical help, share small activities, stay patient, and gently encourage professional support.

Should I push my partner to get out of bed or go out?

Gentle invitations to manageable activities help more than pressure or criticism.

How do I bring up therapy with a depressed partner?

Choose a calm time, express care, share what you have noticed, and offer to help find support.

What are warning signs that my partner needs urgent help?

Talk of death, hopelessness, being a burden, or a plan for suicide means you should seek help right away.

Why is my depressed partner so irritable with me?

Depression can show up as irritability, especially in people who do not appear visibly sad.

How do I cope with the loneliness of supporting a depressed partner?

Keep your own friendships and activities, set limits, and consider talking to a counsellor for yourself.

Can couples counselling help when one partner is depressed?

Yes. It can help partners communicate, reduce conflict, and cope together, often alongside individual treatment.

How long does it take for a partner to recover from depression?

Recovery varies. Many people improve within weeks to months of treatment, though progress is often uneven.
