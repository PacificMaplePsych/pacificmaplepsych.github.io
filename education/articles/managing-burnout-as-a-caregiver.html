Managing Burnout as a Caregiver

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Managing burnout as a caregiver involves sharing the load, protecting time for rest, setting realistic expectations, and getting support for your own emotions and health. Caring for a partner, parent, child, or friend with an illness, disability, or aging-related needs can be deeply meaningful, but it can also be physically and emotionally draining. Caregivers often put their own needs last until exhaustion, resentment, or health problems appear. Caregiver burnout is common and is not a sign of failure. Small, steady changes, combined with practical and emotional support, can make caregiving more sustainable.

Why These Strategies Help

Caregiving often involves long hours, disrupted sleep, financial pressure, complex medical tasks, and constant alertness. Many caregivers also juggle work, children, and other responsibilities. Over time, this can lead to the exhaustion, irritability, and detachment described on the page about caregiver burnout. Some caregivers also experience compassion fatigue, feeling emotionally numb after long exposure to another person's suffering.

Caregiving can also involve grief, as the person being cared for changes or declines. This anticipatory grief can be hard to talk about. Strategies that share responsibility, restore energy, and create space for feelings help prevent the stress system from staying stuck on high alert, and help caregivers keep going without losing themselves.

Strategies to Try

1. Accept and Ask for Help

Make a list of specific tasks others could do, such as picking up groceries, sitting with your loved one for an afternoon, or handling paperwork. When people offer help, you can point to the list.

2. Use Respite Care

Respite services provide short-term care so caregivers can rest. In BC, home and community care through health authorities may offer respite or adult day programs. A family doctor or health authority case manager can explain options.

3. Protect Small Pockets of Time

Even short breaks, a walk, a phone call with a friend, or quiet time with tea, help restore energy.

4. Set Realistic Expectations

No one can do everything. Decide which tasks matter most, and let go of perfection in other areas.

5. Look After Your Health

Keep your own medical appointments, protect sleep where possible, eat regular meals, and move your body. Caregivers often neglect their health, which increases the risk of burnout.

6. Share the Load Within the Family

Hold a family meeting to discuss care needs, divide tasks, and plan for the future. Being specific about what is needed reduces misunderstandings.

7. Connect With Other Caregivers

Caregiver support groups, in person or online, offer understanding, practical tips, and relief from isolation.

8. Make Room for Your Feelings

It is common to feel love, frustration, guilt, sadness, and resentment all at once. Talking or writing about these feelings helps you process them without judgment.

Common Obstacles

Many caregivers feel guilty taking time for themselves or accepting help, especially if they believe they should be able to manage alone. Remembering that rest helps you care for someone else can ease this guilt. Some caregivers find it hard to trust others with care, especially for complex needs. Starting with short breaks and clear instructions can build confidence. Others face limited resources, such as long waitlists for services, cost barriers, or family members who are unwilling to help. Focusing on what is within reach and asking a care coordinator or social worker for guidance can help.

Cultural and family expectations can also shape caregiving. In some families, one person is expected to take on most of the care. Talking openly about limits and needs, and seeking support that fits your values, matters. Parents of children with high needs may also experience parental burnout.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If you feel exhausted most of the time, hopeless, resentful, or detached, if your sleep or health is suffering, or if you notice increased alcohol use or thoughts of harming yourself, professional support can help. Therapy offers space to process difficult feelings, set boundaries, and plan for sustainable caregiving. A doctor can check your health and help connect you with services. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time. If you ever feel close to harming the person you care for, step away and reach out for immediate help.

Common Questions About Managing Caregiver Burnout

How do I ask family members to help with caregiving?

Be specific about tasks and times, and consider a family meeting to divide responsibilities.

What is respite care for caregivers?

Respite care provides short-term care for your loved one so you can rest, attend appointments, or take a break.

How can I find respite services in BC?

A family doctor or health authority case manager can explain home and community care options, including respite and day programs.

Why do I feel resentful toward the person I care for?

Resentment is common when caregiving is exhausting and needs go unmet. It does not mean you do not love them.

Is it selfish to take time away from caregiving?

No. Rest helps you care for someone else and reduces the risk of burnout.

How can a caregiver support group help me?

Groups offer understanding, practical tips, and relief from isolation from people facing similar challenges.

How do I cope with grief while my loved one is still alive?

Anticipatory grief is common. Talking about it, joining a group, or seeing a counsellor can help.

What should I do if I feel close to losing my temper with the person I care for?

Step away safely, take a break, and reach out for help. Frequent strong reactions are a sign you need more support.

I keep cancelling my own doctor visits to care for my dad. Does that matter?

Keep your medical appointments, protect sleep where possible, eat regularly, and move your body.

When should a caregiver see a therapist?

If you feel exhausted, hopeless, detached, or overwhelmed most of the time, therapy can help you cope.
