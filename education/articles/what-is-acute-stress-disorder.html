What Is Acute Stress Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you have been injured, are in danger, or feel unable to keep yourself safe after a traumatic event, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time.

Acute stress disorder is a mental health condition that can develop in the days and weeks after a traumatic event, such as a serious accident, assault, natural disaster, sudden loss, or witnessing violence. It involves intense reactions, including intrusive memories, nightmares, feeling detached or unreal, avoiding reminders, and being constantly on alert. The diagnosis applies when symptoms last from three days to one month after the event and cause significant distress. Many people experience some of these reactions after trauma, and they often fade with time and support. Acute stress disorder describes a more intense response that benefits from attention.

What Acute Stress Disorder Looks Like

  • Intrusive memories. Unwanted memories, flashbacks, or nightmares about the event.
  • Negative mood. Difficulty feeling positive emotions such as happiness or love.
  • Dissociation. Feeling numb, dazed, or detached from reality, or having gaps in memory for parts of the event.
  • Avoidance. Avoiding thoughts, conversations, places, or people linked with the trauma.
  • Arousal. Trouble sleeping, irritability, poor concentration, and startling easily.
  • Strong reactions to reminders of the event.

People may also feel guilt, shame, or anger, and wonder why they cannot just move on. Friends and family may not understand how deeply the event has affected them.

Why It Happens

When a person faces a threat to life or safety, the brain's survival systems activate intensely. Memories of the event may be stored in fragmented, vivid ways, and the threat system can remain on high alert even after the danger has passed. In the first weeks, this is part of how the mind processes what happened. For some people, the response is especially strong.

Risk factors include the severity of the trauma, physical injury, a history of previous trauma or mental health concerns, strong dissociation during the event, and limited support afterward. Dissociation, described further on the page about dissociation, can be a protective response during overwhelming events but can also make processing harder. Ongoing stress after the event, such as legal proceedings, financial problems, or continued danger, can prolong symptoms.

Children and teens can also develop acute stress reactions. They may show distress through clinginess, trouble sleeping, repetitive play about the event, or changes in behaviour at school. Calm routines, reassurance, and age-appropriate information help, along with professional support if reactions are intense.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign of weakness. Strong reactions after trauma are a normal human response.
  • Not the same as PTSD, though it can lead there. PTSD is diagnosed when symptoms last longer than one month.
  • Not inevitable after trauma. Many people recover naturally, especially with support.
  • Not something to force yourself to talk about immediately. Pressured debriefing right after trauma is not recommended.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about the traumatic event, symptoms, their timing, and safety. In the early period, practical support, safety, rest, and connection with trusted people are important. Psychological first aid focuses on calming, practical help, and linking people to resources. When symptoms are intense, trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy started within the first weeks can reduce the risk of developing PTSD. Therapy may include learning about trauma reactions, grounding skills, gradually processing the memory, and reducing avoidance. Sleep and limiting alcohol also support recovery. If symptoms persist beyond a month, treatment for PTSD may be recommended.

Common Questions About Acute Stress Disorder

Why have I felt so shaken for weeks since the accident?

Intense reactions in the first month after trauma are common. When they are severe, they may be acute stress disorder, which involves intense trauma reactions, such as intrusive memories, dissociation, avoidance, and hyperarousal, lasting three days to one month.

How is acute stress disorder different from PTSD?

Acute stress disorder occurs within the first month after trauma. PTSD is diagnosed when symptoms last longer than a month.

Does acute stress disorder always turn into PTSD?

No. Many people recover, but acute stress disorder does increase the risk, so early support is helpful.

Is it normal to feel numb or unreal after a traumatic event?

Yes. Numbness, feeling dazed, or detachment are common dissociative responses after trauma and often ease with time.

What should I do in the first days after a traumatic event?

Focus on safety, rest, basic needs, and connecting with trusted people. Seek professional support if reactions are intense.

Should I talk about the trauma right away?

You do not have to. Pressured debriefing immediately after trauma is not recommended. Share when and with whom you feel ready.

What is psychological first aid?

Psychological first aid is early support focused on safety, calming, practical help, and connecting people with resources.

Can early therapy lower the chance of developing PTSD?

Yes. Trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy started in the first weeks can reduce symptoms and lower that risk.

How do children show acute stress after trauma?

Children may be clingy, have trouble sleeping, replay the event in play, or show behaviour changes at school.

How can I support someone after a traumatic event?

Offer calm presence, practical help, and a listening ear without pressure, and encourage professional support if symptoms are intense.
