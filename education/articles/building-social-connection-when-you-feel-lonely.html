Building Social Connection When You Feel Lonely

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Building social connection when you feel lonely usually works best through small, regular steps, such as reaching out to one person, joining a recurring activity, or deepening a connection you already have. Loneliness is the painful feeling that your relationships do not meet your needs for closeness or belonging. It can happen whether you live alone or with others, have few friends or many. Loneliness is common, especially during transitions such as moving, retiring, a breakup, becoming a parent, or starting a new job. Connection can be rebuilt, though it often takes time and patience with yourself.

Why Connection Helps

Humans are social beings. Connection supports mood, stress management, and physical health. Research has linked long-term loneliness with increased risk of depression, anxiety, poor sleep, and some health problems. The experience is explored further on the page about loneliness.

Loneliness can also change how people think. When lonely, the brain may become more alert to possible rejection, making social situations feel riskier and leading people to withdraw. This can create a cycle, where withdrawal increases loneliness. Small, positive social experiences help break the cycle and rebuild confidence. Connection does not have to mean a large social circle. A few meaningful relationships, and a sense of belonging in a community, often matter most.

Strategies to Try

1. Start With Existing Connections

Reach out to someone you have lost touch with, such as an old friend, coworker, or relative. A short message, such as "I was thinking of you, how have you been?" can reopen a door.

2. Join Something Recurring

Regular activities, such as a class, club, sports team, choir, volunteer group, or faith community, give repeated contact with the same people, which is how friendships often form.

3. Follow Your Interests

Groups built around shared interests, such as hiking, books, games, crafts, or gardening, make conversation easier.

4. Volunteer

Helping others builds connection, purpose, and a sense of belonging.

5. Say Yes More Often

Accepting invitations, even when you feel hesitant, creates opportunities. You can set a time limit if that helps.

6. Take the Initiative

Invite someone for coffee or a walk. Many people are waiting for someone else to make the first move.

7. Deepen Conversations Gradually

Share a little more about yourself over time, and ask questions that show interest in others. Closeness grows through mutual sharing.

8. Use Technology Thoughtfully

Video calls, messaging, and online communities can help, especially for people with limited mobility or living far from loved ones. Balance these with in-person contact where possible.

9. Be Patient

Research suggests friendships usually take many hours of shared time to develop. It is normal for connections to grow slowly.

Common Obstacles

Social anxiety can make reaching out feel frightening. Starting with low-pressure situations, such as activities where the focus is on a task, can help. If fear is intense, the page about social anxiety disorder may be useful. Low mood can reduce energy and make people feel unwanted, so small steps count. Past rejection or hurt can make trust feel risky.

Practical barriers also matter, such as long work hours, caregiving, disability, cost, or living in a new place. Newcomers to Canada may face language barriers and cultural differences, as described on the page about acculturation stress. Life stages such as retirement or children leaving home, explored on the page about empty nest syndrome, can also bring loneliness. Neurodivergent people may find some social settings draining and may prefer smaller or interest-based groups.

It helps to notice self-critical thoughts, such as "no one wants to spend time with me," and treat them as thoughts rather than facts.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If loneliness is persistent, if it comes with low mood, hopelessness, or anxiety that makes connection feel impossible, or if you have thoughts of suicide, professional support can help. Therapy can help with social anxiety, self-esteem, grief, and building relationships. Community services, such as neighbourhood houses, newcomer programs, and seniors' centres, can also offer connection. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Building Connection

Where do I begin rebuilding a social life after years of drifting from people?

Join recurring activities, follow your interests, reach out to people you know, and take the initiative to invite someone.

I have a partner and coworkers, so why do I still feel so alone?

Loneliness is about whether relationships meet your needs for closeness and belonging, not the number of people around you.

How long does it take to build a new friendship?

Friendships often take many hours of shared time to develop, so connections usually grow gradually.

What should I say when reaching out to an old friend?

A short, warm message, such as saying you were thinking of them and asking how they are, is a good start.

Can volunteering help with loneliness?

Yes. Volunteering builds connection, purpose, and a sense of belonging.

Why do I cancel plans when I feel lonely and badly want company?

Loneliness can make the brain more alert to rejection, leading people to pull away, which can deepen loneliness.

Are online communities a good way to connect?

They can help, especially for people with limited mobility, and work well when balanced with in-person contact.

How can I build connection when social situations make me anxious?

Start with low-pressure, activity-based settings, take small steps, and consider support for social anxiety.

Where can newcomers to Canada find community?

Newcomer programs, neighbourhood houses, cultural associations, and faith communities can help.

When is loneliness a reason to see a therapist?

If loneliness is persistent or comes with low mood, hopelessness, or anxiety, therapy can help.
