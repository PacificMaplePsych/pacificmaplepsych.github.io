Why Is It So Hard to Sit Still

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Your leg is bouncing again. You have shifted position in your chair for the fifth time in ten minutes. Long meetings, movies, and car rides feel like a genuine physical effort to sit through, not just a mental one. If sitting still has always felt harder for you than it seems to be for other people, it is worth understanding the range of things that can cause that, rather than assuming it simply reflects poor self-control.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Anxiety. Restlessness is a common physical symptom of anxiety, sometimes showing up as an inability to settle the body even when the mind is not consciously racing. This kind of somatic anxiety can feel almost purely physical, which sometimes makes it harder to connect back to its emotional source.

Caffeine and stimulants. High caffeine intake, especially later in the day or on an empty stomach, produces genuine physical restlessness and jitteriness in many people, entirely independent of any underlying condition.

Restless legs syndrome. This is a distinct, medically recognized neurological condition involving an uncomfortable urge to move the legs, often worse in the evening or when sitting or lying still for extended periods. It is worth ruling out with a doctor if restlessness is specifically concentrated in the legs and worsens at rest.

Medication side effects. Certain medications can produce a restless, jittery physical sensation as a side effect, sometimes called akathisia in more pronounced cases. If restlessness appeared or worsened after starting a new medication, that timing is worth mentioning to whoever prescribed it.

Sensory needs. For some people, particularly those who are autistic, movement and fidgeting serve an important sensory regulation function, helping to manage sensory input rather than reflecting boredom or inattention.

General energy and boredom. Some people simply run at a higher baseline energy level than others, and situations that require prolonged stillness without enough mental stimulation can feel disproportionately difficult regardless of any diagnosis.

When It Looks More Like ADHD

Physical restlessness is part of the hyperactive-impulsive presentation of ADHD, and in children it often looks the way people expect, visible fidgeting, difficulty staying seated, and constant movement. In adults, this frequently changes shape rather than disappearing. It can turn more internal, showing up as an inner sense of restlessness or agitation that is not always outwardly visible, alongside more subtle physical signs like a bouncing leg, tapping, or a persistent need to shift position.

An interesting and often reassuring detail for people with ADHD is that fidgeting sometimes actually helps concentration rather than working against it. Small, repetitive movement can provide just enough stimulation to help the brain stay engaged with a task, which is part of why simply telling someone to sit still and stop fidgeting can backfire, making focus harder rather than easier.

What tends to distinguish ADHD-related restlessness from anxiety-driven restlessness is context. ADHD-related restlessness tends to show up specifically in situations requiring sustained stillness or attention, such as meetings, lectures, or long car rides, regardless of whether anything is stressful about the situation itself. Anxiety-driven restlessness, by contrast, tends to track more closely with situations or thoughts that are specifically worrying, and often comes bundled with other anxiety symptoms like a racing mind or physical tension.

What Can Help

Sanctioned, intentional movement tends to work better than trying to suppress restlessness entirely. Fidget tools, standing desks, taking short walking breaks, or simply allowing yourself to move more during low stakes moments can reduce the pressure of trying to sit perfectly still. Regular physical activity outside of the specific restless moments can also help lower the overall baseline. Reframing fidgeting as a legitimate self-regulation strategy, rather than something to feel embarrassed about or suppress, tends to reduce both the restlessness itself and the shame that often accompanies it.

Common Questions About Restlessness and Fidgeting

Is restlessness always physical, or can it be purely mental?

It can be either, or both at once. Some people experience restlessness mainly as an inner mental agitation with few visible outward signs, while others experience it primarily as physical fidgeting or an urge to move, and many people experience a mix of both.

Could this be restless legs syndrome instead of ADHD?

It is possible, particularly if the sensation is specifically concentrated in the legs, involves an uncomfortable urge to move, and tends to worsen in the evening or when sitting or lying still. This is a distinct medical condition worth discussing with a doctor.

Does caffeine intake explain most of this?

For some people, yes, particularly if restlessness closely tracks with how much caffeine was consumed and when. If restlessness is present regardless of caffeine intake and has been a lifelong pattern, other explanations become more likely.

Why does fidgeting sometimes help me concentrate rather than distract me?

For many people, especially those with ADHD, small repetitive movement provides just enough sensory stimulation to help sustain attention on a task, rather than pulling attention away from it. This is a well recognized pattern, not a contradiction.

Is physical restlessness a symptom of anxiety too?

Yes. Restlessness is a common physical symptom of anxiety and can occur even without a conscious sense of worry. It tends to track more closely with specific stressful situations or thoughts, compared to the more consistent, context-independent restlessness often seen in ADHD.

Do adults with ADHD still show visible hyperactivity, or does it change?

It often changes shape. Visible, outward hyperactivity is more common in children with ADHD, while adults more often experience an internal sense of restlessness alongside subtler physical signs, such as a bouncing leg or a persistent need to shift position.

Can certain medications cause restlessness as a side effect?

Yes. Some medications can produce restlessness or jitteriness as a side effect, and in more pronounced cases this is called akathisia. If restlessness began or worsened after starting a new medication, it is worth discussing the timing with your prescriber.

Is it unhealthy to suppress the urge to move?

Consistently suppressing a genuine physical or sensory need to move can increase discomfort and make concentration harder rather than easier. Finding sanctioned, low disruption ways to move, rather than forcing complete stillness, tends to work better.

How does a clinician tell the difference between ADHD and anxiety-driven restlessness?

A clinician will look at whether restlessness shows up consistently across many settings regardless of stress level, whether it has been present since childhood, and whether it occurs alongside other ADHD features like inattention and impulsivity, versus tracking closely with specific worries or anxious thoughts.

What accommodations exist for restlessness in school or work settings?

Common accommodations include permission to stand or move during lectures or meetings, access to fidget tools, standing desk options, and scheduled movement breaks. What is appropriate depends on the setting and is often easier to arrange once a clear assessment has identified the underlying cause.
