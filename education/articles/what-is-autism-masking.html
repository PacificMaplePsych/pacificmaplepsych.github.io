What Is Autism Masking

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Autism masking, also called camouflaging, refers to the conscious or unconscious effort autistic people make to hide or suppress autistic traits in order to fit in with non-autistic expectations. It can include copying others' social behaviour, forcing eye contact, suppressing stimming, and scripting conversations. Masking can help people avoid rejection or discrimination, but it takes a great deal of energy and is linked with exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and autistic burnout. Masking also contributes to late or missed diagnosis, especially in women and girls.

For a personal description of how masking can feel, see the page on feeling like you are performing in social situations.

What Autism Masking Looks Like

  • Studying and copying other people's expressions, gestures, and phrases.
  • Forcing or rehearsing eye contact, even when it is uncomfortable, as explored on the page about struggling with eye contact.
  • Suppressing stims, such as rocking or hand movements.
  • Preparing scripts for conversations and social situations.
  • Hiding intense interests or sensory discomfort.
  • Pretending to understand jokes or social cues.
  • Changing personality to match different groups.

At work, masking might look like appearing socially confident while feeling drained. At home, people may collapse after a day of masking, needing silence and solitude. Some mask so well that others, and even they themselves, do not realize they are autistic.

Why It Happens

Masking often begins in childhood, when autistic children notice they are treated differently for being themselves. Teasing, correction, or exclusion teach them to hide traits to stay safe or be accepted. Social expectations about eye contact, small talk, and emotional expression reward masking. Some people mask to protect jobs, relationships, or safety. Research suggests autistic women and girls mask more on average, possibly due to social expectations, which contributes to their later diagnosis. Masking can become so habitual that people lose touch with what their unmasked self feels like.

The costs build over time. Masking uses cognitive and emotional energy continuously, leaving less for work, relationships, and rest. Many autistic adults connect years of masking with exhaustion after socializing, burnout, and a sense of not knowing who they really are.

Unmasking is rarely all or nothing. Many autistic adults start by noticing which masking behaviours cost them the most, such as forced eye contact or suppressing stims, and allowing themselves to drop those in safe settings first. Spending time with other neurodivergent people often feels easier, because less translation is needed. Over time, people may find a middle ground that protects their wellbeing while still meeting the demands of work and daily life.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary politeness. Everyone adapts socially, but autistic masking involves suppressing core traits, often at significant cost.
  • Not deception. Masking is usually a survival strategy, not an attempt to fool people.
  • Not only autistic. People with ADHD and other differences mask too, though autistic masking is especially well studied.
  • Not something everyone can simply stop. Unmasking safely depends on environment and support.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

Masking is explored during adult autism assessments, which consider how traits may be hidden. Clinicians ask about childhood experiences, social effort, and recovery needs. Support focuses on reducing the costs of masking: identifying safe spaces to unmask, building self-understanding, setting boundaries around social demands, and addressing burnout, anxiety, or depression. Neurodiversity-affirming therapy helps people explore their authentic preferences and communicate their needs.

Common Questions About Autism Masking

What does masking mean for autistic people?

Masking means hiding or suppressing autistic traits, such as copying social behaviour or forcing eye contact, to fit in with non-autistic expectations.

Is camouflaging the same as masking?

Yes. Camouflaging and masking are used interchangeably to describe hiding autistic traits in social situations.

Why do autistic women mask more?

Research suggests autistic women and girls mask more on average, likely influenced by social expectations, which contributes to later or missed diagnoses.

What are the long-term effects of masking?

Long-term masking is linked with exhaustion, anxiety, depression, burnout, and a reduced sense of identity.

How does years of camouflaging build toward a crash?

Yes. The constant energy required to mask can contribute to autistic burnout, a state of deep exhaustion and reduced ability to function.

Why do I not know who I am without masking?

Masking from a young age can become so habitual that people lose touch with their natural preferences. Exploring these in safe settings can help.

How do autism assessments account for masking?

Clinicians ask about childhood, social effort, recovery needs, and hidden traits, since masking can make autism less visible during interviews.

Is it safe to stop masking?

It depends on the environment. Many people unmask gradually in safe settings, with supportive people, while making choices about when masking still feels necessary.

Do people with ADHD mask too?

Yes. People with ADHD often mask by over-preparing or hiding difficulties. Masking is especially well studied in autism but occurs in other neurodivergent people.

How can therapy help with reducing masking?

Neurodiversity-affirming therapy can help people understand their traits, identify safe spaces to unmask, set boundaries, and recover from burnout.
