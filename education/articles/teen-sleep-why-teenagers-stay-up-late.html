Teen Sleep: Why Teenagers Stay Up Late

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Teenagers often stay up late because their internal body clock naturally shifts later during adolescence. The brain releases melatonin, the hormone that signals sleepiness, later in the evening than it did in childhood, so many teens genuinely do not feel tired until 11 p.m. or later. Combined with early school start times, homework, activities, social connection, and phones, this means many teens get far less than the 8 to 10 hours of sleep they need. Chronic sleep loss affects mood, attention, learning, and risk-taking. Some teens develop a more extreme pattern called delayed sleep-wake phase, where falling asleep before the early hours of the morning is very difficult. Small, consistent changes can help.

Why Teens Stay Up Late

A Shifting Body Clock

Puberty shifts the circadian rhythm later, so teens are biologically inclined to fall asleep and wake later.

Slower Build-Up of Sleep Pressure

The drive to sleep builds more slowly during the day in adolescence, making it easier to stay awake at night.

Screens and Social Life

Phones keep teens connected at night, and light and stimulation delay sleep further, as described on the page about changing your relationship with your phone.

Busy Schedules

School, homework, sports, and jobs can push bedtime later.

Stress and Mood

Anxiety, low mood, and racing thoughts can make it hard to settle, and some teens stay up late to get time to themselves, as described on the page about why people stay up late even when tired.

How Sleep Loss Affects Teens

  • Mood. Irritability, low mood, and anxiety increase with poor sleep.
  • Learning. Attention, memory, and grades can suffer.
  • Risk. Drowsy driving and risk-taking increase.
  • Health. Sleep supports growth, immunity, and physical health.
  • Weekend catch-up. Sleeping in very late on weekends shifts the body clock further and makes Monday mornings harder.

What Can Help

  • Keep wake times fairly consistent. Try to limit weekend sleep-ins to about two hours later than weekdays.
  • Get morning light. Bright light soon after waking helps shift the body clock earlier.
  • Dim the evenings. Lower lights and reduce screens in the last hour before bed.
  • Charge phones outside the bedroom. This reduces late-night scrolling and notifications.
  • Limit caffeine after midday, including energy drinks.
  • Wind down. Calm routines, such as reading or a warm shower, help, as described on the page about sleep hygiene strategies.
  • Involve your teen. Teens are more likely to change habits they help choose.

School Start Times

Research suggests that later school start times are associated with more sleep and better mood and attendance in teens. While families cannot usually change school schedules, understanding this biology can help parents respond with more empathy.

When to Get Help

If your teen cannot fall asleep until very late despite consistent efforts, is missing school, falls asleep in class, or shows signs of anxiety or depression, it may be time to get support. A physician can check for medical issues, assess for delayed sleep-wake phase, and discuss treatment, such as timed light exposure. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia can be adapted for teens, as described on the page about CBT for insomnia. If low mood or worry is part of the picture, therapy for teens can help, and the page about why parenting a teenager is so hard offers support for parents.

What It Is Not

A teen who stays up late is not necessarily lazy or defiant. Their biology makes early sleep genuinely harder. That said, sleep loss is still a real concern, and supporting healthy sleep is worth the effort.

Common Questions About Teen Sleep

Why do teenagers stay up so late?

Teenagers stay up late because their body clock naturally shifts later during adolescence, releasing melatonin later in the evening.

How much sleep do teenagers need?

Teenagers need about 8 to 10 hours of sleep per night, though many get much less.

Is it normal for teens to sleep in on weekends?

Weekend sleep-ins are common in teens, but sleeping much later than weekdays can shift the body clock and make weekdays harder.

When is a teen's late bedtime more than normal teen sleep?

A teen's late bedtime may be delayed sleep-wake phase if they cannot fall asleep until very late despite efforts and regularly struggle to wake for school.

Do phones affect teen sleep?

Yes. Phones affect teen sleep through light, stimulation, and late-night social contact, which delay falling asleep.

How does lack of sleep affect teenagers?

Lack of sleep in teenagers can affect mood, attention, learning, risk-taking, and physical health.

How can I help my teenager sleep better?

Help your teen sleep better with consistent wake times, morning light, dim evenings, phones charged outside the bedroom, and less caffeine.

Is lack of sleep linked to low mood in teenagers?

Yes. Lack of sleep is linked to low mood, irritability, and anxiety in teenagers, and depression can also disrupt sleep.

Should my teenager take melatonin?

Talk to a physician before your teenager takes melatonin. Timing and dose matter, and it is not right for everyone.

When should I get help for my teen's sleep?

Get help if your teen cannot fall asleep until very late despite efforts, misses school, or shows signs of anxiety or depression.
