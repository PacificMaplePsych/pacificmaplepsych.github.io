Why Am I So Hard on Myself

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Being hard on yourself usually means you have developed a strong inner critic, a voice that points out mistakes, sets high standards, and rarely offers credit. For many people, this voice began as a way to stay safe, avoid criticism, or earn approval, and it often feels like the thing that keeps them motivated. Over time, though, harsh self-criticism tends to increase stress, anxiety, and low mood, and it rarely improves performance. It is a learned pattern, which means it can be softened.

People describe calling themselves stupid after small mistakes, focusing on the one thing that went wrong in an otherwise good day, dismissing their achievements, or speaking to themselves in ways they would never speak to a friend.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Early criticism. Growing up with frequent correction, comparison, or conditional approval can teach children to criticize themselves before anyone else can.

Perfectionism. People who feel they have to be perfect often use self-criticism to push themselves.

Comparison. People who compare themselves to everyone around them often come out feeling short.

Anxiety. Anxious minds replay mistakes and imagine how others will judge them.

ADHD or learning differences. Years of struggling with tasks others found easy, and being called lazy or careless, can build a harsh self-image.

Workplace or cultural pressure. Environments that reward constant achievement can reinforce self-criticism.

When It Looks More Like a Self-Esteem Concern

Self-criticism becomes a concern when it shapes how you see your whole self, not just your performance. At that point, it is closely tied to low self-esteem. Signs include:

  • A persistent sense of never being good enough, even after success.
  • Dismissing praise and struggling to accept compliments.
  • Believing that if you stopped criticizing yourself, you would become lazy or fail.
  • Feeling shame, not just disappointment, after mistakes.
  • Avoiding challenges because failure would confirm your worst beliefs.
  • Low mood, anxiety, or exhaustion linked to constant self-judgment.

Research on self-compassion suggests that treating yourself with kindness after setbacks is linked with greater resilience and motivation, not less. The fear that self-compassion will make you complacent is common, but people who respond to mistakes with understanding are often more willing to try again. Chronic self-criticism is also linked with depression, so if the inner critic comes with persistent low mood, it is worth seeking support.

What Can Help

  • Notice the critic. Recognizing the voice, and even giving it a name, helps you see it as a pattern rather than the truth.
  • Ask what you would say to a friend. Speaking to yourself as you would to someone you care about often softens the tone.
  • Balance the record. At the end of the day, write down a few things that went well or that you handled with effort.
  • Separate standards from worth. You can aim high while accepting that falling short does not make you less valuable.
  • Treat mistakes as information. Ask what you can learn rather than what the mistake says about you.
  • Practise self-compassion. A brief pause to acknowledge that something is hard, that others struggle too, and to offer yourself kindness can reduce shame.

Common Questions About Being Hard on Yourself

Why am I so critical of myself?

Self-criticism often develops from early criticism, perfectionism, anxiety, or repeated struggles. It can start as a way to avoid disapproval but becomes a habit that increases stress.

What is an inner critic in psychology?

An inner critic is the internal voice that judges, blames, and points out flaws. Everyone has one, but for some people it is harsh, constant, and hard to question.

Does self-criticism help me improve?

Research suggests harsh self-criticism tends to increase anxiety and avoidance, while self-compassion is linked with greater motivation and resilience after setbacks.

Will I become lazy if I stop being hard on myself?

This is a common fear, but self-compassion usually supports effort and persistence. People who treat themselves kindly after mistakes are often more willing to try again.

Can childhood experiences make someone overly self-critical?

Yes. Frequent criticism, comparison, or approval that depended on achievement can teach children to judge themselves harshly, a pattern that often continues into adulthood.

Is being hard on myself linked to depression?

Chronic self-criticism is associated with depression and can both contribute to it and be worsened by it. If self-criticism comes with low mood, consider seeking support.

How can I stop calling myself names after mistakes?

Notice the words you use, pause, and replace them with something you would say to a friend. With practice, the automatic harsh response becomes less frequent.

What is compassion-focused therapy?

Compassion-focused therapy helps people who experience high shame and self-criticism develop a kinder inner voice and a greater sense of safety with themselves.

Why do I focus on the one thing that went wrong?

The brain tends to notice threats and mistakes more than successes. Anxiety and perfectionism strengthen this tendency, so deliberately noticing what went well can help balance it.

How long does it take to become less self-critical?

It varies, but many people notice shifts within weeks to months of practising self-compassion or working with a therapist. Change tends to be gradual rather than sudden.
