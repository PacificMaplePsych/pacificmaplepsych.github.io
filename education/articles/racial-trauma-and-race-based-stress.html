Racial Trauma and Race-Based Stress

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Racial trauma, also called race-based traumatic stress, describes the emotional and psychological impact of experiencing racism and discrimination. It can result from direct experiences, such as racial violence, harassment, or being treated unfairly at work or school, as well as from repeated everyday slights known as microaggressions, systemic barriers, and witnessing racism against others, including in the media. Its effects can include anxiety, hypervigilance, anger, sadness, low self-worth, and symptoms similar to PTSD. Racial trauma is not a formal diagnosis, but research consistently links racism to poorer mental and physical health. It is often cumulative, building over time and across generations.

Sources of Race-Based Stress

  • Overt racism, such as racial slurs, threats, or violence
  • Discrimination in employment, housing, education, health care, or policing
  • Microaggressions, such as comments about where you are "really" from or assumptions about ability
  • Systemic racism embedded in institutions and policies
  • Vicarious exposure, such as seeing racial violence in the news or online
  • Historical and generational trauma, including colonization and residential schools, as described on the page about generational trauma

How Racial Trauma Can Show Up

  • Hypervigilance, constantly scanning for threat or bias, as described on the page about hypervigilance
  • Anxiety and fear in certain settings
  • Anger and frustration
  • Sadness, grief, or hopelessness
  • Shame or internalized negative beliefs
  • Physical symptoms, such as headaches, poor sleep, or tension
  • Avoidance of places or people associated with discrimination
  • Code-switching exhaustion, from constantly adjusting how you speak or present yourself

Why It Matters

Repeated experiences of racism act as chronic stressors. Over time, they can affect mental health, sleep, and physical health. Many people are told they are overreacting, which can add self-doubt and isolation. Newcomers may experience racism alongside the stresses of migration, as described on the page about acculturation stress.

Racial Trauma in Health Care and Therapy

Some people hesitate to seek mental health support because of past experiences of discrimination in health care or concern that a therapist will not understand or believe them. Others worry that talking about racism will be minimized or reframed as a personal problem. These concerns are understandable. A culturally responsive therapist acknowledges racism as real, asks about cultural identity and experiences without assumptions, and does not expect clients to educate them. It is reasonable to ask a potential therapist how they approach race and culture in therapy and to choose someone you feel comfortable with. Therapy can also be a place to explore the impact of generational and community experiences, not only individual events.

What It Is Not

Racial trauma is not a personal weakness or oversensitivity. Its effects are real responses to real experiences. It is also not limited to dramatic events. The cumulative impact of everyday discrimination can be just as significant.

What Supports Healing

  • Validation. Having your experiences acknowledged rather than dismissed.
  • Community and connection. Spending time with people who share or understand your experiences.
  • Cultural strengths. Drawing on traditions, identity, and pride.
  • Boundaries. Limiting exposure to distressing news or harmful environments when possible.
  • Advocacy and action, for those who find meaning in it.
  • Culturally responsive therapy, in which the therapist understands and acknowledges racism and its effects.

Some people prefer a therapist who shares their cultural background or language, as described on the page about therapy in Punjabi, Cantonese, Mandarin, and Urdu.

When to Seek Support

Consider support if experiences of racism are affecting your mood, sleep, relationships, or work, or if you notice trauma symptoms. A therapist who takes racism seriously and practises culturally responsive, trauma-informed therapy can help you process experiences and build resilience.

Common Questions About Racial Trauma

Is race-based traumatic stress the same as racial trauma?

Yes. Race-based traumatic stress is another name for racial trauma, the emotional and psychological impact of experiencing racism and discrimination.

Is racial trauma a diagnosis?

Racial trauma is not a formal diagnosis, but research consistently links racism to poorer mental and physical health.

Can microaggressions cause racial trauma?

Yes. Repeated microaggressions, such as comments about where someone is really from, can add up over time and contribute to race-based stress.

How does racial trauma show up in everyday life?

Racial trauma can show up as hypervigilance, anxiety, anger, sadness, shame, physical tension, and avoiding places linked to discrimination.

Can racism cause PTSD symptoms?

Yes. Experiences of racism can lead to trauma symptoms similar to PTSD, especially after violence or repeated discrimination.

Can watching racist incidents in the news be traumatic?

Yes. Repeated exposure to racial violence in the news or online can cause vicarious race-based stress.

What is generational racial trauma?

Generational racial trauma is the passing of the effects of historical racism, such as colonization, across generations.

How can I cope with racial trauma?

Coping with racial trauma can include validation, community, cultural strengths, boundaries, and culturally responsive therapy.

What is culturally responsive therapy?

Culturally responsive therapy is therapy in which the clinician understands and respects a client's culture and acknowledges the effects of racism.

Can I find a therapist who shares my cultural background?

Many people prefer a therapist who shares their background or language, and our team can help you find a good fit.
