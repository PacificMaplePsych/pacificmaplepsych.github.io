Medication vs. Therapy for Depression

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Do not stop an antidepressant suddenly without talking to your prescriber.

Both medication and therapy are effective treatments for depression, and the best choice depends on how severe the depression is, your history, your preferences, and practical factors. For mild depression, therapy and lifestyle changes are often recommended first. For moderate to severe depression, a combination of therapy and antidepressant medication prescribed by a physician tends to work better than either alone for many people. Therapy teaches skills that can lower the risk of depression returning, while medication can reduce symptoms enough to make daily life and therapy more manageable. It is not a question of which is better overall, but of what fits your situation right now.

Why They Are Easy to Confuse

Research shows that therapy, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, and antidepressant medication produce similar average improvements for many people with moderate depression. Both can lift mood, improve sleep and energy, and reduce hopelessness. Both usually take several weeks to show benefit. Because of this overlap, people often hear conflicting advice about which to choose. Personal stories, stigma, and worries about side effects or cost can also make the decision feel confusing.

Key Differences

  • How they work. Antidepressants act on brain chemistry and systems involved in mood. Therapy changes thought patterns, behaviours, relationships, and coping skills.
  • Speed. Both usually take a few weeks. Some people notice changes in sleep or energy from medication before mood lifts. Therapy often builds gradually over several months.
  • Effort involved. Medication requires taking a daily dose and attending follow-ups. Therapy requires regular sessions and often practice between them, which can be hard when energy is very low.
  • Side effects. Medication can cause side effects, such as stomach upset, sleep changes, or sexual side effects, which vary by person and often ease or can be managed. Therapy can bring up difficult emotions but does not have physical side effects.
  • After stopping. Skills learned in therapy often continue to protect against relapse after sessions end. Medication benefits may fade if it is stopped too early, so prescribers usually recommend continuing for a period after recovery.
  • Access and cost. Medication may be partly covered by drug plans, while therapy costs vary and may be covered by extended health benefits.

When Both Are Used Together

Combining medication and therapy is common, especially for moderate to severe depression, depression that keeps returning, or depression with significant anxiety. Medication may reduce symptoms enough that someone can engage in therapy, while therapy helps address thought patterns, relationships, and life circumstances. Some people start with one and add the other if progress is slow. The full range of options is described on the page about how depression is treated.

Why the Distinction Matters

Understanding the differences helps you make an informed choice with your care team. Questions to consider include:

  • How severe is my depression, and how much is it affecting daily life?
  • Have I had depression before, and what helped then?
  • Do I have the energy to take part in therapy right now?
  • What are my concerns about medication or therapy?
  • Am I pregnant, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding?
  • Is there any history of elevated mood, which may point to bipolar disorder and change medication choices?

A clear assessment, as described on the page about how depression is diagnosed, helps guide these decisions. Lifestyle supports such as those described on the page about exercise and depression can help alongside either approach. Decisions about medication are made with a physician or psychiatrist, and any changes should be made with their guidance.

Common Questions About Medication and Therapy for Depression

Is therapy as effective as antidepressants for depression?

For many people with moderate depression, therapy such as CBT and antidepressants produce similar average improvements.

When is combining medication and therapy best for depression?

Combining medication and therapy is often recommended for moderate to severe depression, recurring depression, or depression with significant anxiety.

Does therapy protect against depression coming back?

Skills learned in therapy often continue to lower the risk of depression returning after sessions end.

Why might I need to stay on antidepressants after I feel better?

Prescribers often recommend continuing antidepressants for a period after recovery, since stopping too early can increase depression relapse risk.

Which works faster for depression, medication or therapy?

Both usually take several weeks. Some people notice sleep or energy changes from depression medication before mood lifts.

What if I have no energy for therapy while depressed?

When depression drains energy, medication may reduce symptoms enough to make therapy more manageable. Starting with shorter goals also helps.

Should I try therapy first for mild depression?

For mild depression, therapy and lifestyle changes are often recommended first, with medication considered if symptoms persist.

What side effects should I ask about before starting an antidepressant?

Ask about stomach upset, sleep changes, and sexual side effects, which vary by person and often ease or can be managed with your prescriber.

Why does my doctor ask about past highs before treating my depression?

Past periods of elevated mood may point to bipolar disorder, which changes how depression medication is chosen.

Can I switch from medication to therapy for depression later?

Many people change their depression treatment over time. Any medication changes should be planned with your prescriber.
