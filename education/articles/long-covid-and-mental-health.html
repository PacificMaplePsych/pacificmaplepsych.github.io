Long COVID and Mental Health

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Long COVID, also called post-COVID-19 condition, refers to symptoms that continue or develop months after a COVID-19 infection, often lasting three months or longer. Common symptoms include fatigue, brain fog, shortness of breath, sleep problems, and post-exertional malaise, a worsening of symptoms after physical or mental effort. Living with long COVID can be very hard on mental health. Many people experience anxiety, depression, grief over lost abilities, frustration with uncertainty, and stress from changes to work and relationships. Long COVID is a physical condition, not a psychological one, but psychological support can help people cope, pace activities, and protect their wellbeing alongside medical care.

How Long COVID Affects Mental Health

  • Grief and loss for previous energy, abilities, and plans
  • Anxiety about symptoms, relapses, and the future
  • Depression related to isolation, pain, and limitations
  • Identity changes when work, roles, or activities change
  • Feeling dismissed when symptoms are not understood or believed
  • Sleep problems that worsen fatigue and mood
  • Cognitive difficulties such as brain fog that affect confidence

Why Mental Health and Long COVID Interact

Ongoing physical symptoms are stressful, and stress can make symptoms like fatigue and poor sleep harder to manage. Some research also suggests that the illness itself may affect the brain and nervous system. Mood changes are not a sign that symptoms are imagined. They are understandable responses to a difficult, often unpredictable condition, as described on the page about coping psychologically with chronic illness.

What It Is Not

Long COVID is not "all in your head," and psychological support does not imply that it is. Therapy is not a cure for long COVID. It is one part of care that helps with coping and quality of life. Pushing through fatigue is also not recommended for people with post-exertional malaise, because it can worsen symptoms.

What Helps

Medical Care

A family doctor can help manage symptoms, rule out other causes, and connect you with specialized services where available.

Pacing

Planning activities within your energy limits, resting before you crash, and gradually adjusting can reduce flare-ups.

Psychological Support

  • Coping with uncertainty and grief
  • Managing anxiety and low mood
  • Adjusting goals and identity
  • Sleep support, such as approaches adapted from CBT for insomnia
  • Communicating needs to family, friends, and employers

Connection

Peer support groups and understanding relationships can reduce isolation.

Work Adjustments

Accommodations or time off may be needed, as described on the page about taking a mental health leave from work in BC.

Living With Uncertainty

One of the hardest parts of long COVID is not knowing how long symptoms will last or whether they will fully resolve. Many people find themselves constantly tracking symptoms, comparing good days with bad days, and trying to predict the future. Therapy approaches such as acceptance and commitment therapy can help people make room for uncertainty while still focusing on what matters to them, as described on the page about acceptance and commitment therapy. This does not mean giving up on recovery. It means building a meaningful life alongside symptoms rather than putting life on hold until they disappear. Small goals, flexible plans, and celebrating progress, even when it is uneven, can help protect mood and hope.

When to Seek Support

Consider support if long COVID is affecting your mood, relationships, or sense of self, or if anxiety or depression is making symptoms harder to manage. A therapist experienced with chronic illness can help you find ways to live as fully as possible. The page about psychological approaches to chronic pain describes related strategies.

Common Questions About Long COVID and Mental Health

What is long COVID?

Long COVID, or post-COVID-19 condition, refers to symptoms that continue or develop months after a COVID-19 infection, often lasting three months or longer.

Why are anxiety and depression common with long COVID?

Anxiety and depression are common with long COVID because of ongoing symptoms, uncertainty, and lost abilities, and the illness may also affect the brain and nervous system.

Is long COVID psychological?

No. Long COVID is a physical condition, though it can affect mental health, and psychological support can help with coping.

What is post-exertional malaise?

Post-exertional malaise is a worsening of symptoms after physical or mental effort, common in long COVID.

What can therapy offer someone living with long COVID?

Therapy can help people living with long COVID cope with uncertainty, grief, anxiety, low mood, and changes in identity, as part of overall care.

What is pacing for long COVID?

Pacing means planning activities within your energy limits and resting before you crash, to reduce flare-ups.

Why do I feel grief with long COVID?

Grief with long COVID is common because people lose energy, abilities, roles, and plans they previously had.

Can long COVID cause brain fog?

Yes. Brain fog, including trouble concentrating and remembering, is a common long COVID symptom.

Should I push through fatigue with long COVID?

Pushing through fatigue is not recommended for people with post-exertional malaise, as it can worsen symptoms.

Can I get work accommodations for long COVID?

Yes. People with long COVID may be able to get workplace accommodations or time off, depending on their situation.
