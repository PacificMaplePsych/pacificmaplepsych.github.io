Learning Disabilities in Adults

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Learning disabilities do not go away in adulthood, and many adults have one that was never identified in school. Adults with an undiagnosed learning disability may read slowly and need to reread, avoid writing or rely heavily on spellcheck, struggle with mental math or budgeting, or find studying and certain work tasks unusually exhausting. Many developed clever ways to compensate, which can hide the difficulty but leave them feeling that they work harder than everyone else. Some learn about learning disabilities when their child is assessed, or when returning to school or facing a new job. An adult psychoeducational assessment can clarify strengths and challenges and support accommodations at work or school.

How Learning Disabilities Show Up in Adults

  • Reading. Slow reading, losing your place, rereading for meaning, or avoiding long documents.
  • Writing. Difficulty organizing written work, frequent spelling errors, or anxiety about emails and reports.
  • Math. Difficulty with mental calculations, estimating, budgets, or reading charts.
  • Work. Taking longer than colleagues on certain tasks, or avoiding promotions that involve more reading or writing.
  • Study. Struggling with exams, note-taking, or reading-heavy courses.
  • Emotional effects. Frustration, shame, imposter feelings, or anxiety, as described on the page about feeling like an impostor at work.

Why They Get Missed

Some adults went to school when learning disabilities were less understood. Others were bright enough to keep up in early grades, attended schools with limited assessment resources, or were labelled lazy or careless. Strong verbal skills or hard work can mask difficulties, especially in people who are also gifted, as described on the page about twice-exceptionality. ADHD and anxiety can also overlap and blur the picture.

What It Is Not

A learning disability in adulthood is not a sign of low intelligence, and it does not mean you failed to try hard enough. Not every difficulty with reading or writing is a learning disability either. Lack of opportunity, attention difficulties, depression, or learning in a second language can affect skills. An assessment helps sort out what is going on.

How an Adult Assessment Works

An adult psychoeducational assessment usually includes an interview about your education, work, and history, cognitive and academic testing, and questionnaires about attention and wellbeing. School records, if available, can help. The assessment identifies whether a specific learning disorder is present, describes strengths, and provides recommendations, as described on the page about what a specific learning disorder is. Because ADHD and learning disabilities often occur together, some adults are assessed for both, as described on the page about how ADHD is diagnosed in adults.

How a Diagnosis Can Help

  • Understanding yourself. Many adults feel relief and less self-blame.
  • Accommodations at school. Post-secondary institutions provide accommodations with documentation, as described on the page about academic accommodations at college or university in BC.
  • Accommodations at work. Assistive technology, written instructions, or extra time for certain tasks.
  • Strategies. Text-to-speech, speech-to-text, audiobooks, and calculators can reduce barriers.
  • Building on strengths. Choosing work and study paths that suit how you learn.

Making Sense of the Past

For many adults, a diagnosis of a learning disability prompts reflection on earlier experiences, such as struggling in school, being labelled lazy or careless, or avoiding certain subjects or careers. This can bring a mix of relief, sadness, and frustration about what might have been different with earlier support. These feelings are normal. Some people find it helpful to talk them through with a therapist, especially if past experiences have left a lasting sense of shame or self-doubt. A diagnosis can also be an opportunity to approach new goals with better tools and a clearer understanding of how you learn best.

Common Questions About Learning Disabilities in Adults

Do learning disabilities go away in adulthood?

No. Learning disabilities continue into adulthood, and many adults have one that was never identified in school.

What are signs of a learning disability in adults?

Signs of a learning disability in adults include slow reading, avoiding writing, frequent spelling errors, difficulty with mental math, and exhaustion with certain tasks.

Why are some learning disabilities not diagnosed until adulthood?

Learning disabilities may be missed because of limited assessment in school, strong compensation, giftedness, or being labelled lazy or careless.

Is it worth getting assessed for a learning disability as an adult?

Yes. An adult assessment can bring self-understanding and support accommodations at work or school.

How is a learning disability diagnosed in adults?

A learning disability in adults is diagnosed through a psychoeducational assessment with interviews, cognitive and academic testing, and history.

Can an adult have both ADHD and a learning disability?

Yes. ADHD and learning disabilities often occur together, and some adults are assessed for both.

Can adults get accommodations for a learning disability at university?

Yes. Post-secondary institutions provide accommodations for learning disabilities with appropriate documentation.

What workplace accommodations help adults with learning disabilities?

Workplace accommodations for learning disabilities include assistive technology, written instructions, and extra time for certain tasks.

Does a learning disability mean low intelligence in adults?

No. Adults with learning disabilities often have average or above-average intelligence.

What tools help adults with dyslexia?

Text-to-speech, speech-to-text, audiobooks, spellcheck, and reading apps can help adults with dyslexia.
