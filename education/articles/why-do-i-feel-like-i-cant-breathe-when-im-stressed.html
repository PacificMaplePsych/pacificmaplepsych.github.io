Why Do I Feel Like I Can't Breathe When I'm Stressed

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Your chest feels tight. You try to take a deep breath, but it does not seem to reach the bottom of your lungs, so you try again, and again. It can feel as if the air is not getting in properly, which is understandably alarming. For many people, this happens during stressful moments, busy workdays, or arguments, and sometimes it seems to appear out of nowhere.

Feeling short of breath is a symptom that deserves to be taken seriously. It can have physical causes that need medical care. It is also one of the most common and most frightening ways anxiety shows up in the body.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Lung conditions. Asthma, including exercise-induced and stress-triggered asthma, can cause wheezing, coughing, and tightness. Allergies and respiratory infections can also make breathing harder for a while.

Heart and blood conditions. Heart problems, anemia, and some other medical conditions can cause breathlessness, especially with exertion.

Reflux. Acid reflux can cause chest discomfort and a feeling of something stuck in the throat, which some people experience as difficulty breathing.

After an illness. Some people notice lingering breathlessness and fatigue for weeks or months after a viral illness.

Fitness and posture. Being less active than usual, or spending long hours hunched over a desk, can make breathing feel shallow and tight, particularly through the chest and shoulders.

If you have not had new or ongoing breathing symptoms checked, please see a doctor. And if you ever experience severe shortness of breath, chest pain, blue or grey lips, confusion, or fainting, call 911 right away.

When It Looks More Like Anxiety

When medical causes have been ruled out, or when breathlessness reliably shows up with stress, anxiety is often the key factor. What surprises most people is that anxious breathlessness usually does not involve too little air. It more often involves breathing too much.

During stress, the body shifts into fight or flight mode. Breathing becomes faster and moves up into the chest, and people often start gulping or sighing. This overbreathing, sometimes called hyperventilation, lowers the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. Lower carbon dioxide causes tingling in the hands and lips, lightheadedness, and a sense of air hunger, the feeling that you cannot get a satisfying breath. That feeling prompts more gulping, which keeps the cycle going.

Other signs that anxiety is involved include:

  • Frequent sighing or yawning, or a strong urge to take one big, deep breath.
  • Breathlessness that eases when you are distracted or absorbed in something.
  • Symptoms that come with worry, a racing heart, or a feeling of panic.
  • Normal medical tests, including oxygen levels, despite the uncomfortable sensations.

Fear adds another layer. If you believe the sensation means you are suffocating, your body responds with more adrenaline, which speeds up breathing further. In some people this loop quickly builds into a panic attack.

What Can Help

  • Slow down rather than breathe deeper. It is natural to try to pull in more air, but that usually makes overbreathing worse. Instead, breathe in gently through your nose and let the exhale be slow and long.
  • Breathe low. Place a hand on your belly and let it rise and fall, keeping your shoulders relaxed. Practising this daily when you are calm makes it easier to use when you are stressed.
  • Loosen your upper body. Rolling your shoulders, stretching your chest, and unclenching your jaw can ease the physical tightness that makes breathing feel restricted.
  • Remind yourself what is happening. Knowing that air hunger from anxiety is uncomfortable but not dangerous can take much of the fear out of the sensation.
  • Stay active. Regular movement builds confidence that your lungs and body can handle harder breathing.

Common Questions About Stress and Breathlessness

Am I actually getting enough oxygen when anxiety makes breathing feel hard?

In most cases, yes. Anxiety-related breathlessness usually happens alongside normal oxygen levels. The uncomfortable feeling of not getting enough air is more often caused by breathing too quickly or too shallowly, which disrupts carbon dioxide balance rather than oxygen supply.

What is hyperventilation and why does it cause tingling?

Hyperventilation means breathing faster or more deeply than the body needs. It lowers carbon dioxide in the blood, which affects how nerves and blood vessels behave, leading to tingling in the fingers and lips, lightheadedness, and sometimes muscle tightness.

How can I tell the difference between asthma and anxiety-related breathlessness?

Asthma often involves wheezing, coughing, and symptoms triggered by exercise, cold air, or allergens, and it usually improves with asthma medication. Anxiety-related breathlessness tends to involve sighing and air hunger without wheezing. The two can occur together, so a medical assessment is the best way to know.

Why do I keep feeling the need to take a big, deep breath or yawn?

Frequent sighing and yawning are common signs of a stressed breathing pattern. Each big breath can briefly feel satisfying, but repeated deep breaths can actually maintain the imbalance that causes air hunger in the first place.

Does breathing into a paper bag help?

This approach is no longer generally recommended, because it can be unsafe if the breathlessness has a medical cause and it does not teach a lasting skill. Slowing the breath and lengthening the exhale is a safer and more effective way to settle overbreathing.

When is shortness of breath an emergency?

Call 911 if breathlessness is severe or sudden, or comes with chest pain, blue or grey lips, confusion, fainting, or difficulty speaking in full sentences. These signs need immediate medical attention regardless of whether anxiety might also be involved.

Can tight chest muscles make breathing feel restricted?

Yes. Stress often leads people to tense their shoulders, chest, and upper back, and to breathe mainly with those muscles. That tension can make each breath feel shallow and effortful, which adds to the sense that breathing is difficult.

Why does trying to control my breathing sometimes make it worse?

Focusing anxiously on your breath can make you more aware of every small sensation and lead to forcing deeper breaths. Gentle, slow breathing practised regularly when you are calm tends to work better than trying hard to fix your breathing in the middle of stress.

Can anxiety-related breathlessness happen at night?

It can. Some people notice it when lying down to sleep, when the mind finally has space to worry, or when waking suddenly from a stressful dream. Night-time breathing problems can also have medical causes such as sleep apnea, so it is worth mentioning to a doctor.

Can exercise help with feeling short of breath from stress?

Regular physical activity strengthens breathing muscles and helps the body get used to faster breathing in a safe context. That familiarity can make stress-related breathlessness feel less threatening, and it has broader benefits for anxiety too.
