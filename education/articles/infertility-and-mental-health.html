Infertility and Mental Health

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If the strain of infertility has led to thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. In British Columbia, you can also call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse.

Infertility is usually defined as not becoming pregnant after a year of trying, or six months for people over 35, or being unable to carry a pregnancy to term. It affects many individuals and couples, and its emotional impact can be profound. Infertility often involves repeated cycles of hope and disappointment, medical procedures, financial strain, and uncertainty about the future. Research has found that the psychological distress associated with infertility can be comparable to that of other major health conditions. These feelings are understandable responses to a very difficult experience, and support can make a real difference.

How Infertility Can Affect Mental Health

  • Grief each month or after each unsuccessful treatment cycle.
  • Anxiety during treatment and while waiting for results.
  • Low mood, hopelessness, or depression.
  • Envy or pain around pregnancy announcements, baby showers, or children.
  • Feeling isolated or misunderstood by friends and family.
  • Guilt, shame, or feeling that your body has failed you.
  • Strain on the relationship, including differences in coping styles.
  • Loss of control over life plans and identity.
  • Financial stress from treatment costs.

Many of these experiences are explored on the page about struggling with infertility.

Why It Happens

Infertility often touches deeply held hopes about family, identity, and the future. Each month or treatment cycle can bring anticipation followed by loss, creating a repeated grieving process. Fertility treatments involve hormones, procedures, and waiting periods that can affect mood and increase stress. The process can dominate daily life, affecting work, finances, and relationships. Many people keep infertility private, which can lead to isolation. Because others may not see it as a loss, infertility is often a form of disenfranchised grief.

Pregnancy loss, including miscarriage, can be part of the infertility journey and adds further grief, as described on the page about grief after pregnancy loss. Couples may cope differently, with one partner wanting to talk and the other preferring to focus on practical steps, which can create distance. People without partners, and those building families through donors or surrogacy, may face additional challenges and decisions.

Decisions about when to continue, change, or stop treatment can be especially hard. Some people move toward adoption, fostering, or living without children, each of which involves its own emotions and adjustments. There is no single right path, and support can help people make choices that fit their values.

The two-week wait between procedures and results is often described as one of the hardest parts of treatment. Planning distracting activities, limiting symptom checking, and leaning on support can make this period more manageable.

What It Is Not

  • Not your fault. Infertility is a medical condition with many causes.
  • Not caused by stress, though stress can make the experience harder to cope with.
  • Not something you should just stay positive about. Grief and frustration are valid.
  • Not a measure of your worth. Your value as a person is not defined by fertility.

How It Is Supported

Therapy can help people process grief, manage anxiety and depression, cope with treatment decisions, and navigate relationship strain. Cognitive behavioural therapy and mindfulness-based approaches can reduce distress during treatment. Couples therapy can help partners understand each other's coping styles and stay connected. Support groups, including those offered by fertility clinics and community organizations, connect people with others who understand. Setting boundaries around social events and conversations, limiting time spent researching, and making space for non-fertility-related activities can protect wellbeing. Fertility counselling is often available through clinics.

Common Questions About Infertility and Mental Health

How common is emotional distress with infertility?

Very common. Research shows infertility can cause distress comparable to other major health conditions.

Why does each negative pregnancy test feel like a loss?

Each cycle brings hope, so a negative result can bring grief, and repeated losses add up over time.

Why do I feel so up and down during a treatment cycle?

Hormones, procedures, and waiting periods can increase anxiety, irritability, and low mood.

Does stress cause infertility?

Stress is not considered a main cause of infertility, but it can make the experience harder to cope with.

Is it wrong to feel envy when friends announce a pregnancy?

No. Announcements can highlight your own loss and longing, and envy or pain is a common, understandable response.

How can couples cope with infertility together?

Talk openly about feelings and coping styles, share decisions, and consider couples therapy if distance grows.

Is infertility a form of grief?

Yes. Infertility involves grieving hoped-for pregnancies, children, and plans, often without social recognition.

How can I set boundaries around baby-related events?

It is okay to decline or limit attendance, and to share only what you are comfortable with.

Which approaches ease the emotional strain of fertility treatment?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness-based approaches, grief counselling, and couples therapy can all help.

How do people decide when to stop fertility treatment?

It is a personal decision based on health, finances, emotional wellbeing, and values. Counselling can help clarify options.
