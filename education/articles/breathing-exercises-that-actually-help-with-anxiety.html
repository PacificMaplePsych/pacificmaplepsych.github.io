Breathing Exercises That Actually Help With Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Breathing exercises that help with anxiety usually involve breathing slowly and gently, with a longer exhale than inhale, often around five to six breaths per minute. Techniques such as extended exhale breathing, belly breathing, and box breathing can calm the body's stress response within a few minutes. The key is slow and gentle, not deep and forceful. Big gulps of air can actually make anxiety symptoms such as dizziness and tingling worse. Breathing exercises work best when practised regularly, not only in moments of panic, so the skill is ready when you need it.

Why Breathing Exercises Help

When anxious, many people breathe quickly and shallowly, sometimes without noticing. This can lead to over-breathing, which lowers carbon dioxide in the blood and causes symptoms such as light-headedness, tingling, chest tightness, and a sense of not getting enough air. These sensations can feel frightening and add to anxiety, as described on the page about feeling like you cannot breathe when stressed.

Slow breathing with longer exhales activates the calming branch of the nervous system, slowing heart rate and signalling safety. It also gives the mind a simple focus, which can interrupt worry. Breathing is one of the few parts of the stress response people can change directly, which is why it is a core tool in many therapies. It fits alongside other body-based skills described on the page about nervous system regulation.

Strategies to Try

1. Extended Exhale Breathing

Breathe in gently through your nose for about four counts, then out slowly through your mouth or nose for about six counts. Continue for two to five minutes. The longer exhale is what calms the body.

2. Belly Breathing

Place one hand on your chest and one on your belly. Breathe in so that your belly rises more than your chest, then breathe out slowly. This encourages slower, fuller breaths.

3. Box Breathing

Breathe in for four counts, hold gently for four, breathe out for four, and pause for four. If holding feels uncomfortable, skip the holds or shorten them.

4. Paced Breathing With an App or Visual

Follow a visual guide or app that expands and contracts at a slow pace, around five to six breaths per minute.

5. Sigh It Out

Take a normal breath in, a small extra sip of air, then a long, slow sigh out. Repeating this a few times can release tension quickly.

6. Breathing Plus Grounding

Combine slow breathing with noticing your surroundings or feeling your feet on the floor, as described on the page about grounding techniques.

7. Breathing Plus Muscle Release

Release tension in your shoulders and jaw on each exhale. This pairs well with progressive muscle relaxation.

Common Obstacles

Many people take deep, fast breaths when told to breathe deeply, which can increase dizziness. Focus on slow and gentle rather than big. Some people find that paying attention to their breathing makes them more anxious, especially those who fear breathing sensations. Starting with short practice, keeping your eyes open, or focusing on counting rather than sensations can help. Others try breathing only during intense panic and feel it does not work. Practising daily when calm makes it more effective in hard moments.

Breathing exercises can become a way of trying to control or eliminate anxiety completely, which can backfire. They work best as a way to ride out anxiety, not to make it disappear instantly. In panic disorder, therapy often focuses on reducing fear of sensations rather than relying only on breathing, as explained on the page about panic attacks and panic disorder.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If anxiety or panic attacks are frequent, lead you to avoid situations, or interfere with daily life, therapy can help address the causes. Cognitive behavioural therapy is highly effective for anxiety and panic. If you have chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or symptoms that feel new or different, seek medical care or call 911, since some medical conditions can resemble anxiety.

Common Questions About Breathing Exercises for Anxiety

Which breathing exercise is best for anxiety?

Slow breathing with a longer exhale, around five to six breaths per minute, is one of the most effective breathing exercises for anxiety.

Why does a longer exhale calm anxiety?

A longer exhale activates the calming branch of the nervous system, slowing heart rate and signalling safety to the body.

Why do big gulps of air leave me dizzy when I try to calm my anxiety?

Big, fast breaths can cause over-breathing, which may increase dizziness and tingling. Slow, gentle breathing with a longer exhale works better for anxiety.

How do I do box breathing?

Breathe in for four counts, hold for four, breathe out for four, and pause for four. Skip the holds if they feel uncomfortable.

How long should I practise breathing exercises for anxiety?

Two to five minutes of slow breathing can help, and daily practice when calm makes breathing more effective during anxiety.

Why do I feel like I cannot get enough air when I am anxious?

Anxious, fast breathing can lower carbon dioxide and cause air hunger, chest tightness, and tingling, even though you are breathing enough.

How can I practise calm breathing if breath sensations themselves scare me?

Start with short breathing practice, keep your eyes open, or count rather than focusing on breathing sensations.

Do breathing exercises help during a panic attack?

Slow breathing can help you ride out a panic attack, though therapy for panic also focuses on reducing fear of the sensations.

How does a double inhale followed by a long sigh calm anxious breathing?

This pattern, sometimes called a physiological sigh, is a normal breath in, a small extra breath, then a long, slow exhale, which can quickly release tension.

When is breathlessness a reason to see a doctor instead of doing breathing exercises?

Seek medical care or call 911 for chest pain, severe shortness of breath, fainting, or breathing symptoms that feel new or different.
