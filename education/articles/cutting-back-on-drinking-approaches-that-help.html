Cutting Back on Drinking: Approaches That Help

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you drink heavily every day, stopping suddenly can cause dangerous withdrawal, including seizures. Talk to a doctor before cutting back, or call 8-1-1 in British Columbia for advice. If you have severe symptoms such as confusion, hallucinations, or seizures, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Cutting back on drinking usually works best with a clear goal, a plan for tracking and limiting drinks, strategies for triggers and cravings, and support from others or a professional. Some people want to drink less, while others decide to stop. Both are valid goals. Reducing alcohol can improve sleep, mood, energy, health, and relationships. Canada's guidance on alcohol and health suggests that risks increase with each additional drink, and that drinking less is better for health. Approaches such as motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioural therapy, and medication prescribed by a physician can help. Change often takes practice, and setbacks are part of the process.

Why Cutting Back Helps

Alcohol affects the brain and body in many ways. It may help people relax in the short term, but it disrupts sleep, can worsen anxiety and depression, and increases health risks. The link between sleep and mood is explained on the page about sleep and mental health. Many people drink to cope with stress, social anxiety, loneliness, or difficult emotions. Over time, the brain can come to rely on alcohol, making it harder to cope without it.

Cutting back gives the body and brain a chance to recover. Many people notice better sleep, clearer thinking, improved mood, and more energy within weeks. Understanding why you drink helps you choose strategies that address the underlying needs.

Strategies to Try

1. Set a Clear Goal

Decide whether you want to reduce or stop, and be specific, such as no more than a certain number of drinks per week, or alcohol-free days.

2. Track Your Drinking

Record how much and when you drink for a couple of weeks. This often reveals patterns and triggers.

3. Know Your Triggers

Notice situations, feelings, and times that lead to drinking, such as stress after work, social events, or boredom.

4. Plan Alternatives

Have other options ready for common triggers, such as a walk after work, a non-alcoholic drink, or calling a friend. The page about stress management techniques offers ideas.

5. Change Your Environment

Keep less alcohol at home, choose alcohol-free social activities, or let others know about your goal.

6. Pace Your Drinks

If you choose to drink, alternate with water, sip slowly, eat beforehand, and choose lower-alcohol options.

7. Ride Out Cravings

Cravings often peak and pass within minutes. Distracting yourself, or noticing the craving without acting on it, can help.

8. Plan for High-Risk Situations

Before events, decide how many drinks you will have, or practise saying "no thanks."

9. Find Support

Talk to supportive friends or family, join a peer support group, or use online programs.

Common Obstacles

Social pressure can make cutting back hard, especially in settings where drinking is expected. Having a response ready, bringing your own non-alcoholic drink, or leaving early can help. Stress, anxiety, or low mood can increase cravings, and finding other ways to cope is important. Some people slip back into old patterns after a hard day or a celebration. A lapse is not a failure. Reviewing what happened and adjusting the plan helps, as described on the page about relapse prevention planning.

Physical dependence can make stopping difficult and risky. Withdrawal symptoms such as shaking, sweating, anxiety, or trouble sleeping are signs to talk with a doctor before continuing. Shame can also get in the way of seeking help. Many people struggle with alcohol, and support is available without judgment.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If you find it hard to cut back despite trying, drink more than intended, experience withdrawal, or notice drinking affecting your health, work, or relationships, professional support can help. Therapy approaches such as motivational interviewing and cognitive behavioural therapy are well supported. A physician can discuss medications that reduce cravings and can supervise withdrawal safely. In BC, health authorities offer substance use services, and a family doctor or 8-1-1 can help you find them.

Common Questions About Cutting Back on Alcohol

How do I start cutting back on drinking?

Set a clear goal, track your drinks, notice triggers, and plan alternatives for common drinking situations.

I drink every night. Can I quit cold turkey on my own this weekend?

If you drink heavily every day, stopping suddenly can be dangerous. Talk to a doctor first, who can plan a safe way to stop.

What are signs of alcohol withdrawal?

Shaking, sweating, anxiety, trouble sleeping, and in severe cases confusion or seizures. Seek medical help if these occur.

Does drinking less improve sleep?

Yes. Alcohol disrupts sleep, and many people notice better sleep within weeks of cutting back.

What does Canadian guidance say about alcohol and health?

Canada's guidance suggests risks increase with each additional drink and that drinking less is better for health.

How can I handle social pressure to drink?

Have a response ready, bring a non-alcoholic drink, choose alcohol-free activities, or leave early.

What should I do if I drink more than I planned?

Treat it as a chance to learn. Review what happened, adjust your plan, and keep going.

Can therapy help me drink less?

Yes. Motivational interviewing and CBT can help you understand patterns, manage triggers, and build new habits.

Are there medications that help with cutting back?

A physician can discuss medications that reduce cravings and support changes in drinking.

Could my regular wine with dinner be part of why my worry has been rising?

It could. Alcohol affects brain chemistry and sleep, which can increase anxiety and lower mood as it wears off, even at moderate amounts.
