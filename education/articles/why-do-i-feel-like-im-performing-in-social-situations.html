Why Do I Feel Like I'm Performing in Social Situations

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You walk into a social event and something switches on. You watch your facial expressions, choose your words carefully, laugh at the right moments, and mirror how others are acting. People might see you as friendly or outgoing, but inside it feels like a performance. Afterward, you are completely drained, and you may feel like no one really knows the real you.

Most people adjust how they act depending on the setting. When social situations feel like constant acting, and the effort leaves you exhausted, it is worth understanding what is driving it.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Social anxiety. Fear of being judged can lead you to monitor yourself closely and try to act in ways that avoid criticism. This often goes along with replaying conversations afterward.

People-pleasing. If you learned that being liked depends on keeping others happy, you may shape yourself around what others want, much like struggling to say no.

Low self-esteem. Believing your real self is not good enough can make performing feel safer, especially if you feel like an impostor in other areas too.

Trauma or difficult early relationships. Growing up needing to read and manage others' moods can make performing feel like second nature.

ADHD. Some adults with ADHD work hard to hide impulsivity or distractibility, which can make socializing feel effortful.

When It Looks More Like Autistic Masking

Many autistic people learn to mask, or camouflage, their natural ways of communicating and behaving so they fit in. Masking might mean forcing eye contact, suppressing movements, copying others' expressions, preparing scripts, or hiding strong interests. It often starts in childhood and can become so automatic that people do not realize they are doing it.

Signs that autistic masking may be involved:

  • You study and copy how others act, sometimes adopting different personalities with different groups.
  • You rehearse conversations or prepare topics before social events.
  • You force eye contact or expressions that do not come naturally.
  • You feel extremely drained after socializing, even with people you like.
  • You also relate to sensory sensitivities, a need for routine, or deep interests.
  • You were diagnosed late or not at all, and may have been told you seem fine.

Masking can help people get by, but long-term masking is linked with exhaustion, anxiety, depression, and a lost sense of identity. Many autistic adults, particularly women, are identified later in life partly because masking hid their differences.

What Can Help

  • Notice when you perform. Awareness of when and why you shift helps you make choices about it.
  • Find safe spaces. Spend time with people and places where you can relax and be yourself.
  • Plan recovery time. Schedule rest after social events to recharge.
  • Experiment with small changes. Try dropping one performance behaviour at a time in low-stakes settings.
  • Connect with others. Communities of people with similar experiences can ease isolation.

Common Questions About Feeling Like You're Performing Socially

Why do I feel like I'm acting around other people?

Feeling like you are performing socially can come from social anxiety, people-pleasing, low self-esteem, past experiences, ADHD, or autistic masking. Understanding the cause can help you find the right support.

What is autistic masking?

Autistic masking, or camouflaging, is hiding natural autistic traits to fit in, such as forcing eye contact, copying expressions, suppressing movements, or following social scripts.

Is masking only something autistic people do?

Many people adjust how they act in different settings. Masking in the autism sense tends to be more constant, effortful, and draining, and involves hiding core ways of being.

Why am I so tired after socializing even with friends?

Social exhaustion can happen with introversion, social anxiety, or masking. When socializing requires constant self-monitoring or performing, it uses a lot of mental energy.

Can masking lead to burnout?

Yes. Long-term masking is linked with autistic burnout, a state of deep exhaustion, reduced ability to cope, and sometimes loss of skills. Reducing masking and increasing rest can help recovery.

Why do I change my personality depending on who I'm with?

Some shifting is normal. When it feels like becoming a different person with each group, it may come from people-pleasing, social anxiety, or autistic masking.

Why do autistic women often get diagnosed later?

Autistic women and girls are often diagnosed later partly because they may mask more, and their traits may not match stereotypes of autism. Many are identified in adulthood.

How can I stop feeling like I'm performing socially?

Notice when you perform, spend time in safe spaces, try small changes in low-stakes settings, and seek support for underlying causes like anxiety or autism.

Does social anxiety make people feel fake in conversations?

It can. Social anxiety leads to intense self-monitoring and efforts to avoid judgement, which can make interactions feel scripted or like you are hiding your real self.

Can therapy help with masking and feeling like I'm performing?

Yes. Therapy can help you understand why you perform, reduce anxiety, build self-acceptance, and find ways to be more authentic while staying safe and connected.
