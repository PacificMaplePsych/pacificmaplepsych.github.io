What Happens After Your Child Is Diagnosed With ADHD

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

After a child is diagnosed with ADHD, the next steps usually include understanding the report, learning about ADHD as a family, starting parent training or parent support, working with your child's school on supports, and talking with a physician about whether medication might help. Canadian guidelines recommend combining approaches rather than relying on one alone. For younger children, behavioural parent training is usually the first step. For school-aged children and teens, a combination of school supports, skills, and sometimes medication often works best. ADHD is a difference in how the brain manages attention and activity, not a sign of poor effort or parenting, and children with ADHD can thrive with the right support.

Step 1: Understand the Report

Your child's assessment report explains the diagnosis, your child's strengths, any co-occurring difficulties such as anxiety or learning challenges, and recommendations. Ask questions at the feedback session, as described on the page about what a feedback session after a psychological assessment is. Keep copies for school and other services.

Step 2: Learn About ADHD Together

Understanding ADHD helps parents respond with patience rather than frustration. Explaining ADHD to your child in age-appropriate, strengths-based language can reduce shame. The page about what ADHD is describes the different presentations.

Step 3: Parent Training and Support

Behavioural parent training teaches strategies such as clear instructions, routines, consistent consequences, and lots of positive attention. It is recommended as a first-line approach, especially for younger children, and helps families reduce conflict. The pages about parenting a child with ADHD and parent coaching explain more.

Step 4: Work With the School

Share the report with your child's school and ask about supports such as:

  • Seating and environment. Sitting near the teacher and away from distractions.
  • Instructions and tasks. Breaking work into smaller steps, with written and verbal instructions.
  • Movement breaks and fidget tools.
  • Extra time for tests and assignments.
  • Regular communication between home and school.

Some students may receive formal plans or designations, depending on their needs and school district processes.

Step 5: Consider Medication With a Physician

Medication can be very effective for reducing ADHD symptoms in school-aged children and teens. It is prescribed by a physician, such as a paediatrician, family doctor, or psychiatrist, who can discuss benefits, side effects, and monitoring. Whether to try medication is a family decision, and it works best alongside other supports.

Step 6: Build Skills and Routines at Home

Visual schedules, timers, checklists, and predictable routines support executive functioning. Regular physical activity, good sleep, and time for interests and strengths all help, as described on the page about why autistic and ADHD children often struggle with sleep.

Step 7: Watch for Emotional Needs

Children with ADHD may struggle with frustration, low self-esteem, anxiety, or rejection sensitivity. Therapy can help with emotional regulation and confidence. Look after your own wellbeing too, as described on the page about staying calm as a parent when you feel overwhelmed.

What a Diagnosis Does Not Mean

An ADHD diagnosis does not limit what your child can achieve. Many people with ADHD are creative, energetic, and passionate. Some parents recognize ADHD traits in themselves after their child's diagnosis, as described on the page about whether to get assessed for ADHD as an adult.

Common Questions After a Child's ADHD Diagnosis

What should I do after my child is diagnosed with ADHD?

After an ADHD diagnosis, understand the report, learn about ADHD, start parent training or support, work with the school, and discuss medication with a physician.

Is medication necessary for ADHD in children?

Medication is not required for every child with ADHD. It can be very effective and works best alongside parent training and school supports.

Should parent training come before medication for young children with ADHD?

For preschool-aged children with ADHD, behavioural parent training is generally recommended as the first step, while older children often benefit from combined approaches.

What school supports help children with ADHD?

School supports for ADHD include preferential seating, smaller task steps, movement breaks, extra time, and regular home-school communication.

What words help a child understand their ADHD diagnosis?

Simple, strengths-based words help, such as explaining that their brain works in a different way that brings both challenges and strengths.

Who prescribes ADHD medication for children?

ADHD medication for children is prescribed by a physician, such as a paediatrician, family doctor, or psychiatrist.

What can therapy add for a child with ADHD besides medication?

Therapy can help a child with ADHD build emotional regulation, confidence, and coping skills, and works alongside parent training and any medication.

Should parents blame themselves after a child's ADHD diagnosis?

No. ADHD is a neurodevelopmental difference, not the result of bad parenting, though parenting strategies can help a child with ADHD thrive.

Should my child's teacher know about their ADHD diagnosis?

Sharing an ADHD diagnosis with your child's teacher can help the school provide supports and work with you.

Can parents have ADHD too?

Yes. ADHD runs in families, and some parents recognize ADHD in themselves after their child is diagnosed.
