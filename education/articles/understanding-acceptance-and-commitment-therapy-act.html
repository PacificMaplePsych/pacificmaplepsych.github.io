Understanding Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT, pronounced like the word act) is an evidence-based therapy that helps people relate differently to painful thoughts and feelings, so they have less power, while taking action toward what matters most. Rather than trying to eliminate or argue with difficult experiences, ACT teaches people to notice them with openness and curiosity, and to choose actions guided by personal values. The goal is psychological flexibility, the ability to stay present and act meaningfully even when life is hard. ACT is part of a family of therapies influenced by behavioural science and mindfulness, and it has research support for many concerns.

How ACT Works

ACT starts from the idea that pain is a normal part of life, but struggling against pain can create more suffering. Trying hard to avoid anxious thoughts, sad feelings, or uncomfortable sensations often backfires, making them stronger or shrinking life as people avoid situations. ACT helps people step out of this struggle.

ACT is built around six core processes:

  • Acceptance. Making room for difficult feelings and sensations, rather than fighting or avoiding them.
  • Cognitive defusion. Seeing thoughts as thoughts, not facts or commands. For example, noticing "I am having the thought that I will fail" instead of believing "I will fail." This can reduce the grip of patterns like rumination.
  • Present-moment awareness. Paying attention to what is happening now, with curiosity.
  • Self-as-context. Recognizing that you are more than your thoughts and feelings, a stable observer who notices them.
  • Values. Clarifying what matters most to you, such as connection, creativity, or kindness.
  • Committed action. Taking steps in line with your values, even when discomfort shows up.

ACT differs from traditional CBT in its focus. CBT often helps people examine and change the content of thoughts, while ACT focuses on changing one's relationship with thoughts. Both can be effective, and some therapists combine them. ACT also shares ideas with compassion-based approaches, such as those described on the page about compassion-focused therapy.

What Sessions Are Like

ACT sessions blend conversation with experiential exercises. A therapist might guide mindfulness practices, use metaphors, or invite you to try exercises that show how thoughts work. For example, you might imagine placing thoughts on leaves floating down a stream, or repeat a difficult word until it loses its meaning. Metaphors, such as struggling in quicksand or being a passenger on a bus driven by your values, help make ideas memorable.

Values work is central. You might explore what matters to you in different areas of life, such as relationships, work, health, and community, and identify small actions to move toward them. Between sessions, you might practise mindfulness, notice how you react to thoughts, or take a values-based step.

ACT is often delivered over around eight to sixteen sessions, though it varies. It can be done individually, in groups, or through workshops and online programs. The pace is collaborative, and therapists adjust exercises to fit your needs.

Who It Can Help

ACT has research support for anxiety, depression, chronic pain, stress, OCD, and substance use, and it is used for many other concerns, including burnout, psychosis, and adjusting to illness. It is particularly well known for helping people living with chronic pain, where it focuses on living well alongside pain rather than only reducing it. It can also help people facing life transitions or uncertainty, and those who want to build a more meaningful life. The main types of anxiety it can help with are described on the page about anxiety disorders.

ACT may be less suitable for people who want a highly structured, skills-focused approach, or who are in acute crisis and need stabilization first. Some people find the ideas abstract at first, though exercises and metaphors often make them clearer. For some conditions, such as OCD or PTSD, specialized treatments may be combined with ACT.

Common Questions About ACT

What does ACT stand for in therapy?

ACT stands for acceptance and commitment therapy and is pronounced like the word act.

What does it look like to stay flexible when my mind keeps telling me I will fail?

It is the ability to stay present, make room for difficult experiences, and act in line with your values.

What is cognitive defusion?

It is a skill for seeing thoughts as thoughts rather than facts, which reduces their power over you.

Will an ACT therapist try to prove my anxious thoughts wrong like my old CBT worksheets did?

Usually not. CBT often focuses on changing thoughts, while ACT focuses on changing your relationship with them.

Does acceptance mean giving up or liking my situation?

No. Acceptance means making room for difficult feelings so you can focus energy on what matters.

What role do values play in ACT?

Values guide action. ACT helps you clarify what matters and take steps toward it, even with discomfort.

Can ACT help with chronic pain?

Yes. ACT is well known for helping people live meaningfully alongside chronic pain.

What kinds of exercises are used in ACT sessions?

Sessions use mindfulness, metaphors, and experiential exercises, such as imagining thoughts floating by on leaves.

How long does ACT usually take?

Many people attend around eight to sixteen sessions, though length varies.

Is ACT effective for anxiety and depression?

Research supports ACT for anxiety and depression, as well as stress, OCD, and substance use.
