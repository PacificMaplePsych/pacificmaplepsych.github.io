What Is Perfectionism

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Perfectionism is a pattern of setting extremely high standards, striving to be flawless, and judging yourself harshly when you do not meet those standards. It often comes with a belief that your worth depends on achievement, appearance, or others' approval. Perfectionism is sometimes seen as a positive trait, and high standards can support good work. But research shows that the self-critical side of perfectionism is linked with anxiety, depression, eating disorders, burnout, and relationship difficulties. Perfectionism is not a diagnosis. It is a way of thinking and behaving that can be understood and changed.

What Perfectionism Looks Like

  • Feeling like you have to be perfect to be acceptable.
  • All-or-nothing thinking, where anything less than perfect feels like failure.
  • Spending excessive time on tasks to get details right.
  • Procrastinating or avoiding tasks out of fear of doing them badly.
  • Harsh self-criticism after mistakes, as described on the page about chronic self-criticism.
  • Difficulty delegating or accepting help.
  • Seeking reassurance or approval from others.
  • Rarely feeling satisfied with achievements.
  • Holding others to very high standards as well.

Researchers describe several types of perfectionism:

  • Self-oriented perfectionism. Demanding perfection from yourself.
  • Socially prescribed perfectionism. Believing others expect you to be perfect and will judge you if you are not. This form is most strongly linked with anxiety and depression.
  • Other-oriented perfectionism. Expecting perfection from other people.

Researchers also distinguish between perfectionistic strivings, which involve high standards and drive, and perfectionistic concerns, which involve fear of mistakes and harsh self-evaluation. Perfectionistic concerns are most closely tied to distress.

Why It Happens

Perfectionism often develops in childhood. Children who receive praise mainly for achievements, face harsh criticism, or feel they must be good to be loved may learn that mistakes are dangerous. Growing up in competitive or unpredictable environments can reinforce the idea that control prevents problems. Temperament, anxiety, and cultural or workplace pressures also play a role. Social media and constant comparison can intensify perfectionistic expectations.

Perfectionism can feel protective, as if meeting high standards will prevent rejection or failure. In practice, it tends to increase stress, anxiety, and avoidance. Perfectionism and anxiety often reinforce each other, as explored on the page about anxiety and perfectionism, and perfectionism is a strong risk factor for burnout. In some people, rigid perfectionism is part of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, described on the page about OCPD and OCD.

Perfectionism can look different across areas of life. Some people are perfectionistic mainly at work, others about appearance, parenting, fitness, or their home. Noticing where perfectionism is strongest can help focus change where it matters most, while allowing more relaxed standards elsewhere.

Perfectionism can also affect health. Long hours, poor sleep, and constant pressure can contribute to headaches, digestive problems, and exhaustion. Paying attention to these signals can be a helpful reminder that sustainable standards protect wellbeing.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as high standards. Healthy striving is flexible and allows for mistakes.
  • Not a guarantee of success. Perfectionism often slows progress and increases procrastination.
  • Not a character flaw. It is a learned pattern that often served a protective purpose.
  • Not something you must fully abandon. The goal is flexible, compassionate standards.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism is well supported. It helps people identify rigid rules, test beliefs about mistakes through behavioural experiments, and practise good enough standards. Self-compassion work reduces harsh self-criticism. Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people act on values rather than fear of failure. Practical steps include setting time limits on tasks, prioritizing, celebrating effort, and deliberately allowing small imperfections. Many people find that as perfectionism loosens, they feel calmer, more productive, and more satisfied.

Common Questions About Perfectionism

What is perfectionism?

Perfectionism is setting extremely high standards and judging yourself harshly when you do not meet them, often tying worth to achievement.

What are the types of perfectionism?

Researchers describe self-oriented, socially prescribed, and other-oriented perfectionism.

Which type of perfectionism is most harmful?

Socially prescribed perfectionism, believing others demand perfection, is most strongly linked with anxiety and depression.

Is perfectionism a good or bad trait?

High standards can be helpful, but self-critical perfectionism is linked with distress, burnout, and avoidance.

Where does perfectionism come from?

It often develops from early experiences of conditional approval, criticism, or pressure, along with temperament.

Is perfectionism a mental illness?

No. It is a pattern of thinking and behaving, though it is linked with several mental health conditions.

How is perfectionism linked with OCPD?

Rigid perfectionism and control are core features of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, though most perfectionists do not have OCPD.

What is good enough in overcoming perfectionism?

Good enough means meeting a reasonable standard and moving on, rather than striving for flawless outcomes.

Do I expect too much of the people around me?

Some perfectionists do. It can lead to high expectations of others, difficulty accepting help, and fear of disappointing people.

How is perfectionism treated?

CBT for perfectionism, self-compassion work, and acceptance-based approaches help people build flexible standards.
