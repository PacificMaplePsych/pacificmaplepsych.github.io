Bedtime Resistance and Night Waking: What Helps

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Bedtime resistance and night waking usually improve with a consistent, calm routine, a predictable bedtime, and a gradual approach to helping children fall asleep more independently. Children often resist bedtime because they want more time with parents, feel worried, are overtired or not tired enough, or have learned that stalling works. Many children who wake at night need the same conditions they had when falling asleep, such as a parent lying beside them. Gentle strategies that slowly reduce parental involvement, combined with reassurance and praise, help most children within a few weeks. If sleep problems are linked to anxiety, neurodevelopmental differences, or medical issues, extra support may be needed.

Why Bedtime Becomes a Struggle

Children are wired to seek closeness with parents, and bedtime means separation. They may also feel anxious about the dark, being alone, or the next day. Inconsistent bedtimes, screens, and late naps can leave children not sleepy enough, while overtired children may become wired and resistant. When stalling, such as asking for water or one more story, sometimes works, children learn to keep trying. None of this is deliberate defiance. It is how children learn.

Strategies That Help

1. Set a Consistent Bedtime and Wake Time

Keep times similar every day, including weekends. Choose a bedtime when your child is genuinely sleepy.

2. Use a Predictable Routine

A 20 to 30 minute routine, such as bath, pyjamas, teeth, story, and lights out, signals that sleep is coming. A picture chart can help younger children.

3. Build in Connection

Spend a few minutes of calm, focused time together during the routine. Children who feel connected often resist less.

4. Plan for Common Requests

Offer water, a bathroom trip, and a favourite comfort item before lights out. Some families use a bedtime pass that can be exchanged for one request.

5. Gradually Reduce Your Presence

If your child needs you to fall asleep, try sitting beside the bed, then moving your chair a little further away every few nights until you are out of the room. This is sometimes called a gradual retreat.

6. Return Calmly and Briefly

If your child gets up or calls out, return them to bed calmly with minimal talk, using the same words each time. Brief check-ins can reassure anxious children.

7. Respond to Night Waking the Same Way

Use the same calm, brief response at night as at bedtime, so your child learns to settle back to sleep.

8. Praise and Reward Progress

Notice efforts the next morning. A sticker chart can motivate young children.

Common Obstacles

Changes often get harder before they get easier, as children test whether the new routine will last. Consistency between caregivers matters. Illness, travel, and developmental changes can cause setbacks. Some parents feel guilty about any approach that involves tears. Gentle, gradual methods let you stay responsive while still making change. If you feel exhausted or overwhelmed, the page about staying calm as a parent when you feel overwhelmed may help.

When to Get More Support

If your child is very fearful at bedtime, has frequent nightmares, or strategies have not helped after several weeks of consistent effort, talk to your family doctor and consider professional support. Anxiety may need targeted help, as described on the page about how to support an anxious child. Sleep is often more complex for autistic children and children with ADHD, as described on the page about why autistic and ADHD children often struggle with sleep. Support for parents, described on the page about parent coaching, can help you build a plan and stick with it.

Common Questions About Bedtime Resistance and Night Waking

Why does my child fight bedtime every night?

Children fight bedtime because they want closeness, feel worried, are not tired enough or overtired, or have learned that stalling sometimes works.

How do I stop my child from getting out of bed?

Return your child to bed calmly and briefly each time, using the same words, and praise them in the morning for staying in bed.

What is the gradual retreat method for sleep?

The gradual retreat method involves sitting beside your child's bed and moving further away every few nights until you are out of the room.

Why does my child wake up at night and need me?

Children often wake at night and need a parent because they fell asleep with that parent present and need the same help to settle again.

How long does it take to fix bedtime problems?

With consistent strategies, many bedtime problems improve within one to three weeks, though they may get harder before they get better.

What is a bedtime pass?

A bedtime pass is a card a child can exchange for one request after lights out, such as a hug or a drink, which reduces stalling.

Should my child have a bedtime routine?

Yes. A consistent 20 to 30 minute bedtime routine signals that sleep is coming and reduces bedtime resistance.

Can anxiety cause bedtime resistance?

Yes. Anxiety about separation, the dark, or the next day can cause bedtime resistance and night waking.

Do I have to let my child cry it out to fix bedtime problems?

No. Gentle, gradual approaches let you stay responsive while still helping your child learn to fall asleep more independently.

When should I get help with my child's bedtime problems?

Get help if bedtime problems continue after weeks of consistent effort, involve strong fear, or affect your child's mood or your wellbeing.
