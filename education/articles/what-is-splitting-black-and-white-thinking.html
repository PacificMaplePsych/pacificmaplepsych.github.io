What Is Splitting (Black-and-White Thinking)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Splitting, also called black-and-white or all-or-nothing thinking, is a pattern of seeing people, oneself, or situations in extremes. Someone may be viewed as completely wonderful one moment and completely bad the next, with little room for the middle ground where most people and experiences actually sit. Everyone thinks in extremes at times, especially under stress. Splitting becomes a concern when it happens often, shifts suddenly, and causes painful swings in relationships and self-image. It is commonly associated with borderline personality disorder, but it can also appear with trauma, depression, and anxiety.

What Splitting Looks Like

  • Idealizing a new partner or friend, then seeing them as entirely bad after a disappointment.
  • Feeling that a single mistake means you are a complete failure.
  • Shifting quickly from loving a job to hating it.
  • Remembering only the negatives about someone during conflict.
  • Difficulty holding mixed feelings, such as loving someone who has hurt you.
  • Using words like always, never, perfect, or ruined.
  • Ending relationships abruptly after a perceived slight.

During a split, the current view can feel completely true. Later, the person may feel confused or regretful about how strongly they reacted, especially if the relationship was damaged.

Why It Happens

Splitting is often understood as a way the mind protects itself from overwhelming emotions. Holding contradictory feelings about someone important can be very painful, especially when there is fear of rejection or abandonment. Dividing people into all good or all bad simplifies that pain, even though it creates new problems. In young children, black-and-white thinking is a normal stage of development. With consistent, safe caregiving, children learn to see people as a mix of good and bad qualities.

When early environments were chaotic, invalidating, or traumatic, this integration may not fully develop. Intense emotions, a sensitive nervous system, and fear of abandonment can then trigger splitting under stress. Splitting is also linked with feeling rejected intensely by small comments, since a small slight can instantly shift how someone is seen. Similar thinking appears in depression, where people may view themselves or the future as entirely negative.

Splitting can also affect how people see themselves. A good day at work may bring a sense of being capable and worthy, while a small setback the next day can bring a sense of being worthless. These rapid shifts in self-image can be exhausting and make it hard to feel steady. Learning to notice and name these swings is an important early step.

What It Is Not

  • Not deliberate manipulation. Splitting is usually an automatic emotional response, not a calculated tactic.
  • Not only found in borderline personality disorder. Many people think in extremes under stress.
  • Not a sign that someone cannot change. Skills and therapy can help people hold more balanced views.
  • Not the same as having clear boundaries. Deciding a relationship is unhealthy after careful thought differs from a sudden switch to all bad.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will explore patterns in relationships and self-image, emotional intensity, and any history of trauma, and will consider whether a personality disorder, mood disorder, or trauma-related condition is present. Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) is particularly helpful. The word dialectical itself refers to holding two opposing truths at once. DBT teaches mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal skills. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps people notice extreme thoughts and look for evidence in between. Mentalization-based and schema therapies can build the ability to understand others' minds and hold mixed feelings.

Simple practices can also help, such as pausing before acting on a strong reaction, writing down both positive and negative qualities of a person, and replacing always and never with sometimes.

Common Questions About Splitting

Why is splitting also called all-or-nothing thinking?

Because it sorts people, yourself, or events into opposite extremes, such as perfect or ruined, with little room for the middle ground.

Do people without a personality disorder ever split?

Yes. It is common in borderline personality disorder but can also appear with trauma, depression, anxiety, and ordinary stress.

Why do I suddenly see someone I love as all bad?

Strong emotions, such as hurt or fear of rejection, can trigger splitting, making the current negative view feel like the whole truth.

Is splitting a form of manipulation?

Usually not. Splitting is typically an automatic emotional response rather than a deliberate attempt to control others.

What happens to friendships when someone swings between idealizing and devaluing?

It can lead to idealizing and devaluing partners or friends, sudden breakups, intense conflict, and difficulty repairing after disagreements.

What does the dialectical part of DBT teach about extremes?

It teaches that two opposite things can be true at once, such as loving someone and being hurt by them, and builds skills in mindfulness, emotion regulation, and relationships.

What can I do when I notice myself splitting?

Pause before acting, name the emotion, list both good and bad qualities, and wait until you feel calmer to make decisions.

Can splitting be directed at myself?

Yes. People may see themselves as entirely good after success and entirely worthless after a mistake.

What should a friend do when they suddenly become the bad one?

Stay calm, avoid arguing about the extreme view in the moment, set clear boundaries, and revisit the conversation when emotions settle.

Does all-or-nothing thinking improve with therapy?

Yes. Therapies such as DBT, cognitive behavioural therapy, and mentalization-based therapy can help people develop more balanced views.
