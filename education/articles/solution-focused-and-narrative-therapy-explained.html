Solution-Focused and Narrative Therapy Explained

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Solution-focused therapy and narrative therapy are two collaborative, strengths-based approaches that focus less on analyzing problems and more on possibilities. Solution-focused brief therapy (SFBT) helps people identify what they want, notice what is already working, and take small steps toward their goals, often in relatively few sessions. Narrative therapy helps people see that they are not their problems, explore the stories they tell about their lives, and develop alternative stories that reflect their values and strengths. Both treat people as experts on their own lives and emphasize hope and agency. They can be used on their own or alongside other approaches.

How These Approaches Work

Solution-Focused Brief Therapy

SFBT was developed in the 1980s by Steve de Shazer, Insoo Kim Berg, and colleagues. It is based on the idea that change is always happening, and that people already have strengths and resources they can build on. Rather than exploring the causes of a problem in depth, SFBT focuses on the preferred future. A well-known technique is the miracle question: "Suppose a miracle happened overnight and the problem was solved. What would be the first small sign that things were different?" Therapists also ask about exceptions, times when the problem was less intense, and use scaling questions, such as "On a scale from one to ten, where are you now, and what would one step higher look like?"

Narrative Therapy

Narrative therapy was developed by Michael White and David Epston. It is based on the idea that people make sense of their lives through stories, and that some stories, often shaped by culture, family, or past experiences, can be limiting. A central practice is externalizing the problem, speaking about it as something separate from the person, such as "the anxiety" or "the self-doubt," rather than "my anxiety" or "I am anxious." This reduces blame and opens space to explore how the problem influences life and how the person resists it. Therapists help people notice moments that do not fit the problem story and build a richer, preferred story around them.

What Sessions Are Like

Solution-Focused Sessions

SFBT sessions are conversational and forward-looking. The therapist asks many questions about goals, strengths, and what has helped before, and may end sessions with compliments and a suggestion to notice or try something small. SFBT is often brief, sometimes three to eight sessions, though some people attend longer.

Narrative Sessions

Narrative sessions explore how the problem has affected your life, what you value, and moments when you acted in line with those values. Therapists may use letters, documents, or invite important people to witness the new story. Narrative therapy may be short or longer-term, depending on goals.

Who They Can Help

Solution-focused therapy is used for a wide range of concerns, including stress, relationship difficulties, school and work problems, and mild to moderate anxiety and depression. Research suggests it can be helpful for many people, though evidence varies by concern. It suits people who want a practical, goal-focused approach. Narrative therapy is often used for identity, self-esteem, trauma, grief, family difficulties, and experiences shaped by culture or discrimination. Its evidence base is smaller but growing. Both approaches can support the work described on the pages about building self-esteem through therapy and building resilience after adversity.

For conditions with specific evidence-based treatments, such as OCD, PTSD, panic disorder, or eating disorders, these approaches are usually combined with or followed by targeted therapies such as CBT.

How They Compare

Both approaches are collaborative, respectful, and strengths-based, and both avoid treating people as broken. Solution-focused therapy is more future-oriented and brief, centred on goals and small steps. Narrative therapy is more reflective, exploring meaning, identity, and the stories that shape life. They share values with person-centred and humanistic therapies, though they use more structured questions. Some therapists blend them.

Common Questions About Solution-Focused and Narrative Therapy

What is the miracle question in solution-focused therapy?

The miracle question in solution-focused therapy asks what would be different if the problem were solved overnight, helping clarify goals.

What does externalizing the problem mean in narrative therapy?

In narrative therapy, externalizing means talking about a problem as separate from the person, which reduces blame and opens space for change.

How many sessions does solution-focused therapy usually take?

Solution-focused brief therapy is often short, sometimes three to eight sessions, though some people attend longer.

Who developed narrative therapy?

Narrative therapy was developed by Michael White and David Epston.

Does solution-focused therapy ignore my problems?

No. Solution-focused therapy acknowledges problems but focuses more on goals, strengths, and what already works.

Why would a solution-focused therapist ask me to rate my week from one to ten?

These scaling questions help you notice progress and picture what one step higher would look like, which makes goals in solution-focused therapy feel concrete.

Can narrative therapy help with identity and self-esteem?

Yes. Narrative therapy is often used to explore identity and self-esteem by building stories that reflect your values and strengths.

Why does solution-focused therapy ask about days when my problem was not so bad?

Those better days, called exceptions, hold clues. Solution-focused therapy explores what was different then so you can do more of it.

Is solution-focused or narrative therapy supported by research?

Research suggests solution-focused therapy helps many people, with varying evidence by concern. Narrative therapy's evidence base is smaller but growing.

How do solution-focused and narrative therapy differ?

Solution-focused therapy is brief and future-oriented, while narrative therapy is more reflective, exploring identity and life stories.
