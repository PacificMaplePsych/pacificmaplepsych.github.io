Coping With the Emotional Side of Infertility

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Coping with the emotional side of infertility usually involves naming the grief and stress as real, finding ways to protect your wellbeing during cycles of hope and disappointment, sharing the load with a partner or trusted people, and setting limits on how much fertility takes over daily life. Infertility can bring sadness, anxiety, anger, guilt, jealousy, and a sense that life is on hold. Fertility treatment adds physical demands, financial pressure, and waiting. These feelings are common and understandable, whether you are at the start of trying or years into treatment. Support can make a real difference in how people cope.

Why Infertility Is So Emotionally Hard

Infertility often involves repeated losses, such as each negative test, a failed cycle, or a pregnancy loss, as well as the loss of an imagined future. Because these losses may not be visible or openly discussed, many people grieve without recognition, as described on the page about disenfranchised grief. The monthly cycle of hope and disappointment can be exhausting, and uncertainty about outcomes makes it hard to plan.

Infertility can also affect identity, relationships, work, and finances. Partners may cope differently, with one wanting to talk and the other withdrawing. Social events, pregnancy announcements, and well-meaning questions can be painful. The mental health effects are explored further on the page about infertility and mental health. Coping strategies help people protect their wellbeing through a process that is often long and unpredictable.

Strategies to Try

1. Acknowledge the Grief

Allow yourself to grieve each loss, including hopes and plans. Naming feelings, through talking, writing, or rituals, can ease their weight.

2. Share the Load as a Couple

Talk openly about how each of you is coping, and accept that you may feel differently at different times. Some couples set aside a limited time each day to discuss fertility, so it does not dominate every conversation.

3. Set Boundaries Around Questions

Decide what you want to share and with whom. Prepare a simple response to intrusive questions, such as "We would rather keep that private, thank you."

4. Protect Yourself From Triggers

It is okay to skip baby showers, mute social media accounts, or take breaks from certain conversations.

5. Plan for Waiting Periods

The wait between procedures and results can be especially hard. Plan distracting, comforting activities and support from others during these times.

6. Look After Your Body

Rest, movement, and nourishing meals support coping, without turning wellness into another test you must pass.

7. Keep Parts of Life Separate From Fertility

Maintain hobbies, friendships, and activities that remind you of who you are beyond infertility.

8. Find Others Who Understand

Fertility support groups, in person or online, can reduce isolation and offer practical insight.

9. Treat Yourself With Compassion

Many people blame themselves. Self-compassion practices, such as those on the page about compassion-focused therapy, can ease harsh self-talk.

Common Obstacles

Financial pressure from treatment can add strain and complicate decisions. Planning ahead and talking openly about limits helps. Work demands, such as frequent appointments, can be hard to manage without disclosing, and people differ in how much they want to share with employers. Partners may disagree about when to continue, pause, or stop treatment, or about options such as donor conception or adoption. These decisions can be emotionally complex and may benefit from counselling. Jealousy toward friends or family who become pregnant is common and does not make you a bad person.

Some people struggle with the decision to stop treatment, which can bring another wave of grief. There is no single right path, and many people find meaningful lives after infertility, with or without children.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If infertility is causing persistent low mood, anxiety, sleep problems, relationship strain, or hopelessness, or if you are struggling after a pregnancy loss, counselling can help. Individual therapy offers space to process grief and cope with uncertainty. Couples counselling, described on the page about how couples therapy works, can help partners support each other and make decisions together. Many fertility clinics also offer counselling. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Coping With Infertility

Why does infertility feel so emotionally overwhelming?

Infertility often involves repeated losses, uncertainty, cycles of hope and disappointment, and strain on relationships and finances.

Is it normal to grieve while going through infertility?

Yes. Grief during infertility is common, including grief for each negative result and for an imagined future.

How can couples support each other through infertility?

Talk openly, accept different coping styles, and set limited times to discuss fertility so it does not dominate every conversation.

How do I cope with pregnancy announcements while facing infertility?

It is okay to mute social media, skip events, or take space. Jealousy during infertility is common and does not make you a bad person.

How do I answer relatives who keep asking about babies while we face infertility?

Prepare a simple response, such as saying you would rather keep that private, to protect yourself from intrusive fertility questions.

What helps me get through the days between an IVF procedure and the result?

Plan comforting, absorbing activities, lean on supportive people, and limit symptom-checking during the wait between IVF or other fertility procedures and results.

Are fertility support groups helpful?

Many people find fertility support groups reduce isolation and offer practical insight from others who understand.

Why do I blame myself for infertility?

Self-blame is common with infertility, even though causes are rarely within anyone's control. Self-compassion can help ease it.

My partner wants another IVF round and I am not sure. How do we decide together?

Disagreeing about continuing fertility treatment is common. Honest talks about limits, hopes, and options, often with counselling, help partners decide together.

Is it common to need counselling during infertility, even if I am coping on the surface?

Yes. Many people seek counselling during infertility, especially if it brings persistent low mood, anxiety, relationship strain, or hopelessness.
