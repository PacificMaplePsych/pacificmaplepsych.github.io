What Is Selective Mutism

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Selective mutism is an anxiety disorder in which a child consistently cannot speak in certain social situations, such as at school or with unfamiliar adults, even though they speak comfortably in others, such as at home with family. It is not a choice or stubbornness. The child wants to speak but feels frozen by anxiety. Selective mutism usually begins before age five and is often noticed when a child starts daycare or school. To be diagnosed, the difficulty must last at least a month, beyond the first month of school, and interfere with learning or social life. With early, gentle, and structured support, most children make significant progress.

Signs of Selective Mutism

  • Speaking freely at home but not at school or in public
  • Freezing, looking down, or appearing expressionless when expected to speak
  • Communicating nonverbally, such as by nodding, pointing, or whispering
  • Difficulty speaking to certain people, even relatives
  • Avoiding eye contact in uncomfortable situations
  • Strong shyness or social anxiety

Why It Happens

Selective mutism is closely linked to social anxiety and often runs in families with anxiety. Children with selective mutism are often temperamentally cautious or sensitive. When they avoid speaking, their anxiety drops, which reinforces not speaking. Over time, others may speak for them, which unintentionally maintains the pattern. Some children with selective mutism also have speech or language difficulties, or are learning more than one language, which can add to the challenge.

How Adults Can Accidentally Maintain Silence

Well-meaning adults often step in to rescue a child who freezes, for example by answering questions for them, accepting nods instead of words, or no longer expecting the child to speak. These responses ease the child's discomfort in the moment, but they also remove opportunities to practise speaking. Over time, others may stop addressing the child directly, which can make speaking even harder. Treatment helps families and teachers find a balance: reducing pressure and anxiety while still offering gentle, structured chances to speak, and allowing time for the child to respond. Small successes, such as whispering one word to a teacher, are celebrated quietly and built upon.

What It Is Not

Selective mutism is not defiance, and it is not usually caused by trauma. It is also different from a child who is simply shy or quiet for a short time when starting school, and from children who are not yet speaking a new language. Autism and language disorders can involve speaking difficulties too, so a careful assessment considers these.

How It Is Treated

Behavioural Therapy

Cognitive behavioural approaches with gradual steps toward speaking are the most effective treatment. A child might begin by speaking to a parent in a quiet classroom, then with a teacher nearby, then directly to the teacher, building comfort step by step.

Stimulus Fading

A trusted person the child talks to is gradually joined by a new person, who slowly takes part in the conversation.

Reducing Pressure

Adults avoid pressuring, bribing, or praising loudly for speaking, while still giving opportunities to communicate.

Parent and School Involvement

Parents and teachers learn strategies to support speaking and avoid accidentally maintaining silence, such as answering for the child, as described on the page about how to support an anxious child.

Speech-Language Support

If there are speech or language difficulties, a speech-language pathologist can help.

Medication

For some older children with severe symptoms, a physician may consider medication alongside therapy.

When to Get Support

Seek help if your child has not spoken at school or in other settings for more than a month, or if it is affecting learning or friendships. Early treatment is more effective, and progress can be faster when started young. The page about anxiety in children and teens describes related concerns.

Common Questions About Selective Mutism

Why can a child with selective mutism talk at home but not at school?

A child with selective mutism can talk at home but not at school because anxiety in certain social settings makes them freeze, even though they want to speak.

What causes selective mutism?

Selective mutism is closely linked to anxiety, often runs in families, and is maintained when not speaking reduces anxiety.

At what age does selective mutism start?

Selective mutism usually begins before age five and is often noticed when a child starts daycare or school.

Is selective mutism the same as shyness?

No. Selective mutism involves a consistent inability to speak in certain settings for more than a month, beyond typical shyness.

Is selective mutism caused by trauma?

Selective mutism is not usually caused by trauma. It is primarily an anxiety disorder.

How is selective mutism treated?

Selective mutism is treated with gradual behavioural therapy, stimulus fading, reduced pressure, and parent and school involvement.

Can children outgrow selective mutism?

Some children improve over time, but early treatment usually leads to faster and more reliable progress.

How can teachers help a child with selective mutism?

Teachers can reduce pressure, allow nonverbal communication at first, and follow a gradual plan toward speaking.

Should I make my child with selective mutism speak?

Pressuring a child to speak usually increases anxiety. Gentle, gradual opportunities work better.

Is selective mutism related to autism?

Selective mutism is different from autism, but speaking difficulties can overlap, so a careful assessment helps.
