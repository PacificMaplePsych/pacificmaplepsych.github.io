What Is EMDR Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

EMDR, which stands for eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, is a structured psychotherapy for trauma. During EMDR, a person briefly focuses on a distressing memory while also attending to bilateral stimulation, such as following the therapist's hand movements with their eyes, hearing alternating tones, or feeling alternating taps. Over a series of sessions, the memory usually becomes less vivid and less upsetting, and the beliefs attached to it, such as "I am not safe," often shift. EMDR was developed by psychologist Francine Shapiro in the late 1980s. It is recommended for PTSD by organizations including the World Health Organization and is also used for other trauma-related difficulties.

How EMDR Is Thought to Work

When something overwhelming happens, the memory can be stored in a raw, fragmented way, so reminders trigger the same fear, images, and body sensations as the original event. EMDR aims to help the brain process the memory more fully so that it can be remembered without the same distress. Researchers continue to study why bilateral stimulation helps. One leading explanation is that holding a memory in mind while doing a second task reduces its vividness and emotional intensity, making it easier to reprocess.

The Eight Phases of EMDR

  1. History and planning. The therapist learns about your history, current difficulties, and goals, and identifies target memories.
  2. Preparation. You learn coping and grounding skills and what to expect.
  3. Assessment. You identify an image, a negative belief, a preferred belief, emotions, and body sensations linked to the memory.
  4. Desensitization. You focus on the memory during sets of bilateral stimulation, noticing whatever comes up.
  5. Installation. The preferred belief, such as "I survived, and I am safe now," is strengthened.
  6. Body scan. You notice any remaining tension in the body.
  7. Closure. Each session ends with steps to help you feel settled.
  8. Re-evaluation. Progress is reviewed at the start of the next session.

What Sessions Are Like

You remain awake, aware, and in control throughout. You do not need to describe the memory in detail out loud, which some people find easier than other trauma therapies. Sessions often last 60 to 90 minutes. The number of sessions varies. A single recent trauma may need only a few processing sessions, while complex or repeated trauma usually takes longer and includes more preparation, as described on the page about complex PTSD.

Coping Between Sessions

Because EMDR can stir up memories and emotions, therapists usually teach coping skills before processing begins, such as calming imagery and grounding techniques.

Who It Can Help

EMDR has strong evidence for PTSD, including after assaults, accidents, and car crashes. It is also used for disturbing memories that do not meet criteria for PTSD, and research is growing for anxiety, phobias, and other conditions.

How It Compares With Other Trauma Therapies

EMDR, cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure, and trauma-focused CBT all have strong evidence for PTSD. The best choice depends on your preferences, your history, and the therapist's training, as described on the page about how PTSD is treated.

What It Is Not

EMDR is not hypnosis, and it does not erase memories. You remember what happened, but it no longer feels as though it is happening now. It can stir up emotions between sessions for a while, so preparation and coping skills are an important part of the process.

Common Questions About EMDR Therapy

How does EMDR therapy help trauma memories feel less upsetting?

EMDR therapy helps trauma memories feel less upsetting by having you briefly recall a memory while following bilateral stimulation, which seems to reduce its vividness and emotional intensity.

Why is EMDR named after eye movements?

EMDR stands for eye movement desensitization and reprocessing, named after the eye movements originally used as bilateral stimulation.

Does EMDR work for PTSD?

Yes. EMDR has strong research support and is recommended for PTSD by organizations including the World Health Organization.

What does bilateral stimulation involve during EMDR?

Bilateral stimulation in EMDR means following the therapist's hand movements with your eyes, hearing alternating tones, or feeling alternating taps while briefly recalling a memory.

Is EMDR easier for people who do not want to describe their trauma aloud?

Some people find EMDR easier because it does not require describing the trauma in detail out loud.

How many EMDR sessions are needed?

The number of EMDR sessions varies. A single trauma may need a few processing sessions, while complex trauma usually takes longer.

Is EMDR the same as hypnosis?

No. During EMDR you are fully awake, aware, and in control, which is different from hypnosis.

Does online EMDR work as well as in-person EMDR?

Online EMDR can be effective, using on-screen eye movements or self-administered taps, and many people find it works well.

Can EMDR make you feel worse at first?

EMDR can stir up emotions or memories between sessions for a while, which is why preparation and coping skills come first.

When was EMDR first developed?

EMDR was first developed in the late 1980s by psychologist Francine Shapiro.
