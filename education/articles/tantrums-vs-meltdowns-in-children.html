Tantrums vs. Meltdowns in Children

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Tantrums and meltdowns can both involve crying, screaming, or flailing, but they are different. A tantrum is usually an outburst linked to wanting something or frustration with a limit, and the child often has some control, may check whether adults are watching, and tends to stop when the goal is met or ignored. A meltdown is a response to being overwhelmed, often by sensory input, emotions, or change, in which the child loses control and cannot simply stop, even if they want to. Meltdowns are common in autistic children and children with ADHD or sensory sensitivities. Tantrums call for calm, consistent limits, while meltdowns call for safety, reduced demands, and calm support.

How to Tell Them Apart

Cause

Tantrums often follow a "no" or a frustrated goal. Meltdowns follow overwhelm, such as noise, crowds, transitions, or accumulated stress.

Control

During a tantrum, a child may adjust their behaviour depending on adult responses. During a meltdown, the child is not in control and does not respond to reasoning, rewards, or consequences.

Audience

Tantrums often need an audience. Meltdowns happen whether or not anyone is watching.

Ending

Tantrums often end when the child gets what they want or realizes it is not working. Meltdowns end when the nervous system calms, often followed by exhaustion.

Age

Tantrums are most common between ages one and four and decline as language and self-control develop. Meltdowns can happen at any age, including in teens and adults, as described on the page about autistic meltdowns and shutdowns in adulthood.

Responding to Tantrums

  • Stay calm and keep your voice low.
  • Hold the limit. Giving in teaches that tantrums work.
  • Name the feeling. "You are really upset that we have to leave the park."
  • Avoid lengthy reasoning in the moment.
  • Keep everyone safe.
  • Reconnect afterward and praise calm behaviour.

Responding to Meltdowns

  • Prioritize safety. Move away from hazards if needed.
  • Reduce input. Lower noise and lights, and give space.
  • Use few words. Speak calmly and simply, or stay quietly nearby.
  • Do not punish. The child is not choosing this.
  • Offer comfort tools such as a favourite object, deep pressure, or a quiet space, if the child finds these helpful.
  • Allow recovery time afterward without expecting an immediate explanation.

After a Meltdown

Once a child has calmed down, they may feel tired, embarrassed, or upset about what happened. This is not the time for a lecture. Offer comfort, water, or quiet time. Later, when everyone is calm, you can gently talk about what led up to the meltdown and what might help next time, using simple language or visuals for younger children. Looking for patterns, such as hunger, tiredness, noisy places, or transitions, can help you plan ahead and reduce future meltdowns.

Preventing Meltdowns

Notice early signs, such as covering ears, pacing, or increased irritability. Prepare children for transitions, plan breaks in busy environments, and maintain predictable routines, as described on the page about sensory overload. Teaching calming skills when the child is calm can help, as described on the page about calming and breathing exercises for kids.

When to Seek Support

Consider support if outbursts are frequent, intense, involve harm to self or others, continue well beyond the preschool years, or happen alongside other concerns, such as communication differences, sensory sensitivities, or attention difficulties. An assessment for autism or ADHD may help clarify your child's needs. Support for parents, described on the page about parent coaching, can help you respond with confidence. The page about staying calm as a parent when you feel overwhelmed may also help.

Common Questions About Tantrums and Meltdowns

How can I tell a tantrum from a meltdown?

A tantrum is usually an outburst linked to wanting something, with some control, while a meltdown is a loss of control caused by overwhelm.

What causes meltdowns in children?

Meltdowns in children are caused by overwhelm from sensory input, emotions, transitions, or accumulated stress.

Are meltdowns a sign of autism?

Meltdowns are common in autistic children, but they also occur in children with ADHD, sensory sensitivities, or high stress.

How should I respond to a tantrum?

Respond to a tantrum by staying calm, holding the limit, naming the feeling, keeping everyone safe, and reconnecting afterward.

What helps a child calm down during a meltdown?

During a meltdown, keep the child safe, reduce noise and light, use few words, avoid punishment, and allow recovery time.

At what age do tantrums stop?

Tantrums are most common between ages one and four and usually decline as language and self-control develop.

Should I punish a child for a meltdown?

No. Children are not in control during a meltdown, so punishment does not help and can increase distress.

What early signs warn that a child's meltdown is coming?

Early warning signs of a meltdown can include covering ears, pacing, increased irritability, or withdrawing, which signal a child needs less input or a break.

Can teens have meltdowns?

Yes. Meltdowns can happen at any age, including in teens and adults, especially when overwhelmed.

When should I get help for my child's tantrums or meltdowns?

Seek help if outbursts are frequent, intense, harmful, continue beyond the preschool years, or occur with other developmental concerns.
