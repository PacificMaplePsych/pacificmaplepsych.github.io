What Is Birth Trauma

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or you are worried about your safety, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Birth trauma refers to the psychological distress some people experience after a birth they found frightening, painful, or out of their control. It can follow emergencies, such as a sudden caesarean, heavy bleeding, or concern for the baby's life, but it can also follow births that looked routine to others. What makes a birth traumatic is how it was experienced, including feeling unheard, powerless, or afraid. Some people develop postpartum post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Partners who witnessed the birth can be affected too. Birth trauma is common and treatable.

What Birth Trauma Looks Like

  • Intrusive memories, flashbacks, or nightmares about the birth.
  • Avoiding reminders, such as the hospital, birth stories, or medical appointments.
  • Feeling on edge, easily startled, or unable to relax.
  • Anger, guilt, or shame about how the birth went.
  • Feeling detached or numb, including difficulty bonding with the baby.
  • Strong fear about future pregnancies or births.
  • Distress during intimacy or physical examinations.

At home, reminders of the birth may appear constantly, from the baby's birthday to routine care. In relationships, partners may process the experience differently, which can create distance.

Why It Happens

Birth involves intense physical sensations, vulnerability, and high stakes. When a person fears for their own life or their baby's, feels a loss of control, or feels dismissed or mistreated by care providers, the brain can store the experience as a traumatic memory. Risk factors include emergency interventions, severe pain, previous trauma or pregnancy loss, lack of support during labour, and a baby needing intensive care. Poor communication and not being included in decisions often play a large role. Sleep deprivation and the demands of newborn care can make it harder to process what happened.

What It Is Not

  • Not about whether the birth was medically "fine." A healthy outcome does not cancel a traumatic experience.
  • Not postpartum depression, though they can overlap. Postpartum depression centres on low mood and loss of interest. Birth trauma centres on trauma responses to the birth itself.
  • Not postpartum anxiety alone. Anxiety can follow a traumatic birth, but intrusive memories and avoidance point toward trauma. The page on feeling anxious all the time since having a baby describes postpartum anxiety.
  • Not intrusive OCD thoughts. Unwanted thoughts about harm coming to the baby are common in postpartum OCD and are different from memories of the birth.
  • Not your fault. How you responded during birth was your body and mind doing their best under extreme circumstances.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A physician, midwife, or perinatal mental health professional can screen for birth trauma and postpartum PTSD, and for co-occurring depression and anxiety. Some hospitals offer birth debriefing, a chance to review what happened and ask questions. Trauma-focused therapies, such as cognitive processing therapy and trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, have good evidence for PTSD and can help process the birth memory. Support for sleep, practical help at home, and involving partners can also aid recovery. For people considering future pregnancies, planning with care providers can reduce fear.

Common Questions About Birth Trauma

Can a birth be traumatic even if the baby was healthy?

Yes. Birth trauma depends on how the birth was experienced, including fear, pain, and loss of control, not only on medical outcomes.

Can you develop PTSD from giving birth?

Postpartum PTSD is post-traumatic stress disorder after childbirth, involving intrusive memories, avoidance, negative mood, and hyperarousal related to the birth.

What makes a birth feel traumatic?

Common factors include emergencies, severe pain, fear for your or your baby's life, feeling unheard or powerless, and separation from the baby after birth.

Can partners experience birth trauma?

Yes. Partners who witnessed a frightening birth can experience trauma responses, including intrusive memories and anxiety, and deserve support too.

How is birth trauma different from postpartum depression?

Postpartum depression centres on low mood and loss of interest. Birth trauma involves trauma responses such as flashbacks and avoidance linked to the birth, though both can occur together.

Why do I avoid anything that reminds me of giving birth?

Avoidance is a common trauma response that protects against painful memories. Over time it can keep distress going, which therapy can help address.

What is a birth debriefing?

A birth debriefing is a conversation, often with a care provider, to review what happened during the birth, answer questions, and help make sense of the experience.

Can a traumatic birth affect bonding with my baby?

It can make bonding harder at first, as trauma responses and exhaustion take up emotional space. Treatment often helps connection with the baby grow.

Will birth trauma affect a future pregnancy?

It can bring strong fear about future births. Planning with care providers and trauma-focused therapy can reduce fear and support a different experience.

Which therapies treat trauma from childbirth?

Trauma-focused approaches such as cognitive processing therapy and trauma-focused CBT have good evidence for PTSD, including after birth.
