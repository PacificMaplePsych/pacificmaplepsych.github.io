Painful Sex and Vaginismus: The Psychological Side

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Pain with sex, particularly with vaginal penetration, is common and treatable. Vaginismus describes involuntary tightening of the pelvic floor muscles that makes penetration painful or impossible, whether during sex, tampon use, or medical exams. In clinical terms, these difficulties fall under genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder. Physical factors, such as infections, skin conditions, hormonal changes, or pelvic floor tension, often play a role, and psychological factors, such as fear of pain, anxiety, past painful experiences, or trauma, can intensify it. Pain and fear can create a cycle: expecting pain leads to tension, which causes more pain. Care often combines medical assessment, pelvic floor physiotherapy, and psychological support.

The Pain-Fear Cycle

  1. A painful experience or fear of pain occurs.
  2. The body anticipates pain and the pelvic floor muscles tighten automatically.
  3. Tension causes more pain or makes penetration impossible.
  4. Fear and avoidance grow, reinforcing the cycle.

Breaking this cycle is a central goal of treatment.

Possible Contributing Factors

Physical

Infections, skin conditions, endometriosis, vaginal dryness from hormonal changes such as perimenopause or breastfeeding, and pelvic floor dysfunction.

Psychological

Anxiety about sex or pain, as described on the page about performance anxiety, negative messages about sex, relationship stress, and past sexual trauma or painful medical experiences, including birth trauma.

Relational

Pressure, lack of communication, or conflict can increase anxiety and tension.

How It Is Treated

Medical Assessment

A family doctor or gynecologist can check for physical causes and provide treatment where needed.

Pelvic Floor Physiotherapy

A pelvic health physiotherapist can help with muscle relaxation, awareness, and gradual use of dilators.

Therapy

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy addresses fears and beliefs about pain and sex.
  • Gradual exposure at your own pace reduces fear and avoidance.
  • Relaxation and mindfulness help reduce muscle tension and anxiety.
  • Trauma-focused therapy can help when past experiences contribute, as described on the page about trauma-informed therapy.

Couples Work

Partners can learn to reduce pressure, communicate, and explore intimacy that does not focus on penetration, as described on the page about mismatched libido in relationships.

What Progress Can Look Like

Treatment usually moves in small, planned steps at a pace the person chooses. Early goals might include learning about pelvic anatomy, practising relaxation of the pelvic floor, and becoming more comfortable with touch that does not involve penetration. Later steps might include using a finger or a small dilator, gradually increasing size, and eventually trying penetration with a partner when that feels right. Many people find that reducing the pressure to reach a particular goal by a certain date actually speeds progress, because the body relaxes when it does not feel rushed. Setbacks, such as a painful experience after a period of progress, are common and do not mean treatment has failed. A therapist can help you understand what happened and adjust the plan. It can also help to remember that sexual connection is much broader than penetration, so intimacy and pleasure do not need to wait until treatment is complete.

What It Is Not

Painful sex is not something you have to endure, and it is not "all in your head." It is also not a sign that something is wrong with your relationship or that you do not care about your partner. Many people feel shame or isolation, but these difficulties are common and very treatable.

When to Get Support

Talk to a health care provider if you have pain with sex, tampons, or exams. If fear, avoidance, or relationship strain is part of the picture, therapy can help alongside medical and physiotherapy care. If sexual trauma is involved, the sexual assault page offers information and resources.

Common Questions About Painful Sex and Vaginismus

Is vaginismus an involuntary muscle response?

Yes. Vaginismus is involuntary tightening of the pelvic floor muscles that makes penetration painful or impossible, even when a person wants to have sex.

Is painful sex common?

Yes. Painful sex is common and treatable, though many people feel embarrassed to talk about it.

What causes vaginismus?

Vaginismus can be caused by physical factors, fear of pain, anxiety, past painful experiences, or trauma, often in combination.

Is vaginismus psychological?

Vaginismus often involves both physical and psychological factors, and treatment usually addresses both.

How is vaginismus treated?

Vaginismus is treated with medical assessment, pelvic floor physiotherapy, gradual dilator use, and therapy such as CBT.

Can anxiety cause pain during sex?

Yes. Anxiety can cause muscle tension and heightened pain sensitivity, contributing to pain during sex.

What is genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder?

Genito-pelvic pain/penetration disorder is the clinical term for persistent difficulties with penetration, pain, fear of pain, or pelvic floor tension.

How can a therapist help with painful sex alongside physiotherapy?

A therapist can help with painful sex by reducing fear and anxiety, addressing trauma, and helping couples communicate, alongside medical and pelvic floor physiotherapy care.

Can vaginismus be cured?

Many people overcome vaginismus with treatment, though it can take time and patience.

Should my partner be involved in treatment for vaginismus?

Involving a partner can help reduce pressure and improve communication, though treatment can also begin individually.
