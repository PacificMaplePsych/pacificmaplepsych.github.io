Why Do I Have Thoughts That Scare Me and I Don't Know Why

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Out of nowhere, a thought arrives that feels completely at odds with who you are, something disturbing, violent, or otherwise deeply unwanted, and it lands with a jolt of fear or shame. Maybe it is about harming someone you love, or something inappropriate, or a fear that you could lose control in some way. If this has happened to you, and especially if it has left you wondering what it means about you as a person, it is important to understand what is actually going on, because this experience is far more common, and far less meaningful, than it feels in the moment.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Ordinary intrusive thoughts. Unwanted, strange, or disturbing thoughts are an extremely common part of normal human cognition. Research on this topic consistently finds that the vast majority of people experience intrusive thoughts of some kind, including violent or disturbing ones, without it meaning anything about their character or actual desires. Most people simply notice the thought, feel briefly unsettled, and let it pass without much further attention.

Sleep deprivation and stress. A tired or overloaded mind is more prone to unusual, vivid, or unsettling thoughts in general, as part of a broader dip in mental regulation rather than anything specific to the content of the thought itself.

Postpartum intrusive thoughts. Many new parents experience distressing intrusive thoughts about harm coming to their baby, which is a well recognized and common experience, especially in the postpartum period, and is not the same as wanting or intending harm.

General anxiety. An anxious mind tends to generate more "what if" thinking across the board, including disturbing hypothetical scenarios, as part of a broader pattern of catastrophic or worst-case thinking.

When It Looks More Like OCD

What turns an ordinary intrusive thought into something more clinically significant is not the content of the thought itself, but the response to it. In OCD, and specifically in a presentation sometimes called purely obsessional OCD, the distressing thought gets stuck, triggering intense anxiety, shame, or a compulsive need to mentally analyze, neutralize, or seek reassurance about what the thought means. Ironically, this response, born from taking the thought too seriously rather than letting it pass, is what tends to make it recur more, not less, since anxious attention to a thought makes the brain more likely to flag it as important and repeat it.

A crucial and genuinely important distinguishing feature: having a disturbing intrusive thought about harm, whether to yourself or someone else, is fundamentally different from wanting to act on it. People experiencing this kind of OCD are typically the opposite of the thought's content, deeply values-driven, conscientious, and specifically horrified by the thought precisely because it contradicts who they are and what they care about. The distress itself is actually a meaningful clue that this is OCD-related rather than a genuine risk, since it reflects how strongly the thought conflicts with the person's actual values and intentions.

This pattern often includes mental compulsions that are less visible than physical ones, silently reviewing the thought, mentally checking whether it means something, seeking reassurance from others or the internet, or trying hard to prove to yourself that you are not the kind of person who would think this, all of which tend to reinforce the cycle rather than resolve it.

What Can Help

Understanding that intrusive thoughts are a normal, near universal part of human cognition, and that their content says nothing about actual character or intent, is often the first and most relieving step. Learning to notice a disturbing thought without engaging in mental analysis or reassurance-seeking, letting it pass through rather than grabbing hold of it, tends to reduce its frequency and intensity over time, even though this can feel counterintuitive at first. Specialized therapy, particularly exposure and response prevention adapted for this kind of intrusive thought, is highly effective and specifically addresses this pattern.

Common Questions About Scary Intrusive Thoughts

Is it normal to have disturbing thoughts sometimes?

Yes, this is extremely common. Research consistently shows that the vast majority of people experience unwanted, strange, or disturbing thoughts at some point, and having one does not mean anything about your character, values, or actual intentions.

Does having a scary thought mean I secretly want to act on it?

No. This is one of the most important distinctions to understand. People who are distressed by intrusive thoughts are typically the opposite of the thought's content, values-driven and specifically horrified because the thought conflicts so strongly with who they actually are.

What is “purely obsessional OCD”?

It refers to a presentation of OCD centred mainly on intrusive, distressing thoughts and mental compulsions, such as mentally reviewing or seeking reassurance, rather than visible physical compulsions like checking or washing.

Why does trying to push a thought away sometimes make it come back stronger?

Giving a thought intense anxious attention, even in an attempt to get rid of it, tends to signal to the brain that the thought is important, which paradoxically makes it more likely to recur. Letting a thought pass without engaging with it tends to reduce this cycle over time.

Is this common after having a baby?

Yes, distressing intrusive thoughts about harm coming to a baby are a well recognized and common experience for many new parents, and are not the same as wanting or intending harm. This is worth mentioning to a healthcare provider if it is causing significant distress.

How is this different from actually being a risk to someone?

The key difference is the presence of genuine distress and horror at the thought, versus intent or desire to act on it. Someone experiencing OCD-related intrusive thoughts is fighting against the thought, not planning around it, which is a meaningful and important distinction.

What are “mental compulsions”?

These are internal responses to an intrusive thought, such as silently reviewing it, mentally checking what it means, or seeking reassurance, that function similarly to visible compulsions like checking a lock, but are less obvious to others.

Can sleep deprivation or stress make intrusive thoughts worse?

Yes. A tired or overloaded mind is generally more prone to unusual or unsettling thoughts, as part of a broader dip in mental regulation, independent of whether OCD is also part of the picture.

What treatment actually helps with this kind of intrusive thought?

Exposure and response prevention, adapted specifically for intrusive thoughts, is a well-researched and highly effective treatment. It works by helping someone tolerate the discomfort of the thought without engaging in mental compulsions, which reduces its grip over time.

What should I do if I'm not sure whether my thoughts are OCD-related or something more serious?

If the thoughts cause genuine distress and horror and feel completely unwanted, that pattern is consistent with OCD, and an assessment can help clarify this further. If you ever feel you may act on a thought of harming yourself or someone else, please contact a crisis line, your doctor, or emergency services right away.
