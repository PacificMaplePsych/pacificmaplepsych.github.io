What Is Postpartum Depression

If you are having thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency. Postpartum depression is a mood condition that develops after having a baby, usually within the first few weeks to months, though it can begin any time in the first year. It involves persistent low mood, loss of interest, exhaustion, and feelings of guilt or worthlessness that go beyond the normal ups and downs of new parenthood. Postpartum depression is one of the most common complications of childbirth. It is not a sign of weakness or bad parenting, and it is not something parents should have to push through alone. With support and treatment, most people recover and feel like themselves again.

What Postpartum Depression Looks Like

  • Persistent sadness, emptiness, or tearfulness.
  • Loss of interest or pleasure in things, including the baby.
  • Feeling disconnected or not bonded with the baby.
  • Extreme fatigue beyond what sleep deprivation explains.
  • Feelings of guilt, shame, or being a bad parent.
  • Irritability or anger.
  • Changes in appetite.
  • Difficulty sleeping even when the baby sleeps, or sleeping too much.
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions.
  • Withdrawing from partner, family, or friends.
  • Thoughts of death, suicide, or harming yourself.

Postpartum depression often occurs alongside postpartum anxiety. Both are part of the broader group of perinatal mood and anxiety disorders.

Why It Happens

Postpartum depression arises from a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors. After birth, hormone levels change dramatically, which can affect mood in some people. Severe sleep deprivation, physical recovery, and the demands of caring for a newborn add strain. Big shifts in identity, relationships, work, and daily life can bring a sense of loss alongside joy.

Risk factors include a personal or family history of depression or anxiety, depression during pregnancy, a difficult or traumatic birth, pregnancy complications, a baby with health concerns, limited support, relationship difficulties, financial stress, and major life stressors. Many parents feel pressure to appear happy, which can make it hard to admit struggling. Non-birthing parents can also experience postnatal depression, as described on the page about paternal postnatal depression.

Postpartum depression can affect the whole family. It may make bonding, feeding, and daily care feel harder, and can strain relationships. Treatment helps parents recover and supports healthy development for babies. Seeking help is an act of care for everyone involved.

What It Is Not

  • Not the baby blues. Baby blues are mild mood swings in the first two weeks that pass on their own. Postpartum depression is more intense and lasts longer.
  • Not a sign you do not love your baby. Many parents with postpartum depression love their babies deeply.
  • Not your fault. It is a medical condition with many contributing factors.
  • Not something that always goes away on its own. Treatment speeds recovery.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Family doctors, midwives, and public health nurses often screen for postpartum depression using questionnaires. Treatment is effective. Psychotherapy, including cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy, helps with mood, thinking patterns, and role changes. Some antidepressants are considered compatible with breastfeeding and may be recommended for moderate to severe depression. Practical support, such as help with night feeds, meals, and household tasks, and protecting sleep reduce strain. Peer support groups for new parents reduce isolation. Involving partners in treatment can help the whole family.

Common Questions About Postpartum Depression

Why do I feel so low since giving birth?

You may be experiencing postpartum depression, a mood condition after birth involving persistent low mood, loss of interest, exhaustion, and guilt beyond the baby blues.

How is postpartum depression different from the baby blues?

Baby blues are mild and pass within about two weeks. Postpartum depression is more intense and lasts longer.

Can depression begin many months after the birth?

Yes. It usually begins within weeks to months after birth, but it can start any time in the first year.

Am I unusual for struggling after having a baby?

No. Postpartum depression is one of the most common complications of childbirth.

Does postpartum depression mean I do not love my baby?

No. Many parents with postpartum depression love their babies deeply but struggle with mood and energy.

Is my partner at risk of low mood after our baby too?

Yes. Non-birthing parents can also experience depression after a baby arrives.

Can I take antidepressants while breastfeeding?

Some antidepressants are considered compatible with breastfeeding. Discuss options with your doctor.

Which talking therapies help after birth?

Cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy are effective, often alongside practical support.

How can my partner support me with postpartum depression?

Partners can help with night feeds and household tasks, protect sleep, listen without judgment, and support treatment.

Who should I call first if I am struggling after birth?

Speak with your family doctor, midwife, or public health nurse, or call 8-1-1 for advice.
