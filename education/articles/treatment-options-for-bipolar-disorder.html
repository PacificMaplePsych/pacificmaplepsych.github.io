Treatment Options for Bipolar Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Signs of severe mania or psychosis, such as not sleeping for days, unsafe behaviour, or unusual beliefs, need urgent care. Call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Bipolar disorder is usually treated with a combination of medication prescribed by a psychiatrist or physician, psychotherapy, education about the condition, and daily routines that support mood stability. Medication, often a mood stabilizer or certain other medications, is the foundation of treatment for most people and helps prevent and treat episodes of mania, hypomania, and depression. Therapy approaches such as psychoeducation, cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal and social rhythm therapy, and family-focused therapy help people recognize early warning signs, manage stress, and stay well. Bipolar disorder is a long-term condition, but with consistent treatment, many people live full, stable lives.

The Main Treatment Options

Medication. Mood stabilizers and certain other medications are prescribed to treat episodes and reduce the risk of future ones. Some antidepressants may be used cautiously alongside a mood stabilizer, since on their own they can sometimes trigger elevated mood, as explained on the page about hypomania. Finding the right medication can take time, and regular monitoring, sometimes including blood tests, is common.

Psychoeducation. Learning about bipolar disorder, its patterns, and personal warning signs is one of the most helpful parts of treatment. It often includes creating a plan for what to do when symptoms appear.

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps people manage depressive thoughts, cope with stress, and notice early signs of mood changes.

Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy. This approach focuses on stabilizing daily routines, especially sleep, and managing relationship stress, since disrupted rhythms can trigger episodes.

Family-focused therapy. Involving family members improves communication, reduces conflict, and helps everyone respond to early warning signs.

Mood tracking. Daily tracking of mood, sleep, and energy, described on the page about mood tracking for bipolar disorder, supports treatment decisions.

Other treatments. For severe or treatment-resistant episodes, a psychiatrist may consider other options, including hospital care.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan depends on the type of bipolar disorder, such as bipolar I, bipolar II, or cyclothymia, the pattern and severity of episodes, and whether mania, depression, or mixed features are most prominent. Co-occurring conditions, such as anxiety, ADHD, or substance use, are considered. Health history, side effects, pregnancy plans, and personal preferences all shape medication choices. A psychiatrist usually leads medication decisions, while therapists support skills and coping. Coordination among providers helps.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress often begins with stabilizing a current episode, which may take weeks to months. Over time, the goal shifts to preventing future episodes, reducing their severity, and improving daily functioning. Many people learn to recognize early signs, such as reduced need for sleep or withdrawal, and act before a full episode develops. Setbacks can still happen, especially during stress, sleep disruption, or medication changes. They are a signal to adjust the plan, not a sign of failure. Long-term treatment is usually recommended, even when someone feels well.

Supporting Your Own Stability

Keeping consistent sleep and wake times is one of the most protective habits. Taking medication as prescribed and discussing concerns with your prescriber, rather than stopping suddenly, reduces relapse risk. Limiting alcohol and avoiding recreational drugs helps. Tracking mood and creating a relapse prevention plan make early action easier. Sharing warning signs with a trusted person means someone can help if you notice changes too late. Building a supportive routine and relationships contributes to long-term wellbeing.

Common Questions About Bipolar Disorder Treatment

What are the main treatments for bipolar disorder?

Bipolar disorder is usually treated with medication, psychoeducation, therapy such as CBT or family-focused therapy, and routines that support mood stability.

Why is medication usually the foundation of bipolar treatment?

Medication such as mood stabilizers helps treat and prevent bipolar mood episodes, which therapy alone usually cannot do.

Can antidepressants be used for bipolar depression?

Some antidepressants may be used cautiously alongside a mood stabilizer, since on their own they can sometimes trigger elevated mood in bipolar disorder.

Why do some bipolar therapies focus so much on regular sleep and daily timing?

Disrupted rhythms can trigger bipolar episodes, so approaches such as interpersonal and social rhythm therapy help stabilize routines, especially sleep, and manage relationship stress.

Should my family be part of my bipolar treatment?

Often it helps. Family-focused therapy improves communication, reduces conflict, and helps family members respond early to bipolar warning signs.

Do I need bipolar treatment even when I feel well?

Long-term bipolar treatment is usually recommended even during stable periods, since it helps prevent future episodes.

Why does my psychiatrist order blood tests for bipolar medication?

Some bipolar medications require regular blood tests to check levels and monitor effects on the body.

What should I do if I want to stop my bipolar medication?

Talk with your prescriber first. Stopping bipolar medication suddenly can increase the risk of relapse.

How long does it take to stabilize a bipolar episode?

Stabilizing a bipolar episode may take weeks to months, depending on its severity and how treatment is adjusted.

What daily habits help keep bipolar disorder stable?

Consistent sleep and wake times, taking medication as prescribed, limiting alcohol, and tracking mood all help keep bipolar disorder stable.
