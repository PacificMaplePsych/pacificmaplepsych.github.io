What Is Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) is a structured, goal-focused form of psychotherapy that helps people understand how their thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and behaviours affect each other, and learn practical skills to change unhelpful patterns. It is one of the most widely researched therapies and has strong evidence for anxiety, depression, insomnia, and many other concerns. CBT focuses mainly on the present and on what keeps problems going, rather than exploring the past in depth. It is collaborative, with the therapist and client working together as a team, and it usually involves practice between sessions. Many people notice improvement within a few months.

How CBT Works

CBT is based on the idea that it is not only situations that shape how we feel, but also how we interpret them and what we do in response. For example, if a friend does not reply to a message, one person might think "they are busy" and feel fine, while another might think "they are angry with me" and feel anxious, then avoid the friend. The same event leads to different feelings and actions depending on thoughts.

Over time, some patterns become automatic and keep difficulties going. Common patterns include catastrophizing, all-or-nothing thinking, mind reading, and harsh self-talk. Behaviours such as avoidance, checking, or withdrawing can bring short-term relief but keep problems going in the long run.

CBT helps people:

  • Notice thoughts, feelings, and behaviours in specific situations.
  • Identify unhelpful thinking patterns and examine them, looking for more balanced perspectives.
  • Test beliefs through behavioural experiments.
  • Gradually face feared situations through exposure, rather than avoiding them.
  • Increase meaningful and enjoyable activities through behavioural activation.
  • Build skills such as problem-solving, relaxation, and assertiveness.

CBT has many specialized forms, including CBT for insomnia, exposure and response prevention for OCD, and trauma-focused therapies such as cognitive processing therapy. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy combines CBT with mindfulness to help prevent depression relapse.

What Sessions Are Like

CBT sessions are usually structured. A typical session might begin with checking in on mood and the past week, setting an agenda, reviewing between-session practice, working on a specific skill or problem, and planning practice for the coming week. The therapist is active, asking questions, explaining ideas, and sometimes using worksheets or diagrams.

Early sessions involve understanding your concerns, setting goals, and learning how CBT works. Later sessions focus on applying skills to real situations. Between sessions, you might track thoughts, try an experiment, practise exposure, or schedule activities. This practice is where much of the change happens.

CBT is often shorter-term than some therapies, commonly around eight to twenty sessions, though the length depends on the concern and goals. Some people return for booster sessions later. CBT can be done individually, in groups, online, or with guided self-help materials.

Who It Can Help

CBT has strong evidence for anxiety disorders, depression, panic, social anxiety, OCD, PTSD, insomnia, and some eating disorders. It is also used for stress, anger, chronic pain, health anxiety, and low self-esteem. It can be adapted for children, teens, older adults, and neurodivergent people.

CBT suits people who like a structured, practical approach and are willing to practise between sessions. It may be less helpful on its own when people want to explore the past in depth, when difficulties are rooted in complex trauma or long-standing relationship patterns, or when someone is in crisis and needs stabilization first. Other approaches, such as emotion-focused, psychodynamic, or schema therapy, may fit better or be combined with CBT. Medication prescribed by a physician is sometimes used alongside CBT for moderate to severe concerns.

Common Questions About CBT

What is the main idea behind cognitive behavioural therapy?

CBT is based on the idea that thoughts, feelings, body sensations, and behaviours affect each other, and changing patterns can improve wellbeing.

How many CBT sessions will I usually need?

CBT often involves around eight to twenty sessions, depending on the concern and your goals.

Is there homework in CBT?

Yes. Practice between sessions, such as tracking thoughts or trying experiments, is an important part of CBT.

Does CBT look at my past or only the present?

CBT mainly focuses on the present and what keeps problems going, though past experiences may be discussed when relevant.

What problems does CBT have the strongest evidence for?

CBT has strong evidence for anxiety disorders, depression, panic, OCD, PTSD, insomnia, and some eating disorders.

How would my CBT therapist help me test whether a fear is actually true?

It is a planned activity that tests whether a belief is accurate, such as speaking up in a meeting to test a fear.

Why would a CBT therapist ask me to schedule enjoyable activities when I feel low?

It is a CBT technique that increases meaningful and enjoyable activities to gradually improve mood.

Can CBT be done online?

Yes. CBT can be delivered through video sessions, online programs, or guided self-help.

Is CBT a good fit if I want to explore my childhood?

CBT focuses mainly on the present, so other approaches may fit better or be combined with CBT for deeper exploration.

Can CBT be used alongside medication?

Yes. CBT is often combined with medication prescribed by a physician for moderate to severe concerns.
