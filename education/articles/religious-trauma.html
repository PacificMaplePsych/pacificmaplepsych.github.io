Religious Trauma

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Religious trauma describes lasting emotional or psychological harm resulting from experiences within a religious or spiritual setting. It can stem from fear-based teachings about punishment or hell, intense shame about sexuality or the body, controlling or authoritarian leadership, rejection by family or community, or abuse within a religious institution. People may experience anxiety, guilt, fear, difficulty trusting themselves, a loss of identity or community, and sometimes trauma symptoms such as intrusive thoughts. Religious trauma is not a formal diagnosis, and it does not mean religion itself is harmful. Many people find deep meaning in faith. The focus is on harmful experiences and their impact, and healing can include leaving, changing, or reconnecting with faith.

Common Sources of Religious Harm

  • Fear-based teachings about punishment, damnation, or the end of the world
  • Shame about sexuality, gender identity, or the body
  • Strict control over behaviour, relationships, clothing, or thinking
  • Shunning or rejection for doubting, leaving, or coming out
  • Spiritual abuse, where religious authority is used to manipulate or control
  • Sexual, physical, or emotional abuse within religious institutions
  • Suppression of emotions or questions

Signs of Religious Trauma

  • Anxiety, guilt, or fear related to religious beliefs or practices
  • Intrusive thoughts about sin, punishment, or being bad, sometimes overlapping with scrupulosity
  • Difficulty making decisions or trusting your own judgment
  • Shame about sexuality, body, or desires
  • Grief over lost community, family relationships, or certainty
  • Identity confusion after leaving or changing beliefs
  • Trauma symptoms such as nightmares or panic linked to religious reminders

Why It Can Be So Hard

Religious communities often shape a person's entire world, including family, friendships, values, and sense of purpose. Leaving or questioning can mean losing many of these at once, which can lead to deep loneliness, as described on the page about identity loss after a life transition. Messages learned in childhood can feel deeply ingrained, even after beliefs change.

Relationships With Family and Community

For many people, religious trauma is intertwined with relationships. Questioning or leaving a faith can create tension or estrangement with parents, siblings, a partner, or an entire community. Some people continue to participate in family religious events while privately holding different beliefs, which can feel isolating. Others find ways to stay connected with loved ones while setting boundaries around certain topics. There is no single right approach, and decisions often change over time. Therapy can help people think through these relationships, manage guilt and conflict, and find ways to stay connected where possible while honouring their own beliefs.

What It Is Not

Recognizing religious trauma is not an attack on faith or religious people. Many people have positive experiences in religious communities, and some heal while remaining in or returning to their faith. Religious trauma is about harmful experiences and their impact, not about whether a person is religious.

What Supports Healing

  • Naming the experience and recognizing its impact.
  • Exploring beliefs at your own pace, without pressure toward any particular outcome.
  • Addressing shame, particularly around sexuality and identity, as described on the page about 2SLGBTQ+-affirming therapy.
  • Grieving losses, such as community, family relationships, or certainty.
  • Building new sources of meaning and connection.
  • Trauma-informed therapy for symptoms such as anxiety, intrusive thoughts, or nightmares, as described on the page about trauma-informed therapy.

When to Seek Support

Consider support if religious experiences continue to cause fear, shame, anxiety, or relationship difficulties. A therapist who is respectful of all beliefs and experienced with religious trauma can help you make sense of your experiences without judgment. If abuse occurred, the Abuse Info Hub offers information and resources.

Common Questions About Religious Trauma

Is religious trauma about faith itself or harmful experiences?

Religious trauma refers to harmful experiences in a religious setting, such as fear-based teaching, control, or abuse, not faith itself.

Is religious trauma a real diagnosis?

Religious trauma is not a formal diagnosis, but it describes real and recognizable effects of harmful religious experiences.

How does religious trauma affect adults who grew up in strict faith communities?

Adults with religious trauma from strict communities may experience guilt, fear of punishment, shame about sexuality, difficulty trusting themselves, and grief over lost community.

What causes religious trauma?

Religious trauma can be caused by fear-based teachings, shame, strict control, shunning, spiritual abuse, or abuse within religious institutions.

Does religious trauma mean I have to leave my faith?

No. Some people heal while staying in or returning to their faith, while others leave. Healing follows your own path.

What is spiritual abuse?

Spiritual abuse is when religious authority or beliefs are used to manipulate, control, or harm someone.

Is religious trauma linked to scrupulosity?

Religious trauma can overlap with scrupulosity, a form of OCD involving intense fear of sin or moral wrongdoing.

How does religious trauma affect 2SLGBTQ+ people?

2SLGBTQ+ people may experience religious trauma through shame or rejection related to their identity, and affirming support can help.

What does trauma-informed therapy for religious trauma look like?

Trauma-informed therapy for religious trauma respects your beliefs, moves at your pace, and helps address fear, shame, grief, and identity questions without pushing any outcome.

How do I find a therapist for religious trauma?

Look for a therapist who is respectful of all beliefs, trauma-informed, and experienced with religious or spiritual concerns.
