Why Can't I Stop Gambling

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Gambling problems can bring intense financial stress and hopelessness. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Gambling can become hard to stop because of the way it affects the brain's reward system. Unpredictable wins, near misses, and the excitement of the next bet create powerful urges, and losses often lead to chasing, the drive to keep playing to win money back. Stress, boredom, loneliness, and low mood can all make gambling feel like an escape. When gambling continues despite serious harm and repeated attempts to stop, it may be gambling disorder, a recognized and treatable condition.

People describe telling themselves they will stop after one more bet, hiding losses from family, borrowing money to keep playing, or feeling a rush of relief when gambling and then deep shame afterward.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Easy access. Online casinos, sports betting apps, and constant advertising make gambling available at any time, which can turn occasional play into a habit.

Escape from stress or feelings. Gambling can offer distraction from worries, conflict, grief, or loneliness, much like other ways of numbing difficult emotions.

Boredom and the need for excitement. Some people seek the thrill and stimulation gambling provides.

ADHD and impulsivity. Adults with ADHD may find it harder to stop once they start, and more drawn to fast, stimulating games.

Financial pressure. Money worries can create a belief that a big win will solve problems. People who feel anxious about money may be especially vulnerable to this.

Mood episodes. Heavy gambling alongside little need for sleep and high energy can be a sign of hypomania or mania, which should be assessed.

When It Looks More Like Gambling Disorder

Gambling disorder is a behavioural addiction. It involves a persistent pattern of gambling that causes significant problems and feels hard to control. Signs include:

  • Needing to gamble with larger amounts to feel the same excitement.
  • Feeling restless or irritable when trying to cut back.
  • Repeated unsuccessful efforts to stop or control gambling.
  • Frequently thinking about gambling or planning the next bet.
  • Gambling when feeling distressed, anxious, or low.
  • Chasing losses by returning to win back money.
  • Lying to hide gambling, or relying on others to cover debts.
  • Jeopardizing relationships, work, or finances because of gambling.

Gambling disorder often occurs alongside depression, anxiety, and alcohol or substance use. Like other addictions, it is shaped by brain chemistry, environment, and emotional needs, not by weak character. It shares some features with other hard-to-control behaviours, such as compulsive buying and compulsive scrolling. Treatment is effective, and many people recover.

What Can Help

  • Limit access. Delete gambling apps, use blocking software, and consider self-exclusion programs, which are available through provincial gambling providers in BC.
  • Put barriers on money. Ask someone you trust to help manage finances for a while, and limit access to credit.
  • Tell someone. Sharing what is happening reduces secrecy and shame and brings support.
  • Notice triggers. Track when urges arise, such as after stress, payday, or drinking, and plan alternatives.
  • Delay urges. Urges often peak and pass. Waiting and doing something else can help them fade.
  • Use free support services. British Columbia offers free, confidential gambling support and counselling services, and your doctor can help connect you.

Common Questions About Not Being Able to Stop Gambling

Why do I keep gambling even when I keep losing?

Losses often trigger chasing, the urge to win money back. Unpredictable wins and near misses also keep the brain's reward system engaged, making it hard to stop even as losses grow.

What is gambling disorder?

Gambling disorder is a recognized behavioural addiction involving persistent, hard-to-control gambling that causes significant harm to finances, relationships, work, or wellbeing.

What does chasing losses mean?

Chasing losses means continuing to gamble in an attempt to win back money already lost. It is one of the most common signs that gambling is becoming a problem.

Are online sports betting apps more addictive?

They can be riskier because they are always available, fast, and heavily marketed, with in-game betting that allows rapid repeated bets.

Why do near misses make me want to keep playing?

Near misses activate reward pathways in the brain much like wins do, creating a sense that a win is close. This keeps people playing even though outcomes are random.

Can depression or anxiety lead to problem gambling?

Yes. Gambling can become a way to escape difficult feelings. At the same time, gambling losses often worsen depression and anxiety, creating a cycle.

What is self-exclusion from gambling?

Self-exclusion is a voluntary program where you ask to be banned from casinos or online gambling sites for a set period. In BC it is offered through the provincial gambling provider.

How can I tell my family about my gambling problem?

Choose a calm moment, be honest about what has happened, and share what support you need. Many people find that telling a loved one is a turning point toward recovery.

Is gambling addiction treatable?

Yes. Therapy, especially cognitive behavioural approaches, support groups, and practical financial safeguards help many people stop or significantly reduce gambling.

Where can someone in BC get help for gambling?

British Columbia offers free, confidential gambling support and counselling services. A family doctor, a psychologist, or a crisis line can also help connect you with support.
