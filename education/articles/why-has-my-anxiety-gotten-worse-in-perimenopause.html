Why Has My Anxiety Gotten Worse in Perimenopause

You have always been fairly steady, or maybe you have lived with some anxiety but managed it well. Then, somewhere in your forties, something changed. Worry arrives out of nowhere, your heart races at night, small things feel overwhelming, and you wake at 3 a.m. with your mind spinning. You might not even connect it to hormones at first, especially if your periods are still fairly regular.

Increased anxiety is one of the most common, and most overlooked, experiences of perimenopause, the transition years before periods stop completely. Understanding the connection can bring relief and point toward effective support.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Thyroid changes. Thyroid problems become more common in midlife and can cause anxiety, a racing heart, and sleep problems that mimic perimenopause. A simple blood test can check this.

Midlife pressures. Caring for teenagers and ageing parents at the same time, career demands, relationship changes, and financial pressure often peak in the same years, which can leave people feeling like they have nothing left to give.

Disrupted sleep. Night sweats and lighter sleep in perimenopause lead to tiredness, which lowers tolerance for stress. Many people describe waking up with racing thoughts.

Existing anxiety or ADHD. People with a history of anxiety, premenstrual mood changes, or ADHD often notice symptoms intensifying during this transition.

Heart palpitations. Hormonal shifts can cause palpitations, which can be frightening and set off anxiety about heart health. The general pattern is explored in Why Does My Heart Race for No Reason. New palpitations should always be checked by a doctor.

When It Looks More Like Perimenopause-Related Anxiety

During perimenopause, levels of estrogen and progesterone rise and fall unpredictably, sometimes dramatically from month to month. These hormones interact with brain systems involved in mood, stress, and sleep, so the fluctuations can directly affect anxiety. Progesterone in particular tends to have a calming effect, and its decline can leave some people feeling more on edge.

Signs that perimenopause may be contributing include:

  • Anxiety that is new, or noticeably worse, starting in your late thirties to early fifties.
  • Changes in your menstrual cycle, such as irregular timing or heavier or lighter periods.
  • Hot flashes, night sweats, or new sleep problems.
  • Mood symptoms that fluctuate with your cycle, sometimes resembling premenstrual mood crashes.
  • Irritability, brain fog, or feeling on edge without an obvious reason.

Perimenopause can last several years, and symptoms often vary over time. People who are also going through menopause-related changes in other ways, such as after surgery or cancer treatment, may experience these shifts more suddenly.

What Can Help

  • Talk to a doctor. A family doctor or menopause-informed practitioner can discuss medical options, including hormone therapy and other treatments, and check for thyroid or other causes.
  • Track symptoms. Noting mood, sleep, and cycle changes helps you and your doctor see patterns.
  • Protect sleep. Cooler bedrooms, breathable bedding, and consistent routines can reduce sleep disruption.
  • Move regularly. Exercise supports mood, sleep, and stress regulation during this transition.
  • Reduce alcohol and caffeine. Both can worsen anxiety, hot flashes, and sleep problems.

Common Questions About Anxiety in Perimenopause

Can perimenopause cause anxiety?

Yes. Fluctuating estrogen and progesterone levels during perimenopause affect brain systems involved in mood and stress, and many people experience new or worsening anxiety during this transition.

At what age does perimenopause anxiety usually start?

Perimenopause often begins in the forties, though it can start in the late thirties. Anxiety may appear before other obvious signs, such as major changes to periods.

How do I know if my anxiety is hormonal?

Clues include anxiety that is new or worse in midlife, changes in your cycle, sleep disruption, hot flashes, or mood that fluctuates with your cycle. A doctor can help assess hormonal and other possible causes.

Can perimenopause cause panic attacks?

Some people experience panic attacks for the first time during perimenopause. Hormonal shifts, palpitations, and poor sleep can all contribute. New heart symptoms should be checked medically.

Does hormone therapy help with anxiety in perimenopause?

For some people, hormone therapy can help with mood, sleep, and hot flashes. Whether it is appropriate depends on individual health factors, so it is best discussed with a doctor familiar with menopause care.

Why do I wake up anxious at night during perimenopause?

Night sweats, hormonal shifts, and lighter sleep can cause frequent waking, and the mind may start worrying once awake. Addressing sleep and anxiety together often helps.

Can perimenopause make ADHD symptoms worse?

Many people report that ADHD symptoms such as forgetfulness, focus problems, and emotional reactivity worsen during perimenopause, likely due to hormonal effects on brain chemistry.

Could my symptoms be thyroid instead of perimenopause?

Possibly. Thyroid problems can cause anxiety, heart palpitations, sleep issues, and weight changes that overlap with perimenopause. A blood test can check thyroid function.

Does CBT help with perimenopause symptoms?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy has evidence for reducing anxiety, improving sleep, and helping people cope with hot flashes and other symptoms during the menopause transition.

Will perimenopause anxiety go away after menopause?

For many people, anxiety linked to hormonal fluctuation eases once hormone levels stabilize after menopause. Support during the transition can make the process more manageable.
