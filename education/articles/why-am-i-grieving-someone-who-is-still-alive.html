Why Am I Grieving Someone Who Is Still Alive

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Your parent is still here, but the person you knew is slipping away with dementia. A partner's illness has changed everything about your life together. A sibling lost to addiction is alive but unreachable, or a family member no longer speaks to you. You feel sadness, longing, and loss, yet there has been no funeral, no clear ending, and few people seem to recognize what you are going through. You may even feel guilty for grieving at all.

Grieving someone who is still alive is real grief. It is common, often misunderstood, and can be especially hard because it has no clear shape or timeline.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Caregiver exhaustion. Caring for someone who is ill can leave you drained and feeling like you have nothing left to give, which can blend with grief.

Depression. Ongoing loss can lead to low mood, loss of interest, and hopelessness, especially without support.

Relationship change. Sometimes what you are grieving is the relationship you had, or hoped to have, rather than the person themselves.

Mixed emotions. Anger, frustration, relief, and guilt often sit alongside sadness. These are normal and do not mean you love the person any less.

When It Looks More Like Anticipatory or Ambiguous Grief

There are two closely related types of grief for someone still living. Anticipatory grief is mourning a loss you know is coming, such as when someone has a terminal illness. Ambiguous grief, sometimes called ambiguous loss, happens when someone is physically present but psychologically absent, as with dementia or severe mental illness, or psychologically present but physically absent, as with estrangement.

Signs of this kind of grief include:

  • Sadness and longing for who the person used to be, or for the relationship you had.
  • Grief that comes in waves as new losses appear, such as a parent no longer recognizing you.
  • Guilt about grieving someone who is still alive, or about wishing it were over.
  • Feeling alone because others do not see this as a loss.
  • Anger, sometimes at the person, which can feel confusing, as explored in Why Do I Feel Angry at the Person Who Died.
  • Periods of feeling numb or disconnected.

Because there is no clear ending, this kind of grief can be harder to process than grief after a death. There may be no rituals, no time off, and no clear permission to mourn.

What Can Help

  • Name it as grief. Recognizing that what you feel is grief can bring relief and self-compassion.
  • Hold both truths. The person is still here, and you have lost something important. Both can be true at once.
  • Find people who understand. Caregiver groups, dementia support organizations, and family support groups can reduce isolation.
  • Create meaning. Small rituals, such as writing letters or sharing memories, can help mark losses others may not see.
  • Look after yourself. Rest, respite care, and support are not selfish. They help you keep going.

Common Questions About Grieving Someone Still Living

Can you grieve someone who is still alive?

Yes. Grieving someone who is still living is common when a person has dementia, a serious illness, addiction, or when a relationship ends through estrangement. It is real grief, even without a death.

What is anticipatory grief?

Anticipatory grief is mourning a loss before it happens, such as when a loved one has a terminal illness. It can include sadness, anxiety, and preparing emotionally for the loss.

What is ambiguous loss?

Ambiguous loss is a loss without clarity or closure, such as when someone is physically present but psychologically changed, or psychologically present but physically absent. It can make grief harder to resolve.

Why do I feel guilty for grieving my parent with dementia?

Many caregivers feel guilty grieving someone who is still here, or for sometimes wishing the suffering would end. These feelings are normal parts of ambiguous grief and do not mean you love them less.

How do I cope with grief over estrangement from a family member?

Acknowledge the loss, even if the estrangement was necessary. Support from people who understand, therapy, and small rituals to mark the loss can help. It is common to feel both relief and sadness.

Is it normal to feel angry at someone I'm caring for?

Yes. Caregiving is demanding, and illness can change how someone behaves. Anger and frustration are common and understandable. Talking about these feelings can reduce guilt and build resilience.

Does anticipatory grief make grief after death easier?

Sometimes it helps people prepare, but it does not replace grief after a death. Many people still experience strong grief when the loss happens, and both deserve support.

How can I grieve a loved one lost to addiction who is still alive?

Grieving the person you used to know, or the relationship you hoped for, is a real loss. Family support groups, therapy, and setting healthy boundaries can help you cope.

Why don't people understand my grief for someone still living?

Society tends to recognize grief mainly after death. Without rituals or clear endings, others may not see your loss. Finding people with similar experiences can help you feel understood.

How can therapy help with ambiguous grief?

Therapy can help you name and validate your loss, work through mixed feelings like guilt and anger, cope with caregiving stress, and find meaning while living with uncertainty.
