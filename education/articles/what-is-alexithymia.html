What Is Alexithymia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Alexithymia is a personality trait or pattern that involves difficulty identifying, describing, and understanding one's own emotions. The word comes from Greek and roughly means no words for emotions. People with alexithymia do have feelings, but they may struggle to recognize what those feelings are, tell them apart from physical sensations, or put them into words. They may also focus more on external details than on inner experience. Alexithymia is not a mental health diagnosis. It exists on a spectrum and is more common among autistic people and people who have experienced trauma. With support, emotional awareness can grow.

What Alexithymia Looks Like

  • Not knowing what you are feeling, or only noticing that you feel good or bad.
  • Confusing emotions with physical sensations, such as mistaking anxiety for hunger or illness.
  • Difficulty answering questions such as how does that make you feel?
  • Avoiding talking about feelings, often because they are hard to name.
  • Being described as logical, practical, or unemotional by others.
  • Emotions building up unnoticed and then surfacing suddenly.
  • Difficulty recognizing others' emotions from subtle cues.
  • Little interest in daydreaming or imagining.

Many people with alexithymia notice physical signs, such as a racing heart or stomach discomfort, without linking them to emotions. This can lead to frequent visits to doctors for symptoms that are partly emotional.

Why It Happens

Alexithymia seems to arise from a combination of factors. Some people appear to be born with a tendency toward it, and it is estimated to be much more common among autistic people than in the general population, as described on the page about autism spectrum disorder. Research suggests some difficulties in autism once attributed to low empathy may be better explained by co-occurring alexithymia.

Early experiences also matter. Children learn to understand emotions when caregivers notice and name feelings for them. When this does not happen, as in childhood emotional neglect, emotional vocabulary and awareness may not develop fully. Trauma can lead people to shut down emotions as protection, and long-term emotional suppression can make feelings harder to access. Alexithymia is also linked with depression, anxiety, eating disorders, substance use, and some neurological conditions.

Alexithymia can make relationships challenging. Partners may feel that the person is distant or uncaring, while the person themselves may feel confused about what they are supposed to be feeling. Couples often benefit from understanding that the difficulty lies in recognizing and naming emotions, not in caring.

Everyday situations can reveal alexithymia. Someone may realize they were upset only after snapping at a colleague, or discover they were anxious when a headache appears after a stressful meeting. Noticing these after-the-fact clues can be a starting point for building awareness in the moment.

What It Is Not

  • Not a lack of emotions. People with alexithymia feel emotions but have difficulty recognizing and describing them.
  • Not a lack of caring. Many people with alexithymia care deeply about others.
  • Not a diagnosis. It is a trait that exists on a spectrum.
  • Not the same as autism, though it is common among autistic people.
  • Not fixed. Emotional awareness can be developed.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

Questionnaires such as the Toronto Alexithymia Scale can help measure alexithymia, alongside a clinical conversation. Support focuses on building emotional awareness gradually. Helpful strategies include noticing body sensations and linking them to possible emotions, using emotion wheels or lists to expand vocabulary, and journalling about situations and reactions. Therapy can provide a structured space to practise. Approaches that emphasize body awareness, mindfulness, and emotion-focused work can help. For autistic people, neurodiversity-affirming approaches that respect different ways of experiencing emotions are important. Over time, many people become more able to recognize and communicate what they feel.

Common Questions About Alexithymia

Is there a name for not knowing what I am feeling?

Yes. Alexithymia is difficulty identifying, describing, and understanding your own emotions. It is a trait rather than a diagnosis.

Do people with alexithymia have feelings?

Yes. They experience emotions but find it hard to recognize, name, or describe them.

How common is alexithymia in autism?

Alexithymia is estimated to be much more common among autistic people than in the general population.

Can trauma cause alexithymia?

Trauma and childhood emotional neglect can contribute, as shutting down emotions may have been protective.

Why do I confuse emotions with physical sensations?

People with alexithymia often notice bodily signs of emotion, such as a racing heart, without linking them to feelings.

How is alexithymia measured?

Questionnaires such as the Toronto Alexithymia Scale are commonly used, along with clinical conversation.

Does alexithymia affect relationships?

It can. Partners may feel distant from someone who struggles to describe emotions, even when care is present.

How can I become more aware of my emotions?

Notice body sensations, use emotion wheels to expand vocabulary, journal about reactions, and practise in therapy.

Is alexithymia the same as lacking empathy?

No. Alexithymia concerns recognizing one's own emotions. Some difficulties once linked with low empathy may reflect alexithymia instead.

Can people learn to recognize their emotions later in life?

Yes. Therapy that focuses on body awareness, mindfulness, and emotional vocabulary can build awareness over time.
