Finding an ADHD-Informed Therapist

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

An ADHD-informed therapist is a mental health professional who understands how ADHD affects attention, organization, emotions, relationships, and self-esteem, and who adapts therapy to fit how ADHD brains work. This matters because traditional therapy can unintentionally clash with ADHD, for example by relying on remembering homework, sitting through long unstructured sessions, or treating missed appointments as a lack of commitment. An informed therapist offers practical strategies, understands the emotional side of ADHD, and helps people build systems that work. Finding the right fit can make therapy far more effective and far less discouraging.

Why ADHD Experience Matters

ADHD affects more than focus. Many adults with ADHD live with years of feeling misunderstood, criticized, or labelled as lazy. Common experiences include shame, anxiety, low self-esteem, rejection sensitivity, and difficulty regulating emotions. Therapists without ADHD knowledge may focus on these symptoms without recognizing the underlying ADHD, or suggest strategies that rely on the very skills ADHD makes harder.

An ADHD-informed therapist recognizes how executive function differences shape daily life, understands common co-occurring conditions such as anxiety and depression, and knows how ADHD shows up differently across people, including those who mask their ADHD or were diagnosed later in life. They also understand how therapy fits alongside other treatments, as outlined on the page about how ADHD is treated in adults.

What to Look For

Specific ADHD Experience

Look for therapists who describe training or experience working with ADHD, not just general interest. Some have training in CBT adapted for ADHD or ADHD-focused approaches.

A Practical, Flexible Style

Many people with ADHD benefit from a structured but flexible approach, with clear goals, summaries, and concrete strategies. An informed therapist may offer written notes, reminders, or shorter check-ins between sessions.

Attention to Emotions and Shame

Good ADHD therapy goes beyond time management. It addresses self-criticism, relationships, and the emotional impact of years of struggle.

A Non-Judgmental Approach

An informed therapist understands that missed tasks or forgotten appointments are often ADHD-related and works with you on systems rather than blaming you.

Collaboration With Other Providers

If you take medication or see a coach, a good therapist can coordinate with them, with your consent.

Questions to Ask

  • What experience do you have working with adults with ADHD?
  • How do you adapt therapy for ADHD?
  • Do you help with practical strategies as well as emotions?
  • How do you handle missed sessions or forgotten homework?
  • Can you also assess for ADHD or other conditions, or refer for assessment?

It is fine to meet with a few therapists before choosing. Feeling understood matters.

Common Obstacles

The process of finding a therapist, researching options, sending emails, and booking appointments, can itself be hard with ADHD. Breaking it into small steps, setting a timer, or asking a friend to sit with you while you make calls can help. Cost and wait times may be barriers. Checking workplace benefits, asking about sliding-scale fees, and considering virtual therapy can widen options within BC.

Some people feel discouraged by past therapy that did not help. A different approach, or a therapist with ADHD experience, may make a significant difference. If you do not yet have a diagnosis, some therapists can help you explore whether an ADHD assessment might be useful.

When More Support Is Needed

If ADHD comes with significant depression, anxiety, substance use, or thoughts of suicide, a broader care team may help, including a physician or psychiatrist. Medication is prescribed by a physician and often works well alongside therapy. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Finding an ADHD Therapist

What does ADHD-informed therapy mean?

It means therapy delivered by someone who understands ADHD and adapts sessions, strategies, and expectations to fit how ADHD brains work.

Why might regular therapy not work well for my ADHD?

Therapy that relies on remembering homework or long unstructured sessions can clash with ADHD, leading to frustration.

What questions should I ask a potential ADHD therapist?

Ask about their ADHD experience, how they adapt therapy, whether they offer practical strategies, and how they handle missed sessions.

Can an ADHD therapist help with shame and self-esteem?

Yes. Good ADHD therapy addresses shame, self-criticism, and the emotional impact of years of struggle.

How can I find a therapist when ADHD makes the search overwhelming?

Break the search into small steps, set a timer, or ask a friend to sit with you while you contact therapists.

Should my therapist know if I take ADHD medication?

Yes. Sharing this helps your therapist understand your treatment and coordinate care with your consent.

Do I need an ADHD diagnosis before seeing an ADHD-informed therapist?

No. Some therapists can help you explore whether an assessment might be useful.

What if I keep forgetting my therapy homework?

An informed therapist will work with you on reminders and systems rather than seeing it as a lack of effort.

Would video sessions from home be easier for me to show up to with ADHD?

Yes. Virtual therapy can widen your options and may be easier to fit into your schedule.

Is an ADHD coach the same as an ADHD therapist?

No. Coaches focus on goals and accountability, while therapists also address emotions and mental health.
