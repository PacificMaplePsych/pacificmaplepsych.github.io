Emotion-Focused Therapy (EFT) for Individuals and Couples

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Emotion-focused therapy (EFT) is an evidence-based approach that treats emotions as central to how people make sense of their lives and relationships. Rather than trying to eliminate difficult feelings, EFT helps people notice, understand, and work with them, so emotions can guide healthier choices. It has two main forms. Emotion-focused therapy for individuals helps people process painful feelings such as shame, sadness, and fear. Emotionally focused couples therapy helps partners understand the emotional cycles behind their conflicts and rebuild a secure bond. Both forms were developed in part by researchers in Canada and have been studied for decades.

How Emotion-Focused Therapy Works

For Individuals

Emotion-focused therapy for individuals is based on the idea that emotions carry important information about needs. Some emotions are healthy and adaptive, such as sadness at a loss that helps a person grieve, or anger that helps them set a boundary. Others are painful and stuck, often formed through early or repeated experiences, such as a core sense of shame or of being unlovable. These stuck feelings can drive anxiety, depression, and harsh self-criticism.

EFT helps people access these deeper feelings in a safe way, understand what they need, and transform painful emotions by connecting with more adaptive ones, such as self-compassion or protective anger. The phrase often used is that emotion changes emotion. Therapists help people build emotional regulation skills alongside this work.

For Couples

Emotionally focused couples therapy draws on attachment theory, the idea that adults need to feel emotionally safe and connected to a partner. Conflict often reflects fears of disconnection. Partners can get caught in a negative cycle, such as one partner pursuing and criticizing while the other withdraws, as described on the page about the pursue-withdraw pattern. EFT helps couples see the cycle as the shared problem, rather than blaming each other. Therapists help partners understand the vulnerable feelings underneath, such as fear of being unimportant or of failing, and express these in ways that invite closeness.

What Sessions Are Like

Individual Sessions

Sessions focus on present feelings and recent experiences that stirred strong emotions. A therapist may slow down a moment, ask what you notice in your body, and help you name the feeling and what it needs. Some sessions use experiential exercises, such as imagining speaking to someone who hurt you, or having a dialogue between a self-critical part and the part that feels criticized. EFT is often shorter-term for concerns like depression, commonly around sixteen to twenty sessions, though longer therapy is common for trauma or complex patterns.

Couples Sessions

Couples usually attend together. Early sessions explore the relationship history and map the negative cycle. As therapy continues, the therapist guides partners to share deeper feelings and needs directly with each other, and helps the other partner respond with care. Many couples experience moments of renewed understanding and closeness. Couples EFT is often around eight to twenty sessions, depending on the concerns. Some couples also have occasional individual sessions.

In both forms, EFT involves more talking about feelings in the moment than skills homework, though therapists may suggest reflection or practice between sessions.

Who It Can Help

Emotion-focused therapy for individuals has research support for depression and has been used for anxiety, trauma, grief, self-criticism, and relationship difficulties. It suits people who want to understand their feelings more deeply and who feel ready to explore emotions.

Emotionally focused couples therapy is one of the most studied couples approaches, with research suggesting many couples experience improved satisfaction and connection. It can help with recurring conflict, emotional distance, rebuilding trust after a betrayal, and the stress of life changes. It is also used with families. It is not suitable when there is ongoing abuse or coercive control, where individual support and safety planning come first, and resources are listed on the page about abuse resources. People who prefer a highly structured, skills-focused approach may find cognitive behavioural therapy a better fit, and some therapists combine approaches.

Common Questions About Emotion-Focused Therapy

What is the difference between individual EFT and couples EFT?

Individual EFT helps people process and transform painful emotions. Couples EFT helps partners break negative cycles and rebuild connection.

What does emotion changes emotion mean in EFT?

It means painful, stuck feelings can shift when a person connects with more adaptive emotions, such as self-compassion.

How many sessions of emotionally focused couples therapy do couples usually need?

Many couples attend around eight to twenty sessions, depending on their concerns.

Will I have to express strong emotions in front of my partner?

The therapist helps you share deeper feelings at a pace that feels safe, which often brings partners closer.

Can EFT help if my partner and I keep having the same argument?

Yes. EFT helps couples see the negative cycle behind recurring arguments and respond to each other differently.

Is emotion-focused therapy good for depression?

Individual EFT has research support for depression and helps people work with feelings such as shame and sadness.

What happens in an emotion-focused individual session?

You explore recent emotional experiences, notice body sensations, name feelings and needs, and may try experiential exercises.

Can EFT help rebuild trust after an affair?

Couples EFT is often used to help partners heal from betrayal and rebuild emotional safety.

Is emotionally focused therapy suitable when there is abuse in the relationship?

No. Joint sessions are not recommended with ongoing abuse or coercive control. Individual support and safety planning come first.

Where did emotion-focused therapy come from?

Both forms were developed in part by researchers in Canada, drawing on humanistic and attachment research.
