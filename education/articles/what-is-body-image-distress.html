What Is Body Image Distress

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Body image distress can overlap with eating disorders. Significant weight loss, fainting, purging, or thoughts of suicide need prompt care. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Body image is how you see, think, and feel about your body. Body image distress refers to persistent negative thoughts and feelings about your appearance that cause significant unhappiness, shame, or preoccupation, and affect mood, relationships, or daily life. Almost everyone has moments of feeling unhappy with their body, especially in a culture that places heavy emphasis on appearance. Body image distress is more intense and ongoing. It is not a diagnosis on its own, but it is a common feature of eating disorders, body dysmorphic disorder, depression, and anxiety. A more peaceful relationship with your body is possible.

What Body Image Distress Looks Like

  • Frequent negative thoughts or harsh self-talk about your body.
  • Comparing your body with others, in person or online.
  • Hating how you look in photos or avoiding cameras.
  • Avoiding mirrors, or checking them often.
  • Avoiding swimming, intimacy, social events, or certain clothes.
  • Thinking about food and your body much of the day.
  • Letting weight or appearance determine your mood or self-worth.
  • Dieting, excessive exercise, or other attempts to change your body driven by distress.

Why It Happens

Body image is shaped by many influences. Cultural ideals about thinness, muscularity, youth, and particular features are reinforced by media, advertising, and social media. Diet culture teaches that certain bodies are more valuable than others. Comments from family, friends, coaches, or partners about weight or appearance can have lasting effects, especially in childhood and adolescence. Teasing or bullying about the body is a strong risk factor.

Personal factors matter too. Perfectionism, low self-esteem, anxiety, and depression increase vulnerability. Life changes such as puberty, pregnancy, aging, illness, or disability can change how people feel about their bodies. Weight stigma and discrimination based on appearance, size, race, or disability add further pressure. For some people, body image distress becomes very intense and focused on specific features, which may indicate body dysmorphic disorder.

Body image distress often affects relationships. People may avoid intimacy, turn down invitations, or feel self-conscious around partners and friends. Some seek frequent reassurance, which may bring brief relief but rarely changes how they feel about their bodies.

What It Is Not

  • Not vanity. Body image distress often involves deep pain and shame.
  • Not solved by changing your body. Many people feel the same distress even after weight loss or changes in appearance.
  • Not always an eating disorder, though it can be an early sign or part of one.
  • Not limited to any age, body size, or background. Anyone can experience it.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Therapy can help people understand where their body image beliefs come from and develop a more balanced, compassionate relationship with their bodies. Cognitive behavioural therapy for body image helps challenge negative thoughts, reduce checking and avoidance, and gradually face feared situations. Compassion-focused and acceptance-based approaches reduce shame and help people act on values rather than appearance concerns. Body neutrality, focusing on what the body does rather than how it looks, can reduce pressure. Practical steps include curating social media, limiting body checking, wearing comfortable clothing, and spending time on activities that bring joy. If eating or exercise has become disordered, specialized eating disorder treatment may be recommended.

Common Questions About Body Image Distress

What is body image distress?

It is persistent negative thoughts and feelings about your appearance that cause significant unhappiness and affect daily life.

Is body image distress the same as an eating disorder?

No, but it is a common feature of eating disorders and can be an early warning sign.

Why do I feel bad about my body after using social media?

Curated and edited images can create unrealistic comparisons, increasing dissatisfaction with your own body.

Will losing weight fix my body image?

Often not. Many people feel the same distress after changing their bodies, because the underlying beliefs remain.

Do I have to love my body to feel better about it?

No. An approach called body neutrality focuses on what your body does and how it feels, rather than how it looks.

Can comments from family affect body image?

Yes. Comments about weight or appearance, especially in childhood, can shape body image for years.

How is body image distress different from body dysmorphic disorder?

BDD involves intense preoccupation with specific perceived flaws and repetitive behaviours. Body image distress can be more general.

Why do I avoid swimming or intimacy because of my body?

Shame and fear of being seen can lead to avoidance, which often keeps body image distress going.

How can I reduce body checking?

Notice when and why you check, set limits, and gradually reduce checking with support from therapy if needed.

Which approaches help people make peace with their appearance?

Cognitive behavioural therapy for body image, compassion-focused therapy, and acceptance-based approaches are commonly helpful.
