Understanding Trauma-Informed Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Trauma-informed therapy is an approach to mental health care that recognizes how common trauma is, understands how it can affect thoughts, emotions, the body, and relationships, and shapes therapy to feel safe and empowering. It is not a single technique. Instead, it is a way of practising that prioritizes safety, choice, trust, collaboration, and respect for each person's experience and culture. Trauma-informed care aims to avoid re-traumatizing people, for example by pushing them to share details before they are ready. It can be combined with specific trauma-focused treatments, such as cognitive processing therapy or EMDR, as well as many other therapy approaches.

How Trauma-Informed Therapy Works

Trauma-informed therapy is guided by several principles:

  • Safety. Creating a physically and emotionally safe space, where people know what to expect.
  • Trustworthiness and transparency. Being clear about how therapy works, what information is kept private, and why certain approaches are suggested.
  • Choice and control. Letting people decide what to share, when, and how, and inviting them to pause or stop at any time.
  • Collaboration. Working together as partners, rather than the therapist directing everything.
  • Empowerment. Recognizing strengths and resilience, not just difficulties.
  • Cultural humility. Understanding how culture, identity, history, and systemic factors shape experiences of trauma and healing.

Trauma-informed therapists understand how trauma can affect the nervous system, leading to hypervigilance, shutdown, emotional flashbacks, or difficulty trusting others. These reactions are explained on the pages about trauma responses and the window of tolerance. Rather than seeing symptoms as flaws, trauma-informed therapy sees them as adaptations to difficult experiences.

Therapy often follows a phased approach. The first phase focuses on safety, stabilization, and building coping skills. When a person feels ready, therapy may move into processing traumatic memories, using approaches such as cognitive processing therapy or prolonged exposure, EMDR, or somatic therapies. A final phase focuses on integration, relationships, and building a meaningful life.

What Sessions Are Like

Early sessions often focus on getting to know each other, understanding your goals, and building trust. A trauma-informed therapist will explain how therapy works, what confidentiality means, and your right to decline questions. You do not have to share the details of your trauma to start therapy.

Sessions may include learning grounding and regulation skills, noticing body sensations, and understanding how trauma affects you. Therapists check in often about how you are feeling and adjust the pace. If distress becomes too intense, they may help you slow down and return to a sense of safety. You may be invited to give feedback about what is and is not helpful.

The length of therapy varies. Some people focus on stabilization and skills for a few months, while others with complex trauma may work with a therapist longer.

Who It Can Help

Trauma-informed therapy can help anyone, since many people have experienced trauma, whether or not they identify with that word. It is particularly helpful for people with PTSD, complex PTSD, childhood trauma, abuse, loss, medical trauma, or experiences of discrimination, and for those who have felt unsafe or unheard in past care. The broader picture of trauma is explained on the page about trauma. It is also a useful foundation for therapy with anyone, since trauma is common and not always disclosed.

For people who are currently in unsafe situations, safety planning comes first, and resources are available on the page about abuse resources. When trauma symptoms are severe, specialized trauma-focused treatments are often recommended alongside a trauma-informed approach. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Trauma-Informed Therapy

What does trauma-informed mean in therapy?

It means therapy that recognizes how trauma affects people and prioritizes safety, choice, trust, and collaboration.

Is trauma-informed therapy the same as trauma-focused therapy?

No. Trauma-informed describes a way of practising, while trauma-focused therapies specifically process traumatic memories.

Can I start seeing a trauma-informed therapist without explaining what happened to me?

Yes. You decide what to share and when, and you can begin therapy without sharing details.

What are the principles of trauma-informed care?

Safety, trustworthiness, choice, collaboration, empowerment, and cultural humility are core principles.

What is phased trauma therapy?

It moves through stabilization and skill-building, then processing trauma when ready, then integration and growth.

How does a trauma-informed therapist handle distress in session?

They check in often, adjust the pace, and help you return to a sense of safety when distress becomes intense.

Can trauma-informed therapy help if I am not sure I experienced trauma?

Yes. The approach can help anyone, whether or not they identify their experiences as trauma.

Why do trauma-informed therapists ask for my feedback?

Feedback helps ensure therapy feels safe and useful, and supports your sense of choice and control.

What therapies can be combined with a trauma-informed approach?

Cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, EMDR, somatic approaches, and many others can be delivered in a trauma-informed way.

How long does trauma-informed therapy take?

It varies. Some people focus on skills for a few months, while complex trauma may need longer-term work.
