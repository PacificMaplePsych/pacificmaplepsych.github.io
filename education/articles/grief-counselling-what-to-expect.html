Grief Counselling: What to Expect

Grief counselling is a form of therapy that helps people cope with loss, most often the death of someone close, but also other losses such as divorce, estrangement, a pet, health, or a way of life. A counsellor offers a safe place to talk about the person or what was lost, understand grief reactions, and find ways to carry the loss while continuing to live. Grief is a natural response, not an illness, and many people move through it with the support of friends and family. Counselling can help when grief feels overwhelming, isolating, or stuck, or when there is no one else to talk to.

How Grief Counselling Works

Grief counselling rests on the understanding that grief is a process of adjusting to a changed world. Modern ideas suggest that people move back and forth between focusing on the loss, such as crying, remembering, and longing, and focusing on rebuilding life, such as new routines, roles, and relationships. Both are needed. Grief does not follow fixed stages, as explained on the page about grief and its stages.

Counsellors help people:

  • Tell the story of the loss and the relationship.
  • Express feelings such as sadness, anger, guilt, relief, and numbness.
  • Understand that their reactions are normal.
  • Work through regrets or unfinished conversations.
  • Find ways to remember and stay connected to the person, sometimes called continuing bonds.
  • Adjust to practical changes and new roles.
  • Rediscover meaning and purpose.

Counsellors may draw on approaches such as meaning-centred therapy, narrative therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, and emotion-focused therapy. For grief that stays intense and disabling, specialized treatments for prolonged grief can help.

What Sessions Are Like

The first session usually involves talking about the loss, what has happened since, how you are coping, and what you hope counselling can help with. The counsellor may ask about the person who died, your relationship, and your support network. You can share as much or as little as feels right.

Later sessions follow your needs. Some sessions may focus on memories and emotions, others on practical challenges, such as sleep, work, or handling reminders. Counsellors may suggest writing a letter to the person, looking at photos, creating a ritual, or planning for difficult dates, as described on the page about grief during holidays and anniversaries. It is common to cry in sessions, and also to laugh while sharing memories.

Grief counselling can be short-term, a handful of sessions, or longer-term, depending on the loss and your needs. Some people come for a few months, stop, and return around significant times. Counselling can be individual, with family members, or in a grief group.

Many people worry that counselling will make them feel worse. Sessions can bring up strong feelings, but most people find that having space to grieve brings relief over time.

Who It Can Help

Grief counselling can help anyone who is grieving, especially when:

  • The loss was sudden, traumatic, or violent.
  • The loss was to suicide or overdose.
  • The relationship was complicated, such as with estrangement or conflict.
  • The loss is not widely recognized, such as a miscarriage, a pet, or an ex-partner.
  • Grief feels overwhelming, isolating, or unchanging over time.
  • There are other stressors, such as caregiving, financial strain, or mental health concerns.
  • Someone is facing a loss ahead of time, as described on the page about anticipatory grief.

When loss is traumatic, trauma-focused therapy may also be helpful. When grief comes with significant depression, a doctor may be involved as well.

Common Questions About Grief Counselling

What happens at my first grief counselling appointment?

You usually talk about the loss, how you are coping, and what you hope counselling can help with, sharing as much as feels right.

Is grief counselling only for the death of a person?

No. It can help with many losses, including divorce, estrangement, pets, health, and life changes.

Will I be seeing a grief counsellor for weeks, months, or years?

It varies from a handful of sessions to longer-term support, and some people return around significant dates.

Will talking about my loss in grief counselling make me feel worse at first?

Sessions can bring up strong feelings, but most people find space to grieve brings relief over time.

Will my counsellor expect me to let go of my connection to the person I lost?

They are ways of staying connected to someone who died, such as memories, rituals, or conversations.

Is grief counselling different from regular therapy?

It focuses specifically on loss and adjustment, though counsellors draw on many therapy approaches.

Should I wait a while after a death before starting counselling?

There is no set time. Some people start soon after a loss, while others begin months or years later.

My brother died of an overdose and I feel angry and guilty. Is counselling right for that?

Yes. These losses often bring complex feelings, and specialized support can help.

Can I bring family members to grief counselling?

Some counsellors offer family sessions, which can help families grieve together and understand each other.

How do I know if my grief needs counselling?

If grief feels overwhelming, isolating, or unchanging, or you have no one to talk to, counselling can help.
