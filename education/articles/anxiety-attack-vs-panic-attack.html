Anxiety Attack vs. Panic Attack

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes, with strong physical symptoms such as a pounding heart, shortness of breath, dizziness, and a sense of losing control or dying. It can happen unexpectedly, even during calm moments. "Anxiety attack" is not a clinical term, but people usually use it to describe a period of building worry and tension in response to a stressor, which can be distressing but tends to be less sudden and less intense than panic. In everyday speech the terms overlap, and many people who say they had an anxiety attack actually experienced a panic attack. Both are treatable, and recognizing which you experience can guide what helps.

How They Differ

Onset

Panic attacks come on abruptly and reach their peak within about ten minutes. Anxiety typically builds gradually, often over hours or days.

Triggers

Panic attacks can be unexpected or triggered by specific situations. Anxiety is usually linked to a worry or stressor, such as an upcoming exam or conflict.

Intensity

Panic attacks involve intense fear and strong physical symptoms. Anxiety may feel uncomfortable and persistent rather than overwhelming.

Duration

Panic attacks usually pass within minutes to half an hour, although people may feel shaky afterward. Anxiety can linger for longer periods.

Fear of Dying or Losing Control

This is common in panic attacks and less typical in anxiety.

Why the Words Matter

Using the right term can help you get the right kind of help. If you describe "anxiety attacks" to a doctor or therapist, it helps to explain whether the experience came on suddenly with intense physical symptoms or built gradually with worry and tension. That description guides assessment and treatment. Sudden, intense episodes point toward panic, which responds well to approaches that reduce fear of body sensations. Gradual, worry-driven anxiety may point toward generalized anxiety or stress, which responds to strategies for worry and problem-solving. Many people experience both at different times, and therapy can address each.

Common Symptoms

Panic attacks may include a racing heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, dizziness, sweating, trembling, tingling, nausea, feeling detached, and fear of dying or going crazy.

Anxiety may include worry, restlessness, muscle tension, irritability, trouble concentrating, and difficulty sleeping, as described on the page about anxiety vs. stress.

When It Might Be a Disorder

Occasional panic attacks or periods of anxiety are common. Recurrent unexpected panic attacks, with ongoing worry about having more, may indicate panic disorder, as described on the page about panic attacks vs. panic disorder. Persistent, hard-to-control worry about many things may indicate generalized anxiety disorder, as described on the page about generalized anxiety disorder.

What Helps

During a Panic Attack

Name it, stay where you are if safe, breathe slowly with a longer exhale, and let the wave pass, as described on the page about what to do during a panic attack.

When Anxiety Is Building

Identify the worry, use problem-solving for what you can change, and practise calming skills, such as grounding techniques or breathing exercises.

Longer Term

Cognitive behavioural therapy is highly effective for both panic and anxiety. Exercise, sleep, and limiting caffeine and alcohol also help.

What It Is Not

Neither panic attacks nor anxiety are dangerous, though they feel awful. Because panic symptoms can resemble heart or breathing problems, seek medical care if symptoms are new, different, or include chest pain or fainting.

Common Questions About Anxiety Attacks and Panic Attacks

Are anxiety attacks and panic attacks the same thing?

No. A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear that peaks within minutes, while an anxiety attack usually describes worry and tension that build gradually around a stressor.

Is anxiety attack a medical term?

No. Anxiety attack is not a clinical term, while panic attack is a recognized term in mental health diagnosis.

Does anxiety build slowly while panic comes on suddenly?

Yes. Anxiety usually builds gradually in response to a worry, while a panic attack comes on suddenly and peaks within minutes.

Can an anxiety attack happen without a trigger?

Anxiety usually has an identifiable worry or stressor, while panic attacks are the ones that can strike without any warning.

Is an anxiety attack mostly worry, restlessness, and tension?

Usually, yes. What people call an anxiety attack often involves growing worry, restlessness, muscle tension, and irritability in response to a stressor, rather than a sudden peak of fear.

Are panic attacks worse than anxiety attacks?

Panic attacks are usually more sudden and intense than anxiety attacks, though both can be very distressing.

Can anxiety turn into a panic attack?

Yes. Building anxiety can sometimes lead to a panic attack, especially when physical symptoms trigger more fear.

How do I stop an anxiety attack?

To ease an anxiety attack, identify the worry, use slow breathing or grounding, and focus on what you can control.

Should panic attack symptoms be checked by a doctor?

Yes, if panic attack symptoms are new, different, include chest pain or fainting, or happen often, a doctor should check them.

Does CBT treat both anxiety attacks and panic attacks?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy is highly effective for both anxiety attacks and panic attacks, with skills tailored to each pattern.
