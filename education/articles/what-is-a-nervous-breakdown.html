What Is a Nervous Breakdown

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

"Nervous breakdown" is not a medical or psychological diagnosis, but it is a common way of describing a point where stress becomes so overwhelming that a person can no longer function as usual. People who say they are having a breakdown may be unable to work, stop sleeping or eating normally, cry uncontrollably, feel panicky or numb, or withdraw from others. What is happening underneath is often a recognized condition, such as depression, an anxiety disorder, burnout, acute stress, or a trauma response. The term can be useful because it signals that someone needs help, and with the right support, people recover.

What People Usually Mean

When someone says they are having a nervous breakdown, they often mean:

  • They cannot cope with daily demands, such as work, parenting, or household tasks.
  • Their emotions feel out of control, with frequent crying, anger, or panic.
  • Their body is affected, with exhaustion, insomnia, appetite changes, headaches, or a racing heart.
  • They feel detached or numb, as though watching life from a distance.
  • They are pulling away from friends, family, or responsibilities.

What May Be Happening Underneath

Burnout

Prolonged work or caregiving stress can lead to exhaustion, cynicism, and a sense of having nothing left, as described on the page about what burnout is.

Depression

Low mood, loss of interest, fatigue, and hopelessness that last for weeks may point to depression, as described on the page about what depression is.

Anxiety and Panic

Constant worry, panic attacks, and feeling on edge can become overwhelming, as described on the page about what an anxiety disorder is.

Acute Stress or Trauma

A sudden shock, loss, or traumatic event can trigger intense reactions, as described on the page about acute stress disorder.

Adjustment Difficulties

Major life changes, such as divorce or job loss, can lead to symptoms that are more severe than expected, as described on the page about situational depression and adjustment disorder.

What It Is Not

A nervous breakdown is not a sign of weakness or a permanent state. It is also not the same as psychosis, although in rare cases extreme stress can contribute to losing touch with reality, which needs urgent care. Feeling at your limit is a signal that something needs to change, not proof that you have failed.

What Helps in the Moment

  • Reduce demands. Postpone non-essential tasks and ask for help.
  • Cover the basics. Try to eat, drink water, rest, and get outside briefly.
  • Reach out. Talk to someone you trust and let them know how you are doing.
  • Calm the body. Slow breathing and grounding techniques can help in intense moments.
  • See your doctor. A physician can check for medical causes, discuss medication, and help with time off work.

Recovering

Recovery usually involves identifying what led to the crisis, treating any underlying condition, and making changes to reduce ongoing stress. Therapy can help you understand your limits, rebuild routines, and develop coping strategies. Some people benefit from time away from work, as described on the page about taking a mental health leave from work in BC. With support, many people come out of a breakdown with a clearer sense of what they need.

Common Questions About Nervous Breakdowns

What do people mean when they say they are having a nervous breakdown?

People who say they are having a nervous breakdown usually mean that stress has become so overwhelming they cannot function as usual.

Is a nervous breakdown a real diagnosis?

No. Nervous breakdown is not a medical diagnosis, but it often reflects conditions such as depression, anxiety, burnout, or acute stress.

What are the signs of a nervous breakdown?

Signs of a nervous breakdown include being unable to cope with daily tasks, uncontrollable crying or panic, exhaustion, insomnia, and withdrawing from others.

What causes a nervous breakdown?

A nervous breakdown is usually caused by prolonged or overwhelming stress, sometimes combined with depression, anxiety, trauma, or major life changes.

How long does a nervous breakdown last?

How long a nervous breakdown lasts varies, but with support and treatment many people start to feel better within weeks to months.

What should I do if I think I am having a nervous breakdown?

Reduce demands, cover basic needs, reach out to someone you trust, and see a doctor or therapist for support.

Is burnout the same as a nervous breakdown?

Burnout can lead to what people call a nervous breakdown, but a breakdown can also stem from depression, anxiety, or trauma.

Can a nervous breakdown cause physical symptoms?

Yes. A nervous breakdown can cause exhaustion, insomnia, appetite changes, headaches, and a racing heart.

Can I take time off work for a nervous breakdown?

Yes. A health professional can assess whether time off or accommodations are needed, and BC has several leave options.

Can therapy help after a nervous breakdown?

Yes. Therapy can help identify causes, treat underlying conditions, rebuild routines, and develop coping strategies after a breakdown.
