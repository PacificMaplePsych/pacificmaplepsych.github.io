Coping With Separation and Divorce

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Coping with separation and divorce usually involves allowing yourself to grieve, looking after your basic wellbeing, leaning on support, managing practical changes one step at a time, and gradually rebuilding a life and identity that fits your new circumstances. Separation can bring sadness, anger, relief, guilt, fear, and loneliness, often all at once, whether you chose to leave or not. It also brings practical upheaval, such as moving, finances, and parenting arrangements. These reactions are normal. Most people adjust over time, and many eventually find new stability and meaning.

Why Separation Is So Hard

Separation involves multiple losses at once: a partner, shared routines, future plans, sometimes a home, friendships, financial security, and a sense of identity. Even when a relationship was unhappy, its ending can bring grief. The emotional experience is explored on the page about struggling after a separation or divorce.

Conflict, legal processes, and co-parenting can add stress, and decisions often need to be made while emotions are high. Children, if involved, may need extra support. Many people also find their sense of self shaken, especially after a long relationship, as described on the page about rebuilding identity after a major change.

Strategies to Try

1. Allow Grief

Let yourself feel sadness, anger, and loss, even if you initiated the separation. Grief may come in waves for months.

2. Look After the Basics

Sleep, regular meals, movement, and limiting alcohol help you cope with stress.

3. Lean on Support

Reach out to trusted friends and family, and consider a separation support group. Isolation can make things harder.

4. Take Practical Steps Gradually

Break tasks such as finances, housing, and legal matters into small steps. In BC, family justice services, mediators, and legal aid can provide information. Financial worries are addressed on the page about coping with financial stress.

5. Keep Children Out of Conflict

If you have children, reassure them the separation is not their fault, keep routines, and avoid speaking negatively about the other parent. The page about co-parenting after separation offers more guidance.

6. Set Boundaries With Your Ex

Keep communication brief and focused on necessary matters, especially early on. Limit checking their social media.

7. Delay Big Decisions Where Possible

Major choices, such as moving cities or starting a new serious relationship, may be easier once intense emotions settle.

8. Rebuild Your Life Gradually

Reconnect with interests, try new activities, and build routines that are your own.

9. Be Kind to Yourself

Self-blame is common. Reflect on lessons without harsh judgment.

Common Obstacles

High-conflict separations can make coping much harder, with ongoing disputes over children, property, or communication. Keeping written records, using mediators, and limiting direct contact can help. Some people feel pressure from family or culture to stay together, or shame about divorce. Others struggle with loneliness, especially when friends take sides. Financial strain can be significant, particularly for one-income households. Seeing an ex move on quickly can also be painful. Focusing on your own healing, rather than comparison, helps.

If the relationship involved abuse or coercive control, separation can be a time of increased risk. Safety planning is important, and resources are available on the pages about abuse resources and safety planning. If you are in danger, call 911.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If you feel persistently low, anxious, hopeless, or unable to function, if you are drinking more to cope, or if you have thoughts of suicide, professional support can help. Therapy offers space to process grief, manage conflict, support children, and rebuild your life. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Coping With Separation and Divorce

Is it normal to grieve a divorce I wanted?

Yes. Even when you chose the separation, divorce involves losses of routines, plans, and identity that can bring grief.

Why does separation grief come back in waves months later?

Separation involves many losses, and reminders such as anniversaries or holidays can bring grief back. Recovery often takes many months, with waves along the way.

Where can I find company and support in the first months after a separation?

Reach out to friends and family, join a separation support group, and build new routines and activities after the breakup.

Where can I get information about separation and divorce in BC?

BC family justice services, mediators, and legal aid can provide information about separation, divorce, and parenting arrangements.

How should I communicate with my ex after a separation?

Keep communication with your ex brief, respectful, and focused on necessary matters, especially early after the separation.

What do kids most need to hear when their parents separate?

Children need to hear that the separation is not their fault and that both parents still love them, alongside steady routines and no negative talk about the other parent.

Should I make big decisions right after a divorce?

Where possible, delay major decisions until intense emotions from the divorce have settled.

Why does seeing my ex move on hurt so much?

Seeing an ex move on can reopen grief after a separation. Focusing on your own healing rather than comparison helps.

Is separation riskier when a relationship was abusive?

Yes. Separation from an abusive partner can increase risk, so safety planning is important. Call 911 if you are in danger.

When should I see a therapist during a separation?

If you feel persistently low, anxious, or hopeless, are drinking more, or are struggling to function during a separation, therapy can help.
