How to Know If Therapy Is Working

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Therapy is usually working when you notice gradual changes over weeks or months, such as symptoms easing, coping better in hard moments, understanding yourself more clearly, or relating differently to others, even if some sessions feel difficult. Progress is rarely a straight line. Feeling worse for a while, especially when working through painful experiences, does not necessarily mean therapy is failing. What matters most is the overall direction, a sense of trust with your therapist, and clear goals you are moving toward. If you are unsure, the best first step is to talk about it with your therapist.

What to Expect

Signs that therapy is working often include:

  1. Symptoms are easing. Anxiety, low mood, panic, or other symptoms are less frequent or intense.
  2. You cope better. You handle stressful situations with more skill, recover faster from setbacks, or use new strategies.
  3. You understand yourself more. Patterns, triggers, and reactions make more sense.
  4. Daily life is easier. Sleep, work, relationships, or self-care are improving.
  5. Others notice. Friends or family comment on changes.
  6. You feel safe with your therapist. You can be honest, including about things that are hard to say.
  7. You are moving toward goals. Progress on the goals in your treatment plan, even if slow.

Many therapists use brief questionnaires to track symptoms over time, which can make progress easier to see.

Practical Details

Give it some time. Many people notice early signs of change within the first several sessions, while deeper change can take months. The page about how long therapy usually takes offers a general sense of timelines.

Feeling worse can be part of it. Talking about painful memories or facing fears can temporarily increase distress. This usually eases, and your therapist should help you pace the work.

Signs therapy may not be working. These can include no change after several months, feeling consistently unheard or judged, unclear goals, sessions that feel aimless, or a sense that the approach does not fit your concerns.

Fit matters. Research consistently shows the relationship between client and therapist is one of the strongest predictors of good outcomes. It is reasonable to look for a different therapist if the fit is not right.

What Happens Next

  • Talk about it. Tell your therapist what you have noticed. Good therapists welcome feedback and will adjust.
  • Revisit goals. Make sure you agree on what you are working toward.
  • Consider changes. This might mean a different approach, a different session frequency, as discussed on the page about how often to attend therapy, or adding other supports such as medication through a physician.
  • Consider a new therapist. If concerns continue, a different therapist or specialist may be a better match.
  • Seek more support if needed. If symptoms are worsening or you feel unsafe, more intensive care may be needed. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Whether Therapy Is Working

What are signs that therapy is working?

Signs therapy is working include easing symptoms, coping better, understanding yourself more, easier daily life, and progress toward goals.

Is it normal to feel worse before therapy helps?

Yes. Working through painful experiences can temporarily increase distress in therapy before improvement follows.

How long should I wait before deciding therapy is not working?

Many people notice early changes within several sessions. If there is no change after a few months, talk with your therapist.

What should I do if I feel stuck in therapy?

Tell your therapist what you have noticed, revisit goals, and discuss changing the approach or session frequency.

Is it okay to tell my therapist that therapy is not helping?

Yes. Good therapists welcome feedback about whether therapy is working and will adjust the approach.

Why does the relationship with my therapist matter so much?

Research shows the client-therapist relationship is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy helps.

When should I consider switching to a different therapist?

If you consistently feel unheard, goals remain unclear, or progress stalls despite feedback, a different therapist may be a better fit.

Do therapists measure progress with questionnaires?

Many therapists use brief questionnaires to track symptoms over time, which can make therapy progress easier to see.

Can friends or family notice if therapy is working?

Often yes. Others may notice changes in mood, coping, or how you relate, sometimes before you notice them yourself in therapy.

What if my symptoms are getting worse during therapy?

Tell your therapist right away. More support or intensive care may be needed, and call or text 9-8-8 if you have thoughts of suicide.
