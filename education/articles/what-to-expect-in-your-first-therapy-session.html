What to Expect in Your First Therapy Session

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

In your first therapy session, you and your therapist will mostly get to know each other. The therapist will explain confidentiality and how they work, ask what brings you in, learn about your background and current life, check on safety, and talk with you about your goals. You will usually have time to ask your own questions. It is normal to feel nervous, unsure what to say, or emotional. You do not need to share everything in the first session, and you do not need to have your goals perfectly defined. By the end, you should have a sense of whether this therapist feels like a good fit and what the next sessions may involve.

What to Expect

  1. Consent and confidentiality. The therapist explains how your information is protected, its legal limits, fees, cancellation policies, and how sessions work. The page about whether therapy is confidential explains the details.
  2. What brings you in. You describe what is going on, how long it has been happening, and how it affects you.
  3. Background. Questions about family, relationships, work or school, health, past therapy, and important life events.
  4. Safety questions. Therapists routinely ask about thoughts of self-harm or suicide and about safety at home. This is standard practice.
  5. Strengths and supports. What helps you cope and who is in your corner.
  6. Goals. What you would like to be different, which may later form part of a treatment plan.
  7. Your questions. About the therapist's approach, experience, and what comes next.

Practical Details

Length. First sessions are often about 50 to 60 minutes, and some therapists book a longer intake.

Paperwork. You may be asked to complete intake forms or questionnaires before or after the session.

Format. Sessions may be in person or virtual. For virtual sessions, find a private space and test your technology, as discussed on the page about virtual versus in-person therapy.

Fees and receipts. Ask the clinic about fees, payment, and receipts for your extended health plan before your session.

Feelings afterward. Some people feel relieved, while others feel tired or stirred up. Both are normal.

How to Prepare and Follow Up

  • Jot down a few notes. What you want to talk about and any examples. The page about what to bring to your first appointment has a checklist.
  • Think about what you want from therapy. Even a rough idea helps.
  • Prepare questions. About approach, frequency, length of therapy, and fees.
  • Plan some downtime afterward. A walk or quiet time can help you process.
  • Reflect on fit. Did you feel heard and respected? It is okay to try a different therapist if the fit does not feel right.
  • Book the next session. Many people start weekly, as explained on the page about how often to attend therapy.

If you are in crisis before your appointment, call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About the First Therapy Session

What does a typical first therapy session include?

In a first therapy session, the therapist explains confidentiality, asks what brings you in, learns about your background, checks on safety, and discusses goals.

What should I say in my first therapy session?

In your first session, you can simply describe what is bothering you and why you decided to seek help. The therapist will guide the conversation.

Is it normal to cry in a first therapy session?

Yes. Crying or feeling emotional in a first therapy session is very common, and therapists are comfortable with it.

How many minutes does a first therapy session usually last?

A first therapy session is often about 50 to 60 minutes, and some therapists book a longer intake appointment.

Do I have to talk about my past in a first therapy session?

No. A first therapy session may touch on your background, but you only need to share what you feel ready to share.

Why does a new therapist ask about suicide and safety?

A new therapist routinely asks about suicide and safety with every client. It is standard practice and helps keep care safe.

How do I know if a new therapist is a good fit?

A new therapist may be a good fit if you feel heard, respected, and comfortable asking questions, even if the first session feels awkward.

Which questions are worth asking a new therapist at the start?

In your first therapy session, ask about the therapist's approach, experience with your concern, session frequency, and how progress is reviewed.

How might I feel after my first therapy session?

After a first therapy session, some people feel relieved and others feel tired or stirred up. Both reactions are normal.

What if I do not know what my goals are when starting therapy?

That is okay. Many people start therapy without clear goals, and the therapist can help you clarify them over the first few sessions.
