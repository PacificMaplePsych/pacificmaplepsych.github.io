How to Support Someone Who Is Having Suicidal Thoughts

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If someone is in immediate danger or has already harmed themselves, call 911. For support at any time, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline). You can call 9-8-8 for guidance on supporting someone else. Young people can also contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

Supporting someone who is having suicidal thoughts starts with asking them directly, listening without judgment, helping them stay safe right now, and connecting them with professional help. Many people worry that asking about suicide will put the idea in someone's head. Research shows this is not the case. Asking directly can bring relief and open the door to help. You do not need to be an expert or have the perfect words. Staying calm, taking them seriously, and not leaving them alone with it are among the most helpful things you can do. You also do not have to carry this alone.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

People who have suicidal thoughts often feel overwhelmed by pain, hopelessness, or a sense that they are a burden. They may not truly want to die, but want the pain to stop and cannot see another way. Thoughts can range from passing wishes not to wake up to specific plans. They may come and go. The experience is explained further on the page about suicidal thoughts.

Many people feel ashamed of these thoughts or fear being judged, hospitalized, or seen as weak. This can make it hard to tell anyone. When someone shares suicidal thoughts with you, it is a sign of trust. Some people may express distress indirectly, for example by saying they wish they could disappear, giving away belongings, withdrawing, or talking about being a burden. Some may also be self-harming, which is different from suicide but is a sign of significant distress.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Ask directly. For example, "Are you thinking about suicide?" or "Are you thinking of ending your life?" Clear questions show you can handle the answer.
  • Listen more than you talk. Let them share at their own pace. Reflect what you hear, such as "It sounds like things have felt unbearable."
  • Stay calm and take it seriously. Your steady presence helps.
  • Ask about safety. Gently ask whether they have a plan, access to means, or a timeframe. This helps you understand how urgent the situation is.
  • Reduce access to means. With their agreement where possible, help remove or lock away items that could be used for harm, such as medications or weapons.
  • Help make a plan to stay safe. Talk about who they can contact, what helps them get through hard moments, and what to do if thoughts get stronger.
  • Stay connected. Check in regularly by message, call, or visit.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Minimizing. Phrases such as "you have so much to live for" or "others have it worse" can make people feel unheard.
  • Arguing or lecturing. Debating whether suicide is right or wrong can shut down conversation.
  • Promising secrecy. You may need to involve others to keep them safe. You can say you care too much to keep it secret.
  • Trying to handle it alone. Serious risk needs professional help.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting someone through suicidal thoughts can be frightening and exhausting. It is normal to feel anxious, helpless, or overwhelmed. You are not responsible for another person's choices, and you cannot do this alone. Share the support with others where possible, such as family, friends, or professionals, while respecting privacy as much as safety allows. Talk to someone about your own feelings, such as a counsellor or a crisis line. You can call or text 9-8-8 yourself for guidance. Protecting your sleep and taking breaks helps you stay present. Long-term caregiving can lead to compassion fatigue, which is a sign to seek more support.

Encouraging Professional Help

Encourage the person to talk to a doctor, therapist, or crisis service, and offer practical help, such as finding a counsellor, sitting with them while they call, or going to an appointment together. If they are already in treatment, encourage them to tell their care team about their thoughts.

If the person has a plan, access to means, intends to act soon, or has already harmed themselves, this is an emergency. Call 911 or take them to the nearest emergency department. Stay with them until help arrives if it is safe to do so. For less urgent situations, call or text 9-8-8 for guidance. In BC, you can also call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse. For young people, Kids Help Phone is available at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Common Questions About Supporting Someone With Suicidal Thoughts

I am scared that raising suicide will plant the idea. Is that a real risk?

No. Research shows asking directly does not increase risk and can bring relief and open the door to help.

What words should I use to ask a friend if they are thinking of suicide?

Ask clearly and calmly, such as asking whether they are thinking about suicide or ending their life.

What should I avoid saying to someone who is suicidal?

Avoid minimizing, lecturing, arguing about whether suicide is wrong, or promising to keep it secret.

How do I know when someone's suicidal thoughts have become an emergency?

If they have a plan, access to means, intend to act soon, or have harmed themselves, call 911.

Can I call 9-8-8 for advice about someone else?

Yes. You can call or text 9-8-8 for guidance on how to support someone who is struggling.

If someone tells me they are suicidal and asks me to keep it secret, should I?

You may need to involve others to keep them safe. You can explain that you care too much to keep it secret.

How can I help reduce access to means of suicide for someone I am worried about?

With their agreement where possible, help remove or lock away medications, weapons, or other items that could be used for harm.

How do I cope with the fear of supporting someone who is suicidal?

Share the support with others, talk to a counsellor or crisis line yourself, and take breaks to care for your wellbeing.

What are indirect signs someone may be thinking about suicide?

Talking about being a burden, wishing to disappear, giving away belongings, or withdrawing can be warning signs.

How can I help someone take the first step toward professional help?

Offer to find a counsellor, sit with them while they call, or go with them to an appointment.
