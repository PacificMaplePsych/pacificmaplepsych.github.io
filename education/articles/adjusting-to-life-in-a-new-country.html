Adjusting to Life in a New Country

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Adjusting to life in a new country usually takes months to years, and it tends to go more smoothly when newcomers build routines, find community, keep ties to their culture and language, and allow themselves to grieve what they left behind. Moving countries brings excitement and opportunity, but also practical stress, culture shock, loneliness, and a sense of loss. It is common to feel homesick, unsure of yourself, or out of place for a while, even when the move was chosen. These reactions are a normal part of adjustment, not a sign that the move was a mistake. Support can make the process easier.

Why These Strategies Help

Moving to a new country changes almost everything at once: language, work, social networks, climate, food, systems for health care and banking, and unspoken social rules. People often lose the roles and recognition they had at home, such as a professional identity or a respected place in their community. Many experience what researchers describe as acculturation stress, explored on the page about acculturation stress for newcomers.

Adjustment often moves through phases. An early period of excitement may give way to frustration or homesickness as challenges pile up, then gradually to a sense of adaptation and belonging. These phases are not neat, and people may move back and forth. Strategies that build connection, routine, and a sense of competence help people move through this process, while keeping links to home supports identity and wellbeing. Moving is also one kind of major life transition, with similar emotional patterns.

Strategies to Try

1. Build a Few Routines Early

Regular sleep, meals, exercise, and weekly activities create stability while everything else is new.

2. Find Your People

Connect with others from your home culture through cultural associations, faith communities, or online groups, and also with locals through classes, volunteering, or neighbourhood events. Both kinds of connection matter. Ideas for building friendships are on the page about building social connection when you feel lonely.

3. Use Settlement Services

In BC, free settlement services for newcomers can help with language classes, employment, housing, health care navigation, and community connections. Many operate through neighbourhood houses and community organizations.

4. Learn the Language Gradually

If the local language is new to you, practise a little each day and give yourself credit for progress. Language learning builds confidence and connection.

5. Keep Ties to Home

Regular calls with family, cooking familiar foods, celebrating cultural holidays, and speaking your first language help maintain identity.

6. Expect Culture Shock

Differences in communication, humour, personal space, and workplace norms can feel confusing. Curiosity and asking questions help more than judging yourself or others.

7. Allow Grief

It is normal to miss people, places, smells, and ways of life. Naming this grief helps it move rather than build up.

8. Celebrate Small Wins

Opening a bank account, finding a favourite shop, or having a conversation in a new language are real achievements.

Common Obstacles

Newcomers often face barriers such as recognition of foreign credentials, underemployment, financial pressure, and housing costs. These can affect self-esteem and mood. Loneliness is common, especially in the first year, and making friends as an adult takes time. Some people experience discrimination or feel unwelcome, which adds stress and is not their fault. Family members may adjust at different speeds. Children often pick up language and customs quickly, which can create tension with parents. Talking openly about these differences can help.

Winter darkness can be difficult for people from sunnier climates, and it may affect mood. Getting outside in daylight and staying active can help. Some newcomers also carry experiences of trauma or loss from before they moved, which may surface once daily life is more stable.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If homesickness, low mood, anxiety, or loneliness continue for many months, affect sleep or daily life, or come with hopelessness, therapy can help. Some people find it helpful to work with a therapist who understands migration or shares their language or culture. If past trauma is resurfacing, trauma-informed therapy can help. Loss of identity and role, as described on the page about identity loss after a life transition, can also be explored in therapy. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Adjusting to a New Country

Is it normal to still feel like an outsider two years after immigrating to Canada?

Yes. Adjusting to a new country usually takes months to years, and feeling like an outsider at times is part of the process.

Is homesickness after moving to Canada normal?

Yes. Homesickness is common for newcomers to Canada and often comes in waves, especially around holidays and family events.

What is culture shock and how long does it last?

Culture shock is the confusion and stress of adjusting to unfamiliar norms in a new country. It usually eases gradually over months.

Where can newcomers in BC find free settlement help?

Free settlement services for newcomers in BC offer help with language, jobs, housing, and health care navigation, often through neighbourhood houses.

How can I make friends after moving to a new country?

Join recurring activities, volunteer, connect with cultural or faith communities, and say yes to invitations, since friendships grow with repeated contact.

Why do I feel like I lost my identity after immigrating?

Immigrating can mean losing familiar roles, language, and recognition, which can shake your sense of who you are for a time.

How do I keep my culture while settling into a new country?

Stay in touch with family, cook familiar foods, celebrate cultural holidays, and speak your first language while building new connections.

Why is my child adjusting to Canada faster than I am?

Children often pick up language and customs quickly through school, while adults face more practical and identity pressures as newcomers.

Can the dark winters in Canada affect a newcomer's mood?

Yes. Newcomers from sunnier climates may notice lower mood in Canadian winters. Daylight exposure and activity can help.

When should a newcomer talk to a therapist about adjustment?

If homesickness, low mood, anxiety, or loneliness last for many months or affect daily life, a therapist can help with adjusting to a new country.
