How to Choose the Right Therapist for You

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

To choose the right therapist, look for a qualified professional with experience in your concern, an approach that suits how you like to work, practical details that fit your life, such as location, availability, fees, and benefits coverage, and, most importantly, someone you feel comfortable with. Research consistently shows that the relationship between client and therapist, sometimes called the therapeutic alliance, is one of the strongest predictors of benefit. It is completely acceptable to speak with more than one therapist before deciding, ask direct questions, and change therapists if the fit does not feel right after a few sessions.

How to Search

  1. Clarify what you need. Is it support for anxiety, depression, trauma, relationships, OCD, or an assessment for ADHD or autism? Specialized concerns often benefit from specialized training.
  2. Check credentials. In BC, providers may be registered psychologists, registered clinical counsellors, clinical social workers, or others. The page about psychologists, clinical counsellors, and psychotherapists explains the differences.
  3. Look at approach. Some therapists use structured methods like CBT, while others are more exploratory. See the page about types of therapy.
  4. Read profiles. Clinic websites and directories often describe training, focus areas, and style.
  5. Reach out. Many therapists offer a brief consultation or answer questions before booking.

Practical Details

Experience with your concern. For OCD, look for ERP training. For trauma, look for trauma-focused training. For neurodivergent clients, look for neurodiversity-affirming experience. Examples include the pages about finding an OCD-informed therapist and finding an ADHD-informed therapist.

Identity and values. Some people prefer a therapist who shares or understands aspects of their culture, language, faith, or life experience. It is reasonable to ask about this.

Logistics. Consider in-person or virtual sessions, location, availability, and session frequency.

Cost and coverage. Check fees and whether your extended health plan covers the therapist's designation, as explained on the page about extended health benefits for therapy.

Red flags. A therapist who dismisses your concerns, shares too much about themselves, pressures you, breaks boundaries, or makes you feel judged may not be a good fit.

Questions to Ask

  • What is your training, and what is your professional designation?
  • How much experience do you have with concerns like mine?
  • What approach would you likely use, and why?
  • What does a typical session look like?
  • How often would we meet, and how long might therapy take?
  • How do you measure progress?
  • What are your fees, and do you provide receipts for insurance?

After the first session or two, ask yourself whether you felt heard, respected, and comfortable being honest. It can take a few sessions to settle in, but if something feels consistently off, it is okay to raise it or try someone else. The page about how to know if therapy is working may help.

Common Questions About Choosing a Therapist

What should I look for when picking a therapist?

Choose a therapist with relevant credentials, experience with your concern, an approach that suits you, practical fit, and someone you feel comfortable with.

How much does rapport with a therapist affect results?

A great deal. Research shows the relationship between client and therapist is one of the strongest predictors of benefit, so feeling comfortable matters.

Is it okay to talk to several therapists before choosing?

Yes. Speaking with more than one therapist before choosing is completely acceptable and can help you find a good fit.

What credentials should I look for in a therapist in BC?

In BC, look for registered psychologists, registered clinical counsellors, clinical social workers, or other registered professionals with relevant training.

Should I choose a therapist who specializes in my concern?

For concerns like OCD, trauma, eating disorders, or neurodivergence, a therapist with specialized training is often helpful.

Can I ask a therapist about their cultural background or values?

Yes. It is reasonable to ask a therapist about their understanding of your culture, language, faith, or life experience.

What are red flags when choosing a therapist?

Red flags include a therapist who dismisses your concerns, pressures you, breaks boundaries, shares too much about themselves, or makes you feel judged.

Which questions help me compare therapists before booking?

Ask a potential therapist about training, designation, experience with your concern, approach, session frequency, progress, and fees.

How many sessions before I know if a therapist is right?

It can take a few sessions to settle in with a therapist, but if something feels consistently off, it is okay to raise it or try someone else.

Is it okay to switch therapists?

Yes. Switching therapists is common and acceptable if the fit is not right, and a good therapist will understand.
