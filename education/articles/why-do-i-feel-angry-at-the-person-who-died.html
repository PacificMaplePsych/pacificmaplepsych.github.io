Why Do I Feel Angry at the Person Who Died

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You miss them terribly, and yet there it is: anger. Anger that they did not look after their health, that they left things unfinished, that they took their own life, that they left you to deal with everything alone. Sometimes the anger has no clear target at all, and simply flares at the unfairness of them being gone. Many people feel ashamed of this, as if being angry at someone who died is a betrayal of their memory.

Anger toward a person who has died is one of the most common grief reactions, and one of the least talked about. It does not mean you loved them less. Often it reflects how much their absence hurts and how many feelings were tied up in the relationship.

If grief has brought thoughts of suicide or you feel unable to keep yourself safe, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Protest against the loss. Anger is a natural response to something that feels wrong or unfair, and death often feels exactly that. Part of the anger may simply be the mind protesting that this happened at all.

Choices they made. If the person's death was linked to drinking, smoking, refusing treatment, risky behaviour, or suicide, it is very common to feel angry about the choices that led here, while also feeling guilty for that anger.

Unfinished business. Conversations that never happened, apologies never given, or conflicts never resolved can leave anger with nowhere to go now that the person cannot respond.

New burdens. Being left with debts, paperwork, caregiving, a household to run, or children to raise alone can create a practical anger that sits right alongside sadness.

A complicated relationship. If the person hurt you, was unreliable, or was difficult to love, their death can bring up years of anger that was never fully expressed, sometimes along with relief, confusion, or numbness.

General strain. Exhaustion, poor sleep, and stress after a death can shorten anyone's fuse. Some people notice they feel angrier than usual in general, not only toward the person who died.

When It Looks More Like Grief

Grief is not only sadness. It is a whole mix of emotions, including longing, guilt, relief, fear, and anger, that tend to come in waves. Anger toward the person who died is often part of that mix, particularly early on, and it usually softens as the loss is gradually absorbed.

Anger in grief can be hard to acknowledge because of cultural expectations to speak only well of the dead. When it is pushed down, it often shows up elsewhere: irritability with family, tension, trouble sleeping, or anger directed at doctors, relatives, or yourself. People who tend to avoid talking about how they really feel can find that grief anger leaks out in unexpected ways.

Anger is more likely to become stuck when the death was sudden, traumatic, or by suicide, when the relationship was complicated, or when there is little space to talk about mixed feelings. If anger, bitterness, or preoccupation with the death remains intense for a long time and makes it hard to function, grief-focused therapy can help.

What Can Help

  • Allow both feelings. You can love someone and be angry with them at the same time. Holding both is part of an honest relationship, even after death.
  • Put it into words. Writing an unsent letter to the person, saying what you are angry about and what you miss, can release some of the pressure.
  • Find a safe listener. A trusted friend, support group, or therapist who will not rush to defend the person can make it easier to say what you really feel.
  • Move the energy. Physical activity can help discharge the tension that anger creates in the body.
  • Watch for anger turned inward. Anger at the person often sits beside guilt and self-blame. Noticing both helps you respond to yourself with more compassion.

Common Questions About Anger After a Death

Do other grieving people feel anger toward the person they lost?

Yes. Anger is a very common part of grief, including anger directed at the person who died. It can sit alongside love, sadness, and guilt, and it does not mean the relationship was not meaningful.

Why do I feel guilty for being angry at a loved one who died?

Many families and cultures expect people to speak only well of the dead, so anger can feel disloyal. That guilt is understandable, but anger is a natural reaction to loss and to the parts of the relationship that were painful or unfinished.

Can I be angry at someone who died by suicide?

Yes, and it is very common. People bereaved by suicide often feel anger alongside confusion, guilt, and profound sadness. Support from people who understand suicide loss, including specialized groups or therapy, can be particularly helpful.

Why am I angry at a parent who died when our relationship was difficult?

A death can bring up years of feelings that were never resolved, especially if the parent was hurtful or unreliable. The chance to repair things is gone, which can intensify anger. Therapy can help you grieve both the person and the relationship you did not get to have.

Is being angry at the person who died a sign of complicated grief?

Not on its own. Anger is a normal grief response. It becomes more concerning when it stays intense for a long time, dominates your thoughts, or makes it very hard to function, which can be part of complicated or prolonged grief.

How can I express anger at someone who is no longer here?

Writing an unsent letter, speaking aloud at a graveside or private place, or talking with a therapist can all give the anger somewhere to go. Physical activity can also help release the tension that builds up.

Why do I feel angry at doctors or family members after a death?

Anger in grief often looks for a target, especially when a death feels preventable or unfair. Sometimes it lands on people who were involved in the person's care. Some of that anger may be justified and some may be grief looking for somewhere to land.

Can grief anger turn into anger at myself?

Yes. Anger at the person who died often sits alongside guilt and self-blame, such as feeling you should have noticed something or done more. Recognizing this pattern can help you treat yourself with more understanding.

Will the anger I feel after a death ever go away?

For most people, grief anger softens over time as the loss is gradually absorbed and feelings are expressed. It may resurface around anniversaries or reminders. If it stays intense or unchanged for a long time, support can help it move.

How does grief therapy help with anger toward the person who died?

Grief therapy provides a non-judgmental space to express all feelings about the person, including anger, without rushing to defend or excuse them. This often makes room for more balanced memories and a lasting sense of connection.
