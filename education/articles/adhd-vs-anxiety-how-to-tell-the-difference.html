ADHD vs. Anxiety: How to Tell the Difference

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

ADHD and anxiety are both common, and they can look surprisingly similar. People with either may feel restless, struggle to concentrate, have trouble sleeping, and feel overwhelmed. They also frequently occur together: many adults with ADHD have an anxiety disorder, and some people with anxiety have undiagnosed ADHD. Telling them apart matters, because the most helpful treatment depends on what is driving the difficulties. A careful assessment can clarify whether one, the other, or both are present.

Why They Are Easy to Confuse

Both conditions can involve difficulty focusing, restlessness, irritability, procrastination, poor sleep, and feeling overwhelmed. Both can lead to avoidance of tasks and self-criticism. Adults with ADHD often develop anxiety about forgetting things, missing deadlines, or being judged, which can make ADHD look primarily like anxiety. Anxiety can also impair attention, making someone seem distracted.

Key Differences

  • Source of distraction. In ADHD, attention drifts to whatever is interesting or new. In anxiety, attention is pulled toward worries and threats.
  • Timing. ADHD symptoms begin in childhood and are present across situations, even when calm. Anxiety often fluctuates with stress and specific triggers.
  • Restlessness. ADHD restlessness is often a need for stimulation or movement. Anxious restlessness feels like tension or dread.
  • Procrastination. In ADHD, it often comes from difficulty starting boring tasks. In anxiety, it often comes from fear of failure or making mistakes.
  • Racing thoughts. ADHD thoughts tend to jump between ideas. Anxious thoughts tend to loop around worries.
  • Response to calm. When anxiety is low, ADHD symptoms remain. When anxiety is treated, attention problems caused by anxiety often improve.

People with inattentive ADHD are especially likely to be misdiagnosed with anxiety, because their symptoms are quieter.

When Both Are Present

ADHD and anxiety commonly occur together. Years of struggling with focus and organization can lead to worry and fear of failure, while anxiety can make ADHD symptoms worse by adding mental load. Some people cope with ADHD by becoming anxious perfectionists, over-preparing to avoid mistakes. Others with generalized anxiety may also have trouble focusing, as described on the page about being unable to focus no matter how hard you try. When both are present, treating only one may leave important difficulties unaddressed.

A useful clue is looking back at childhood. ADHD symptoms are present from an early age, often noticed in school reports or family memories. Anxiety can begin at any age and may be linked to particular events or stressors.

Sleep can offer another clue. People with ADHD often struggle to wind down because their minds are busy with ideas or they lose track of time. People with anxiety often lie awake worrying about specific concerns. Both can occur together, and both improve with targeted strategies.

Why the Distinction Matters

Treatment differs. ADHD often responds to medication, ADHD coaching, and cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for ADHD, which focus on systems, time management, and emotional regulation. Anxiety responds to cognitive behavioural therapy, exposure, and sometimes different medications. Some anxiety medications do not help ADHD, and stimulant medication can sometimes increase anxiety if not carefully managed. A thorough assessment considers childhood history, current symptoms, and how difficulties change across situations. Many people find that treating ADHD reduces anxiety, and treating anxiety makes ADHD strategies easier to use.

Common Questions About ADHD and Anxiety

Which clues point toward ADHD rather than worry?

ADHD symptoms begin in childhood, span many situations, and persist even when calm. Anxiety usually fluctuates with stress and centres on worry.

Can ADHD cause anxiety?

Yes. Years of struggling with focus and organization can lead to worry, fear of failure, and anxiety.

Why does anxiety make it hard to concentrate?

Anxiety pulls attention toward worries and threats, making it harder to focus on tasks.

Is it common to have both ADHD and anxiety?

Yes. Many adults with ADHD also have an anxiety disorder.

How does ADHD procrastination differ from anxious procrastination?

ADHD procrastination often comes from difficulty starting dull tasks, while anxious procrastination often comes from fear of failure.

Can stimulant medication make anxiety worse?

In some people it can, so prescribers monitor carefully and adjust treatment when both conditions are present.

Why was I diagnosed with anxiety instead of ADHD?

Inattentive ADHD and ADHD-related worry can look like anxiety, so ADHD is sometimes missed.

What does an assessment for ADHD and anxiety involve?

It looks at childhood history, current symptoms, and how difficulties change across situations and stress levels.

Does treating ADHD reduce anxiety?

For many people, managing ADHD reduces daily stress and the worries that come from struggling to keep up.

Why do my thoughts race if I do not have anxiety?

In ADHD, thoughts may jump quickly between ideas, which can feel like racing thoughts without worry.
