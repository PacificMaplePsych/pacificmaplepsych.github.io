Building Self-Esteem Through Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Building self-esteem through therapy involves understanding where negative beliefs about yourself came from, learning to notice and respond differently to self-criticism, and building a steadier, kinder sense of your worth that does not depend on constant achievement or approval. Low self-esteem is not a diagnosis, but it can affect mood, relationships, work, and decisions, and it often accompanies anxiety and depression. Therapy approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy, compassion-focused therapy, and schema therapy can help. Change is usually gradual, but many people find they feel more confident, less harsh with themselves, and more able to go after what they want.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps people identify core beliefs such as "I am not good enough," notice how these shape thoughts and behaviour, and test them through experiments. It often includes building a more balanced view by collecting evidence of strengths and reducing avoidance or over-preparing.

Compassion-focused therapy. For people with a harsh inner critic, compassion-focused therapy helps build a warm, supportive inner voice. This is especially helpful when shame is strong.

Schema therapy. Schema therapy explores deep, long-standing patterns formed in childhood, such as feeling defective or unlovable, and helps meet unmet emotional needs.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people relate differently to self-critical thoughts and act on their values, rather than waiting to feel confident first.

Psychodynamic and relational approaches. These explore how early relationships shaped self-worth, and use the therapy relationship to experience acceptance.

Trauma-focused therapy. When low self-esteem is linked with trauma, bullying, or abuse, trauma-focused approaches can help process these experiences.

Group therapy. Groups focused on self-esteem or assertiveness offer practice and support from others with similar struggles.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A therapist will explore how low self-esteem shows up in your life, where it may come from, and what you hope will change. The page about low self-esteem describes common patterns. Co-occurring concerns, such as depression, anxiety, eating concerns, or trauma, shape which approach fits best. Some people want practical, structured work on thoughts and behaviours, while others want to explore the past more deeply. Patterns such as perfectionism or people-pleasing may be a focus. Many therapists combine approaches based on your needs and preferences.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress often begins with noticing the inner critic more clearly and understanding where it came from. Over time, people may find they are less harsh after mistakes, more willing to try new things, better at setting boundaries, and more comfortable accepting compliments. They may notice they compare themselves to others less. Change is usually gradual, often over months, since beliefs about the self can be deeply rooted. Setbacks, such as old feelings returning during stress, are common and are part of the process. The page about chronic self-criticism explains why these patterns can be persistent.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Between sessions, keeping a record of things you did well, strengths others notice, or moments you handled differently can counter negative beliefs. Practise responding to self-criticism as you would to a friend. Take small steps toward things you have avoided, and notice what happens. Spend time with people who treat you with respect, and set limits with those who do not. Look after your physical wellbeing, since sleep, movement, and routine support mood. Be patient with yourself, since building self-esteem takes practice.

If low self-esteem comes with persistent low mood, hopelessness, or thoughts of suicide, reach out for support. You can call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Therapy for Self-Esteem

Can therapy really improve self-esteem?

Yes. Approaches such as CBT, compassion-focused therapy, and schema therapy can help people build a steadier sense of worth.

Which therapy is best for low self-esteem?

It depends on your needs. CBT suits structured work, compassion-focused therapy helps with shame, and schema therapy explores deep patterns.

How long does it take to build self-esteem in therapy?

Change is usually gradual, often over months, since beliefs about the self can be deeply rooted.

Will my therapist help me figure out the deep beliefs I hold about myself?

Yes. Identifying core beliefs, deep ideas such as feeling not good enough that shape thoughts and behaviour, is often an early step.

Why does my self-esteem drop when I make a mistake?

A harsh inner critic can treat mistakes as proof of worthlessness. Therapy helps build a more balanced response.

Can group therapy help with self-esteem?

Yes. Groups offer practice and support from others working on similar struggles.

Do I need a formal condition to see a therapist about how I see myself?

No. Low self-esteem is not a diagnosis, but it often accompanies anxiety and depression and is a common reason people seek therapy.

What can I do between sessions to build self-esteem?

Record strengths and successes, respond kindly to self-criticism, take small brave steps, and spend time with supportive people.

Can childhood experiences affect self-esteem as an adult?

Yes. Criticism, neglect, bullying, or trauma can shape beliefs about self-worth that last into adulthood.

How will I know my self-esteem is improving?

You may notice you are less harsh after mistakes, more willing to try new things, and better at setting boundaries.
