Understanding Cannabis Use and Mental Health

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you experience paranoia, hallucinations, or confusion after using cannabis, stop using and seek medical care, calling 911 if you or others are at risk. Do not drive after using cannabis. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8.

Cannabis is legal for adults in Canada, and many people use it without significant problems. It can, however, affect mental health in several ways, both short and long term. Some people use it to ease anxiety, stress, or sleep problems and find short-term relief, while frequent use can worsen these same issues over time. Heavy use, especially of high-THC products and starting in adolescence, is linked with a higher risk of psychosis, dependence, and effects on mood and motivation. Understanding these effects helps people make informed choices.

What the Effects Look Like

  • Anxiety. Low doses may relax some people, but higher THC doses can trigger anxiety or panic, and regular use can increase baseline anxiety.
  • Mood. Heavy use is associated with depression, low motivation, and flat mood for some people.
  • Sleep. Cannabis can help people fall asleep in the short term, but regular use can reduce sleep quality, and stopping often causes sleep disruption and vivid dreams.
  • Psychosis. High-THC cannabis, frequent use, and early starting age increase the risk of psychotic symptoms and, in some people, schizophrenia.
  • Cognition. Frequent use can affect memory, attention, and learning, especially in young people.
  • Dependence. Some people develop cannabis use disorder, with cravings, tolerance, and withdrawal.

Why It Happens

THC, the main psychoactive compound in cannabis, acts on the brain's endocannabinoid system, which helps regulate mood, stress, appetite, sleep, and memory. With frequent use, the brain adapts, leading to tolerance and withdrawal symptoms when use stops. Today's products often contain much higher THC concentrations than in the past, increasing the risk of adverse effects. CBD, another compound, has different effects and does not produce a high, though research on its mental health benefits is still developing. Genetics, age, dose, frequency, and existing mental health conditions all influence risk.

Many people use cannabis to cope with anxiety, sleep problems, pain, or ADHD symptoms. The short-term relief can make it hard to notice that, over months, the underlying problem has not improved or has worsened.

What It Is Not

  • Not harmless for everyone. Legal does not mean risk-free, especially for youth, people with psychosis risk, and heavy users.
  • Not harmful for everyone. Many adults use occasionally without major problems.
  • Not a proven treatment for anxiety or depression. Evidence does not support cannabis as a reliable treatment for these conditions.
  • Not the same as a substance use disorder in every case. Problematic use involves loss of control and harm, as described on the page about substance use disorder.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician can assess patterns of use, reasons for using, mental health symptoms, and effects on life. Canada's Lower-Risk Cannabis Use Guidelines offer practical advice, such as choosing lower-THC products, avoiding daily use, and not driving after use. For people who want to cut back or stop, therapy approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing are effective. Addressing underlying anxiety, sleep problems, or ADHD often makes cutting back easier. Withdrawal symptoms, such as irritability and sleep disturbance, are usually temporary.

Common Questions About Cannabis and Mental Health

How does cannabis affect mental health?

Cannabis can affect anxiety, mood, sleep, memory, and motivation. Heavy or early use, especially of high-THC products, increases risks such as psychosis and dependence.

Does cannabis make anxiety worse?

It can. Low doses may relax some people, but higher THC doses can trigger anxiety or panic, and regular use can raise baseline anxiety over time.

Is cannabis linked to depression?

Heavy cannabis use is associated with depression and low motivation for some people, though the relationship is complex and may run in both directions.

Who is most at risk of psychosis from cannabis?

Young people, frequent users, those using high-THC products, and people with a personal or family history of psychosis are at higher risk.

Does CBD affect the mind the same way THC does?

THC is the main psychoactive compound that causes a high. CBD does not produce a high and has different effects, though research on its benefits is still developing.

Why do I sleep badly when I stop using cannabis?

Regular use changes sleep patterns, so stopping can cause temporary insomnia and vivid dreams. These withdrawal effects usually improve within a few weeks.

Is cannabis safe for teenagers?

Cannabis carries higher risks for adolescents, whose brains are still developing, including effects on memory, learning, and a higher risk of psychosis.

What does lower-risk cannabis use mean?

Canada's lower-risk guidelines suggest delaying use, choosing lower-THC products, avoiding daily use and smoking, and never driving after using cannabis.

How can I tell if my cannabis use has become a problem?

Signs include using more than intended, failed attempts to cut back, cravings, tolerance, withdrawal, and continued use despite harm to mood, work, or relationships.

What kind of support helps people reduce cannabis use?

Cognitive behavioural therapy and motivational interviewing are effective, along with addressing anxiety, sleep, or ADHD that may be driving use.
