Understanding Performance Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Performance anxiety is intense nervousness or fear before or during a situation where a person feels they are being watched, judged, or evaluated. Common examples include public speaking, presentations at work, exams, job interviews, musical or theatre performances, sport, and sexual performance. Some nervousness before a big moment is normal and can even sharpen focus. Performance anxiety becomes a problem when the fear is strong enough to interfere with performance, cause significant distress, or lead people to avoid opportunities that matter to them. It is very common and responds well to treatment.

What Performance Anxiety Looks Like

  • A racing heart, shaking hands, sweating, or a trembling voice.
  • Nausea or feeling sick to your stomach before big events.
  • A dry mouth, blushing, or feeling short of breath.
  • Going blank, losing your place, or freezing under pressure.
  • Days or weeks of dread and worry leading up to the event.
  • Harsh self-criticism afterward, replaying mistakes over and over.
  • Avoiding presentations, auditions, promotions, or competitions.

Many people with performance anxiety are highly capable. They may perform well in practice or in private but struggle when the stakes feel high or an audience is present. This gap between ability and performance can be deeply frustrating.

Why It Happens

Performance anxiety is driven by the body's threat response. When the brain perceives a situation as a threat to status, approval, or success, it releases adrenaline and prepares the body to fight or flee. These physical changes are helpful for running from danger but unhelpful for giving a speech or playing a delicate piece of music. Thoughts often add fuel: predictions of failure, fears of being judged, and beliefs that any visible nervousness will be noticed and criticized.

Several factors increase vulnerability. Perfectionism and high standards raise the perceived stakes. A past embarrassing experience can create a lasting fear. Temperament plays a role, as some people have a more reactive nervous system. Avoidance also keeps anxiety going, because skipping performances prevents the brain from learning that it can cope. Paying close attention to physical symptoms can make them feel more intense, which can increase fear in a cycle.

What It Is Not

  • Not a lack of skill or preparation. Many people with performance anxiety are well prepared and talented.
  • Not always social anxiety disorder. Performance anxiety can be limited to specific situations. When fears extend to many everyday social situations, social anxiety disorder may be a better fit.
  • Not a sign of weakness. Professional performers, athletes, and executives commonly experience it.
  • Not something that requires eliminating all nerves. The goal is to manage anxiety well enough to perform, not to feel nothing.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about the situations that trigger anxiety, physical and mental symptoms, avoidance, and how it affects work, study, or goals. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most studied treatment. It helps people identify and test anxious predictions, reduce safety behaviours, and gradually practise performing in more challenging situations. Skills such as slow breathing, grounding, and shifting attention outward toward the task or audience can reduce symptoms in the moment. Acceptance-based approaches teach people to make room for nerves while still doing what matters. Some people discuss short-term medication options with a physician for occasional high-stakes events.

Preparation matters too, though it works best when it includes rehearsing under realistic conditions rather than endless private practice. Rehearsing in front of a trusted person, recording yourself, or practising in the actual space can build confidence.

Common Questions About Performance Anxiety

What causes performance anxiety?

Performance anxiety comes from the body's threat response when a situation feels high stakes, combined with fears of being judged and predictions of failure.

Why do I perform well in practice but fall apart under pressure?

Adrenaline and anxious thoughts can disrupt memory, fine motor control, and focus when an audience or evaluation is present, even when skills are strong.

Is performance anxiety the same as stage fright?

Stage fright is one form of performance anxiety. The term performance anxiety also covers exams, interviews, sport, presentations, and other evaluated situations.

Can performance anxiety affect athletes?

Yes. Athletes may experience tension, overthinking, and choking under pressure. Sport psychology and anxiety management skills can help.

What can I do in the moment when performance nerves hit?

Slow, steady breathing, grounding your feet, focusing on the task or audience rather than your body, and accepting some nerves can all help.

Will people notice that I am nervous?

Usually far less than you expect. People tend to overestimate how visible their anxiety is to others, a pattern known as the illusion of transparency.

Does avoiding presentations make performance anxiety worse?

Often, yes. Avoidance brings short-term relief but prevents the brain from learning that it can cope, which can strengthen the fear over time.

How well does therapy work for fear of being evaluated?

Very well for many people. Cognitive behavioural therapy, gradual practice, and anxiety management skills are effective for many people.

Is medication used for performance anxiety?

Some people discuss short-term medication with a physician for occasional high-stakes events. Therapy addresses the underlying fear more lastingly.

How can perfectionism make performance anxiety stronger?

Perfectionism raises the perceived stakes, so any mistake feels like failure. Loosening rigid standards often reduces anxiety.
