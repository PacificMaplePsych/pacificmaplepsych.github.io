Bullying and Cyberbullying: Effects and How to Help

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If a child or teen talks about wanting to die or harming themselves, or you are worried about their immediate safety, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Young people can reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Bullying is repeated, intentional behaviour meant to hurt, intimidate, or exclude someone, usually involving a power imbalance. It can be physical, verbal, social, such as spreading rumours or deliberately excluding someone, or online, known as cyberbullying. Cyberbullying can follow children home through phones and social media, making it hard to escape. Bullying can lead to anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, sleep problems, school avoidance, and in some cases thoughts of self-harm. Children often do not tell adults, so knowing the signs matters. Parents can help by listening calmly, documenting what happened, working with the school, and supporting their child's confidence and friendships.

Types of Bullying

  • Physical. Hitting, pushing, or damaging belongings.
  • Verbal. Name-calling, insults, or threats.
  • Social. Exclusion, rumours, or embarrassing someone in front of others.
  • Cyberbullying. Hurtful messages, posts, images, or impersonation online, including sharing private images without consent.

Signs a Child May Be Bullied

  • Reluctance to go to school, as described on the page about school refusal
  • Unexplained injuries or damaged belongings
  • Changes in mood, such as sadness, irritability, or anxiety
  • Changes in sleep or appetite
  • Avoiding devices or seeming upset after using them
  • Losing friends or withdrawing from activities
  • Declining grades

The Mental Health Effects

Bullying is linked to anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, loneliness, and physical complaints. Effects can last into adulthood. Children who are both bullied and bully others, and children who belong to groups more often targeted, such as 2SLGBTQ+ youth or children with disabilities, may be at higher risk.

How Parents Can Help

1. Listen Calmly

Thank your child for telling you, listen without judgment, and reassure them it is not their fault.

2. Avoid Reacting in Ways That Increase Risk

Avoid contacting the other child's family directly or encouraging retaliation, which can escalate the situation.

3. Document

Keep notes, screenshots, and dates of incidents, especially online.

4. Work With the School

Share information with teachers and administrators and ask about the school's response plan. In BC, the ERASE program supports safe schools and includes an anonymous online reporting tool for students and families.

5. Address Cyberbullying

Help your child block and report accounts, adjust privacy settings, and avoid responding to harassment. Serious threats or sharing intimate images of a minor should be reported to police.

6. Build Confidence and Connection

Support friendships and activities where your child feels valued.

If Your Child Is Bullying Others

Stay calm, take it seriously, and explore what may be behind the behaviour, such as difficulty managing emotions or peer pressure. Clear consequences, teaching empathy, and support from the school or a therapist can help.

Bystanders Matter Too

Children who witness bullying can also be affected, feeling anxious, guilty, or unsafe. Teaching children safe ways to support peers, such as staying with the person being targeted, telling a trusted adult, or refusing to share hurtful posts, can reduce bullying and help everyone feel safer.

When to Get Support

Consider professional support if your child shows ongoing anxiety, low mood, school avoidance, self-harm, or changes that do not improve after the bullying stops. Therapy can help children process what happened and rebuild confidence. The pages about signs your child may need mental health support and anxiety in children and teens offer more guidance.

Common Questions About Bullying and Cyberbullying

When does teasing become bullying?

Teasing becomes bullying when it is repeated, intended to hurt, and involves a power imbalance that makes it hard for the child to defend themselves.

What is cyberbullying?

Cyberbullying is bullying online, through hurtful messages, posts, images, impersonation, or sharing private content without consent.

What are signs my child is being bullied?

Signs a child is being bullied include not wanting to go to school, mood changes, unexplained injuries, avoiding devices, and losing friends.

Can being bullied as a child affect mental health in adulthood?

Yes. Childhood bullying is linked to anxiety, depression, and low self-esteem that can continue into adulthood, which is why early support matters.

What should I do if my child is bullied?

Listen calmly, reassure your child, document incidents, work with the school, and seek support if effects persist.

How do I report bullying in BC schools?

Report bullying to your child's school, and in BC you can also use the ERASE program's anonymous online reporting tool.

Should I contact the bully's parents?

It is usually better to work through the school rather than contacting the other family directly, to avoid escalation.

What should I do about cyberbullying?

Help your child block and report accounts, save screenshots, adjust privacy settings, and report serious threats to police.

What if my child is bullying others?

Take it seriously, stay calm, explore what is behind the behaviour, set clear consequences, and seek school or professional support.

Can therapy help a child who was bullied?

Yes. Therapy can help a child who was bullied process what happened, manage anxiety or low mood, and rebuild confidence.
