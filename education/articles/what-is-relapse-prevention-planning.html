What Is Relapse Prevention Planning

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Relapse prevention planning is the process of creating a personal plan to help you stay well after improvement from a mental health or substance use concern. The plan identifies your triggers, early warning signs that things are slipping, and specific steps to take when those signs appear. It is commonly used for depression, anxiety, bipolar disorder, eating disorders, and substance use. Relapse, meaning a return of symptoms, is common and does not mean treatment failed. A good plan helps people catch difficulties early, respond quickly, and recover faster, often preventing a full return of symptoms.

Why Relapse Prevention Planning Helps

Many mental health conditions can recur. For example, people who have had one episode of depression have an increased chance of another, and substance use recovery often involves ups and downs. Symptoms usually return gradually, with small early changes such as sleeping poorly, withdrawing from friends, or skipping routines. These signs are easy to miss or dismiss.

A written plan makes it easier to notice early signs and act before difficulties grow. It also reduces the need to make decisions when you are already struggling, since the steps are decided in advance. Research on relapse prevention approaches, including those used in cognitive behavioural therapy and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, suggests they can reduce the risk of relapse for many people.

Strategies to Try

Building a relapse prevention plan often involves these steps, ideally with a therapist or doctor:

  1. Identify your triggers. Situations that increase risk, such as stress at work, conflict, anniversaries, poor sleep, seasonal changes, or being around substances.
  2. List early warning signs. Changes in thoughts, feelings, and behaviours that appeared before past difficulties, such as irritability, trouble concentrating, cancelling plans, or increased cravings. Ask trusted people what they notice.
  3. Note what keeps you well. Daily habits that support wellbeing, such as regular sleep, movement, social contact, meaningful activities, and taking medication as prescribed. The page about daily routines for mood stability offers ideas.
  4. Plan your responses. Specific steps for when warning signs appear, such as returning to coping skills, reaching out to a friend, scheduling a therapy session, or contacting your doctor.
  5. Identify your support people. Names and contact details of people and services you can reach out to.
  6. Include a crisis plan. Steps and contacts if you feel unsafe, including 9-8-8 and 911.
  7. Review and update. Revisit the plan regularly, especially after stressful events or changes in treatment.

Some people keep their plan on their phone, on paper, or shared with a trusted person. It can help to write it in simple, clear language so it is easy to use when you are not at your best.

Common Obstacles

When things are going well, it can be tempting to drop the habits that helped, or to stop looking at the plan. Scheduling regular check-ins, such as monthly, can help. Some people feel ashamed when warning signs appear and delay acting. Remembering that setbacks are common and that early action works best can reduce shame. Others struggle to notice their own warning signs, which is why asking trusted people to share what they see can be helpful.

Lapses, short returns to old patterns, are different from full relapses. A lapse can be a chance to learn more about triggers and strengthen the plan, rather than a reason to give up.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If warning signs continue despite following your plan, if symptoms are worsening, or if you notice thoughts of suicide, contact your therapist, doctor, or a crisis line promptly. People with bipolar disorder should contact their prescriber quickly if signs of mood episodes appear. Returning to treatment early is a sign of strength and can shorten difficult periods.

Common Questions About Relapse Prevention Plans

What goes into a relapse prevention plan?

Triggers, early warning signs, habits that keep you well, specific responses, support contacts, and a crisis plan.

What are early warning signs of a mental health relapse?

Common signs include poor sleep, withdrawing from people, irritability, skipping routines, and returning thoughts or cravings.

Is a relapse a sign that my treatment did not work?

No. Relapse is common and does not mean treatment failed. Early action often helps people recover faster.

Does one bad week mean I have fully slipped back into old patterns?

A lapse is a short return to old patterns, while a relapse is a fuller return of symptoms.

Who should I share my relapse prevention plan with?

Many people share it with a trusted friend, family member, therapist, or doctor who can help notice warning signs.

When is a good time to revisit and rewrite my plan for staying well?

Review it regularly, such as monthly, and after major stressors or treatment changes.

Can relapse prevention help with substance use recovery?

Yes. It helps people identify triggers, manage cravings, and plan responses to high-risk situations.

Should I keep going to therapy for depression once I feel better, or is that the time to stop?

Some people continue with less frequent sessions to maintain progress, which can reduce the chance of relapse.

Why should a relapse prevention plan include a crisis section?

It helps you know exactly who to contact and what to do if you ever feel unsafe.

How can a therapist help me build a relapse plan?

A therapist can help identify triggers and warning signs, plan responses, and review the plan with you over time.
