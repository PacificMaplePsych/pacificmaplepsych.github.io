Understanding Light Therapy for Seasonal Depression

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Talk to a doctor before starting light therapy if you have bipolar disorder, eye conditions, or take medications that increase light sensitivity.

Light therapy, also called bright light therapy, is a treatment for seasonal depression in which a person sits near a special light box for a set time each day, usually in the morning. The light box gives off bright light, much brighter than ordinary indoor lighting, which helps reset the body clock and can lift mood during darker months. It is one of the best-studied treatments for seasonal affective disorder (SAD), and many people notice improvement within one to two weeks. Light therapy is usually used under the guidance of a doctor, and it can be combined with therapy, medication, and lifestyle changes.

How Light Therapy Works

Seasonal affective disorder is a form of depression that tends to begin in fall or winter and ease in spring, when daylight hours grow longer. In places like British Columbia, short winter days and grey skies reduce exposure to natural light. Reduced light can disrupt the body's internal clock, or circadian rhythm, and affect brain chemicals and hormones involved in mood, sleep, and energy, such as serotonin and melatonin.

Light therapy mimics the effect of bright morning daylight. Light entering the eyes signals to the brain's clock that it is daytime, which can help shift sleep and energy patterns back into a healthier rhythm and improve mood. It works through the eyes, so the light needs to reach them, though you should not stare directly into the box.

What Using It Involves

Light boxes designed for SAD commonly provide around 10,000 lux of light, which is much brighter than typical indoor lighting. A common approach is to sit near the box for around twenty to thirty minutes each morning, soon after waking, while reading, eating breakfast, or working. Your doctor may suggest a different timing or duration depending on your situation.

Practical tips include:

  • Choose a box designed for SAD. Look for one that filters ultraviolet light.
  • Use it consistently. Daily use through the darker months tends to work best.
  • Position it correctly. Place it at the distance recommended by the manufacturer, slightly above eye level and to the side.
  • Start early in the season. Some people begin in early fall if symptoms return each year.

Side effects are usually mild and may include eye strain, headache, nausea, or feeling jittery. Using light therapy too late in the day can interfere with sleep.

Who It Can Help

Light therapy has good evidence for seasonal depression, and research suggests it may also help some people with non-seasonal depression, often alongside other treatments. It can support people who notice that their mood, energy, and sleep worsen in winter. Keeping regular sleep and wake times and getting outside in daylight, as described on the page about routines for mood stability, can add to its benefits.

Light therapy is not suitable for everyone without medical guidance. People with bipolar disorder need to use it only under a doctor's supervision, since bright light can sometimes trigger elevated mood, as explained on the page about bipolar II disorder. People with certain eye conditions, or those taking medications that increase sensitivity to light, should also check with a doctor first. For moderate to severe depression, light therapy often works best combined with therapy and sometimes medication, as outlined on the page about how depression is treated.

Light therapy is usually used in the morning, soon after waking. Using a light box late in the day can interfere with sleep.

How long should I sit in front of a light box each day?

A common approach is around twenty to thirty minutes of light therapy each morning, though your doctor may suggest something different.

How quickly does light therapy start to work?

Many people with seasonal depression notice improvement within one to two weeks of daily light therapy.

What are the side effects of light therapy?

Light therapy side effects are usually mild, such as eye strain, headache, nausea, or jitteriness, and often ease with adjustments.

Is light therapy safe if I have bipolar disorder?

Can light therapy help depression that is not seasonal?

Research suggests light therapy may help some people with non-seasonal depression, often alongside other treatments.

Should I start light therapy before winter begins?

Is a light box enough on its own for winter depression?
