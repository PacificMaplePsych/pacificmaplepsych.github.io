How to Support Someone With an Eating Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If the person is having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Eating disorders can cause serious medical complications. If someone faints, has chest pain, is confused, or seems very unwell, call 911. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 for health advice. Young people can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

Supporting someone with an eating disorder involves expressing care without judgment, avoiding comments about food, weight, or appearance, encouraging specialized professional help, and looking after your own wellbeing. Eating disorders are serious mental health conditions that affect both body and mind. They are not choices or phases, and they can affect people of any age, body size, or background. The person may feel ashamed, defensive, or unable to see how unwell they are. Your support can make a real difference, especially in encouraging them toward treatment, but you do not need to have all the answers.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

Eating disorders, including anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating disorder, often involve intense preoccupation with food, weight, or shape, and behaviours such as restricting, bingeing, purging, or compulsive exercise. For many people, the eating disorder serves a purpose, such as managing overwhelming emotions, providing a sense of control, or coping with stress or trauma.

The person may feel caught between wanting to get better and fearing what recovery would mean. They may hide behaviours, feel intense shame, or become anxious or irritable around meals. Some do not recognize that they are unwell, especially when the disorder affects thinking. Social messages about weight and bodies, explored on the page about diet culture, can make things harder.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Choose a private, calm moment to share concern, focusing on specific behaviours or feelings you have noticed, such as "I have noticed you seem stressed at meals, and I care about you."
  • Listen without judgment. Let them share at their own pace.
  • Focus on feelings, not food. Ask how they are coping emotionally.
  • Be patient. They may deny or minimize at first. Keep the door open.
  • Include them in regular activities that are not centred on food.
  • Model balanced attitudes toward food and bodies.
  • Learn about eating disorders to understand what they are facing.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Commenting on weight, shape, or appearance, even positively.
  • Monitoring or policing food unless guided by their treatment team.
  • Simple solutions, such as "just eat more" or "just stop bingeing."
  • Blaming or shaming, which can increase secrecy.
  • Talking about diets or labelling foods as good or bad around them.
  • Power struggles over food, which often increase distress.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting someone with an eating disorder can be emotionally draining. It is common to feel worried, frustrated, helpless, or guilty. Keep up your own routines, relationships, and activities. Seek support for yourself, such as a counsellor, a support group for families, or trusted friends. Remember that recovery is the person's journey, and you cannot control it. Setting boundaries, such as not discussing numbers or calories, can protect both of you. For parents supporting a child or teen, family-based treatment involves parents directly, with guidance from professionals, and it is important to have support for that role.

Encouraging Professional Help

Eating disorders respond best to early, specialized treatment, often involving a doctor, therapist, and dietitian. Encourage the person to see their family doctor for a medical check, since eating disorders can affect the heart, bones, and other organs. Offer practical help, such as finding a therapist or going with them to appointments. The page about finding an eating-disorder-informed therapist can help. In BC, a family doctor can refer to health authority eating disorder programs.

For young people under eighteen, parents or guardians should be involved in seeking care. If the person faints, has chest pain, is confused, is vomiting frequently, or expresses thoughts of suicide, seek urgent medical help or call 911.

Common Questions About Supporting Someone With an Eating Disorder

How do I tell someone I am worried about their eating?

Choose a calm, private moment, share specific observations with care, and focus on feelings rather than food or weight.

What should I avoid saying to someone with an eating disorder?

Avoid comments on weight or appearance, simple solutions, blame, diet talk, and labelling foods as good or bad.

Why does my loved one deny having an eating disorder?

Shame, fear of change, and the way eating disorders affect thinking can make it hard to recognize or admit.

Should I watch what my loved one eats while they are in eating disorder treatment?

Usually not, unless guided by their treatment team. Monitoring can increase secrecy and conflict.

What is family-based treatment for eating disorders?

It is an evidence-based approach for young people in which parents take an active role in supporting recovery, guided by professionals.

When is an eating disorder a medical emergency?

Fainting, chest pain, confusion, frequent vomiting, or thoughts of suicide need urgent medical help or a call to 911.

My friend looks healthy to me. Could they still be seriously unwell with an eating disorder?

Yes. Eating disorders affect people of all body sizes, ages, and backgrounds, and appearance does not show how unwell someone is.

How can I look after myself while supporting someone with an eating disorder?

Keep your own routines and relationships, set boundaries, and seek support from a counsellor or family group.

How do I help someone find eating disorder treatment in BC?

Encourage a visit to their family doctor, who can check medical health and refer to specialized programs.

Why should I avoid complimenting weight changes?

Even positive comments about weight or appearance can reinforce the focus on body size that fuels the eating disorder.
