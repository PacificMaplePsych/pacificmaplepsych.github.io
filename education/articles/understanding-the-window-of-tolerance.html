Understanding the Window of Tolerance

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

The window of tolerance is a concept, introduced by psychiatrist Dan Siegel, describing the zone of arousal in which a person can experience stress and emotion while still thinking clearly and staying connected to the present. Inside the window, people can feel upset, sad, or anxious but still cope. When stress pushes them outside it, they move into hyperarousal, such as panic, anger, or overwhelm, or hypoarousal, such as numbness, shutdown, or disconnection. The concept is widely used in trauma-informed therapy to help people understand their reactions and build capacity to stay regulated.

What the Window of Tolerance Looks Like

Inside the window. You can feel emotions without being overwhelmed, think things through, communicate, and respond flexibly.

Above the window (hyperarousal). Racing heart, panic, anger, irritability, racing thoughts, feeling on edge, or an urge to fight or flee.

Below the window (hypoarousal). Numbness, exhaustion, blankness, freezing, feeling disconnected, or disconnected from your body.

At work, moving above the window might look like snapping at a colleague. At home, moving below it might look like shutting down during an argument. People can move between states quickly, sometimes swinging from panic to numbness.

Why It Happens

Everyone's window has a size, shaped by temperament, life experiences, and current circumstances. Trauma, especially early or repeated trauma, can narrow the window, making it easier to be pushed into fight, flight, or freeze by smaller stressors. Chronic stress, poor sleep, illness, hunger, and burnout also narrow it temporarily. Supportive relationships, safety, and regulation skills tend to widen it. The concept draws on how the nervous system responds to perceived threat, including the balance between activation and shutdown.

Understanding the window can reduce shame. Reactions such as snapping or shutting down are not character flaws but signs that the nervous system has moved outside its current capacity. That framing helps people focus on returning to the window rather than criticizing themselves.

A simple way to use the idea day to day is to check in with yourself a few times a day. Ask whether you feel calm and present, revved up, or flat and far away. Noticing your state early, before a full reaction, gives you more choice about what to do next, such as taking a break, moving your body, or reaching out to someone you trust. Partners and families can also learn each other's signs, which reduces conflict during stressful moments.

What It Is Not

  • Not a diagnosis. It is a framework for understanding arousal and regulation, not a condition.
  • Not fixed. The window can widen with support, safety, and practice.
  • Not only about trauma. Anyone can be pushed outside their window by stress or fatigue.
  • Not about avoiding all discomfort. Growth often happens at the edge of the window, where challenge is manageable.
  • Not a replacement for trauma treatment. It is often used alongside trauma-focused therapy, not instead of it.

How It Is Used in Treatment

Therapists use the window of tolerance to help clients notice their early warning signs of moving outside it and learn skills to return. For hyperarousal, this might include slow breathing, movement, or grounding. For hypoarousal, it might include gentle activation, such as standing, stretching, or engaging the senses. Trauma-informed therapy paces processing work so that clients stay within, or near, their window, avoiding overwhelm. Over time, as trauma is processed and regulation skills grow, many people find their window widens and they cope with more.

Common Questions About the Window of Tolerance

What is the window of tolerance?

It is the zone in which you can experience stress and emotion while still thinking clearly and staying present. Outside it, people become overwhelmed or shut down.

What does hyperarousal feel like?

Hyperarousal feels like being revved up: racing heart, anxiety, panic, anger, irritability, and racing thoughts, often with an urge to fight or flee.

Why do I go numb and blank when things get too much?

Hypoarousal feels like shutting down: numbness, exhaustion, blankness, disconnection, or freezing, with little energy to respond.

Why does trauma narrow the window of tolerance?

Trauma can leave the nervous system more sensitive to threat, so smaller stressors push people into fight, flight, or freeze more easily.

Can the window of tolerance get bigger?

Yes. Safety, supportive relationships, regulation skills, and trauma processing can widen the window over time.

How can I get back into my window of tolerance when overwhelmed?

For overwhelm, try slow breathing, grounding, or movement. For shutdown, try gentle activation such as standing, stretching, or engaging your senses.

Who created the window of tolerance concept?

Psychiatrist Dan Siegel introduced the term, and it is now widely used in trauma-informed therapy and education.

Does sleep affect my window of tolerance?

Yes. Poor sleep, hunger, illness, and chronic stress all narrow the window temporarily, making overwhelm or shutdown more likely.

How do therapists use the window of tolerance in trauma work?

They help clients recognize when they move outside the window, teach regulation skills, and pace trauma processing so clients stay within manageable limits.

Is shutting down during stress a sign I am outside my window?

Often, yes. Shutting down, going blank, or feeling numb can indicate hypoarousal, a sign that stress has exceeded your current capacity.
