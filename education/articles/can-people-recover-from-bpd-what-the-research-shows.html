Can People Recover From BPD? What the Research Shows

Yes. Research shows that most people with borderline personality disorder (BPD) improve substantially over time. In two large long-term studies in the United States, the large majority of participants no longer met diagnostic criteria for BPD within ten years, and once symptoms eased, relapse was relatively uncommon. Some symptoms, such as self-harm and impulsive behaviour, often improve first, while feelings of emptiness, loneliness, and fear of abandonment may take longer. Full recovery, meaning both fewer symptoms and good functioning in work and relationships, is also possible, though it can take more time and support. Effective treatment can speed up improvement and reduce risk along the way.

What the Long-Term Studies Found

Two major studies followed people with BPD for a decade or more. The McLean Study of Adult Development, led by Mary Zanarini, and the Collaborative Longitudinal Personality Disorders Study both found that most participants experienced remission, meaning they no longer met the criteria for BPD for a sustained period. These findings challenged the older belief that BPD is lifelong and untreatable, a belief described on the page about the history of borderline personality disorder.

The studies also showed that:

  • Acute symptoms improve faster. Self-harm, suicide attempts, and impulsive behaviours often decrease earlier.
  • Some feelings take longer. Chronic emptiness, intense anger, and abandonment fears tend to change more slowly.
  • Functioning matters. Stable work, study, and relationships were harder to achieve than symptom remission for some participants, which is why treatment often focuses on building a meaningful life, not only reducing symptoms.

Remission and Recovery

Remission usually means no longer meeting enough criteria for a BPD diagnosis. Recovery is broader and often includes good relationships, meaningful work or study, and a stable sense of self. Many people describe recovery as building what Marsha Linehan called "a life worth living," which looks different for each person.

What Helps People Recover

Evidence-Based Therapy

Therapies with strong research support include dialectical behaviour therapy, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, schema therapy, and good psychiatric management. They share a structured approach, a focus on emotions and relationships, and a consistent therapeutic relationship.

Skills for Emotions

Learning to name, tolerate, and regulate intense emotions reduces crises, as described on the page about DBT skills for emotional regulation.

Stable Relationships and Routines

Supportive relationships, work or study, and regular routines for sleep and activity help protect progress.

Treating Co-Occurring Conditions

Depression, PTSD, substance use, and eating problems often occur alongside BPD. Addressing them supports overall recovery.

What It Is Not

Recovery does not mean never having strong emotions again, and it is rarely a straight line. Setbacks during stressful periods are common and do not erase progress. Medication can help with specific symptoms, such as depression or sleep problems, but no medication treats BPD itself, so therapy remains central. The page about how borderline personality disorder is treated describes treatment options in more detail.

A Reason for Hope

If you have BPD, or love someone who does, the research offers genuine reason for optimism. Change is possible, and many people who once felt overwhelmed by their emotions go on to have stable relationships, careers, and a sense of who they are. Myths about BPD can make this harder to believe, as explained on the page about BPD stigma and common myths.

Common Questions About Recovery From BPD

Do most people with borderline personality disorder eventually stop meeting criteria?

Yes. Long-term studies show most people with borderline personality disorder eventually no longer meet diagnostic criteria, especially over several years.

What percentage of people recover from BPD?

Long-term studies found that the large majority of people with BPD reached remission within ten years, though full recovery of functioning took longer for some.

Is BPD recovery measured in months or years?

Usually years. Some BPD symptoms improve within months of treatment, but long-term studies show most remission happens over several years.

Do self-harm and impulsivity ease before emptiness in BPD recovery?

Often, yes. In BPD recovery, self-harm and impulsive behaviours tend to improve earlier, while chronic emptiness and abandonment fears usually take longer.

What is the difference between remission and recovery in BPD?

Remission in BPD means no longer meeting diagnostic criteria, while recovery also includes good relationships, work, and a stable sense of self.

Does BPD get better with age?

BPD symptoms often lessen with age, particularly impulsive and self-harming behaviours, and treatment can speed up improvement.

What therapy works best for BPD recovery?

DBT, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, schema therapy, and good psychiatric management all have evidence for BPD.

Why is therapy, not medication, the main treatment for BPD recovery?

No medication treats BPD itself, so recovery relies mainly on therapy, with medication used only for specific symptoms such as depression or sleep problems.

Can people with BPD relapse?

Relapse after remission is relatively uncommon in BPD, though setbacks during stressful periods can happen and do not erase progress.

Can people with BPD have healthy relationships?

Yes. Many people with BPD build healthy, stable relationships, especially as emotional regulation and self-understanding grow.
