Why Do I Get Defensive When Given Feedback

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Getting defensive when given feedback usually happens because criticism feels like a threat to who you are, not just to what you did. The body reacts as though under attack, and the mind rushes to protect itself by explaining, justifying, or pushing back. This is often driven by shame, perfectionism, rejection sensitivity, or a history of harsh criticism. Defensiveness is a very human reaction, but when it becomes automatic, it can strain relationships at work and at home.

People describe feeling their face get hot when a manager suggests a change, immediately listing reasons why they were right, or later realizing the feedback was fair and feeling embarrassed about how they reacted.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Shame. When feedback feels like proof of being inadequate, defending yourself is a way to push that painful feeling away.

Perfectionism. People who feel they have to be perfect may experience any criticism as failure.

Rejection sensitivity. Some people, including many adults with ADHD, feel rejected intensely by small comments, which can trigger a quick defensive reaction.

Past criticism. Growing up with frequent criticism, or working in harsh environments, can make feedback feel dangerous.

Low self-esteem. When self-worth is fragile, feedback can feel like confirmation of your worst fears about yourself.

How the feedback is delivered. Sometimes defensiveness is a response to feedback that is unfair, harsh, or poorly timed. Not all defensiveness is a personal pattern.

When It Looks More Like a Relationship Pattern

In close relationships, defensiveness is one of the patterns couples researchers link with ongoing conflict. When one person raises a concern and the other defends, the first person often feels unheard and presses harder, which increases defensiveness further. Signs that defensiveness has become a pattern include:

  • Responding to complaints with counter-complaints, such as "well, you also..."
  • Explaining your intentions instead of acknowledging the impact on the other person.
  • Feeling attacked even when feedback is offered gently.
  • Arguments that escalate from small concerns into big fights.
  • Others hesitating to raise concerns with you.
  • Difficulty saying sorry, explored on the page about struggling to apologize.

Defensiveness usually protects against shame. Recognizing that the reaction is about self-protection, rather than the facts of the situation, can help you pause and respond more openly. Taking in feedback does not mean agreeing with everything. It means listening first and deciding afterward what is useful.

What Can Help

  • Notice the body signal. A hot face, tight chest, or urge to interrupt can alert you that defensiveness is rising.
  • Pause before responding. Taking a breath or saying "let me think about that" buys time for the reaction to settle.
  • Listen for the kernel of truth. Even imperfect feedback often contains something useful.
  • Separate behaviour from self. Feedback on an action is not a verdict on your worth.
  • Acknowledge before explaining. "I can see how that affected you" helps the other person feel heard. Explanations can come later if needed.
  • Ask for feedback in small doses. Regular, low-stakes feedback helps build tolerance over time.

Common Questions About Getting Defensive With Feedback

Why do I feel attacked when someone gives me feedback?

Feedback can trigger shame or fear of inadequacy, which the body treats as a threat. The defensive reaction is a form of self-protection, even when the feedback is well-intended.

Is defensiveness a sign of low self-esteem?

It often is. When self-worth feels fragile, criticism can seem to confirm negative beliefs about yourself, so pushing back feels necessary for self-protection.

How does defensiveness affect relationships?

Defensiveness can leave others feeling unheard, escalate conflicts, and discourage people from raising concerns. Over time it can erode trust and closeness.

Can ADHD make me defensive about criticism?

It can. Many adults with ADHD have experienced frequent criticism and may have strong rejection sensitivity, which makes feedback feel especially painful.

What can I do in the moment when a manager's feedback makes me bristle?

Pause before responding, listen fully, thank the person, and take time to reflect before deciding what to act on. Asking clarifying questions also helps shift into curiosity.

What is the difference between defensiveness and standing up for myself?

Standing up for yourself involves calmly sharing your view after listening. Defensiveness is an automatic reaction that blocks listening and focuses on protecting yourself.

Why do I explain my intentions instead of accepting criticism?

Explaining intentions can feel like proving you are not a bad person. Acknowledging the impact first, then explaining if needed, usually leads to better conversations.

How can I respond better when my partner criticizes me?

Try to listen for the need behind the complaint, acknowledge how your partner feels, and take responsibility for your part before offering your perspective.

Can past criticism from parents make me defensive now?

Yes. Frequent or harsh criticism in childhood can make feedback feel threatening in adulthood, even when it is offered kindly.

How does therapy help with defensiveness?

Therapy helps identify the shame or fear behind defensiveness, build self-worth, and practise more open responses, making feedback feel less threatening over time.
