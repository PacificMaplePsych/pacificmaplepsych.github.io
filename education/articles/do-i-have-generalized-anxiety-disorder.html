Do I Have Generalized Anxiety Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Generalized anxiety disorder, often called GAD, is a common anxiety condition characterized by persistent, excessive worry about many different areas of life, such as health, work, money, family, and everyday responsibilities. The worry is hard to control and is often accompanied by physical symptoms like muscle tension, restlessness, fatigue, and trouble sleeping. Many people with GAD describe themselves as lifelong worriers. Everyone worries sometimes, especially during stressful periods. GAD is diagnosed when worry is present on more days than not for at least six months and significantly affects daily life. It responds well to treatment.

What Generalized Anxiety Disorder Looks Like

  • Worrying about many things, often jumping from one concern to another.
  • Finding it very hard to stop or control worry, similar to being unable to stop overthinking.
  • Feeling on edge or restless.
  • Tiring easily and feeling drained.
  • Difficulty concentrating or feeling that your mind goes blank.
  • Irritability.
  • Muscle tension, headaches, or jaw clenching.
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep, or restless sleep.
  • Seeking reassurance, over-preparing, or avoiding situations to reduce worry.

Many people with GAD appear calm and capable on the outside. Their worry may be invisible to others, as described on the page about high-functioning anxiety.

Why It Happens

GAD develops from a combination of genetic, biological, and psychological factors. It often runs in families. Some people have a temperament that is more sensitive to threat and uncertainty. Stressful life events, chronic stress, and growing up in unpredictable or anxious environments increase risk.

A key feature of GAD is difficulty tolerating uncertainty. When outcomes are unknown, worry can feel like a way to prepare for or prevent problems. Many people with GAD hold beliefs that worrying is useful, such as it keeps me prepared, alongside fears that worry is dangerous or uncontrollable. Worry can also function as a way of avoiding difficult emotions. These patterns keep worry going. GAD is one of several types of anxiety described on the page about anxiety disorders.

GAD commonly occurs alongside depression, other anxiety disorders, and physical health problems. Over time, constant worry can contribute to exhaustion, sleep problems, and strained relationships. Many people live with GAD for years before seeking help, believing it is simply their personality.

Worry in GAD often focuses on everyday matters, such as being late, a family member's safety, or a small mistake at work. Because these topics seem ordinary, people may not realize how much time and energy the worry consumes until they start tracking it.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary worry. GAD involves persistent, excessive worry that is hard to control and affects daily life.
  • Not a personality flaw. It is a common, treatable health condition.
  • Not always about one big issue. Worry often shifts between many topics.
  • Not something you must live with. Effective treatments are available.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about the nature and duration of worry, physical symptoms, and effects on daily life, and may use questionnaires. A physician may check for medical conditions or substances that can cause anxiety. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most studied psychological treatment for GAD. It helps people recognize worry patterns, test beliefs about worry, reduce reassurance-seeking and avoidance, and build tolerance for uncertainty. Techniques such as scheduled worry time and problem-solving are often used. Mindfulness and acceptance-based approaches help people step back from worry. Applied relaxation can ease physical tension. Medications may be recommended for moderate to severe GAD. Regular exercise, sleep, and limiting caffeine and alcohol support recovery.

Common Questions About Generalized Anxiety Disorder

How do I know if my worrying is generalized anxiety disorder?

GAD involves excessive, hard-to-control worry about many things on most days for at least six months, with physical symptoms and daily impact.

What are the physical symptoms of GAD?

Common physical symptoms include muscle tension, restlessness, fatigue, headaches, and trouble sleeping.

Why do I worry about everything?

In GAD, difficulty tolerating uncertainty and beliefs that worry is useful can keep the mind jumping from one concern to the next.

Is being a worrier the same as having GAD?

Not always. GAD is diagnosed when worry is persistent, excessive, and significantly affects daily life.

Can GAD cause insomnia?

Yes. Worry and tension often make it hard to fall or stay asleep.

What is scheduled worry time?

It is a technique where you set aside a short time each day to worry, postponing worries at other times.

Does GAD often occur with depression?

Yes. GAD commonly co-occurs with depression and other anxiety disorders.

What therapy works best for generalized anxiety?

Cognitive behavioural therapy has the strongest evidence, and mindfulness and acceptance-based approaches also help.

Why do I feel like worrying keeps me safe?

Many people with GAD believe worry helps them prepare, which makes it hard to let go even when it causes distress.

Will I always be a worrier?

Not necessarily. Many people improve significantly with treatment and learn skills to manage worry in the future.
