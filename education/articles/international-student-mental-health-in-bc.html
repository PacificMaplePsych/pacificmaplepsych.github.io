International Student Mental Health in BC

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

International students in BC often face a unique mix of pressures: homesickness, culture shock, language barriers, academic expectations, financial stress, uncertainty about immigration and work permits, loneliness, and pressure to succeed for family back home. These stresses can lead to anxiety, depression, sleep problems, and burnout, yet many international students hesitate to seek help because of stigma, unfamiliarity with mental health services, cost concerns, or worry that it could affect their studies or status. Support is available through campus counselling, student health plans, community services, and private therapy, including therapy in languages other than English.

Common Challenges

  • Homesickness and missing family, food, and familiar routines
  • Culture shock and adjusting to new social norms, as described on the page about adjusting to life in a new country
  • Loneliness, especially in the first year, as described on the page about feeling lonely after moving to a new city
  • Academic pressure and different teaching styles
  • Language barriers and fatigue from working in another language
  • Financial stress, including high tuition and living costs
  • Uncertainty about work permits, careers, and future plans
  • Family expectations and fear of disappointing others
  • Discrimination or feeling like an outsider

Signs You May Need Support

  • Persistent sadness, worry, or irritability
  • Trouble sleeping or sleeping much more than usual
  • Difficulty concentrating or falling behind
  • Withdrawing from classmates or activities
  • Changes in appetite
  • Using alcohol or other substances to cope
  • Feeling hopeless or overwhelmed

Where to Find Support

On Campus

Most BC colleges and universities offer free counselling, wellness programs, international student advisors, and accessibility services, as described on the page about academic accommodations at college or university in BC.

Health Coverage

International students studying in BC for six months or more are generally required to enrol in MSP, though MSP does not cover private therapy, as described on the page about what MSP covers. Many student health plans include some coverage for counselling, so check your plan.

Community and Private Support

Community organizations, cultural associations, and private therapists offer additional support. Therapy in your first language can make it easier to express yourself, as described on the page about therapy in Punjabi, Cantonese, Mandarin, and Urdu.

What Helps

  • Build connection through clubs, cultural groups, and shared activities.
  • Keep routines for sleep, meals, and exercise.
  • Stay in touch with family while also building local support.
  • Ask for help early with academics, finances, or mental health.
  • Be patient with yourself. Adjustment takes time.

When Family Expectations Feel Heavy

Many international students carry a sense of responsibility to their families, who may have made significant sacrifices for them to study abroad. This can create pressure to succeed, reluctance to share struggles, and guilt about taking time for rest or social life. Some students worry that admitting they are having a hard time will disappoint or worry their family. It can help to remember that struggling with adjustment is common and does not mean you are failing. Talking with an advisor, counsellor, or trusted friend can help you think through how much to share with family and how to balance their expectations with your own wellbeing. In some cases, a counsellor who understands your cultural background can make these conversations easier.

What It Is Not

Struggling as an international student is not a sign that you made the wrong choice or that you are weak. Seeking counselling is confidential and will not appear on your academic record. The page about whether therapy is confidential explains privacy and its limits.

Common Questions About International Student Mental Health

What mental health challenges do international students face?

International students often face homesickness, culture shock, loneliness, academic pressure, financial stress, and uncertainty about the future.

Where can international students get counselling in BC?

International students in BC can get counselling through campus services, student health plans, community organizations, and private therapists.

Does MSP cover therapy for international students?

No. MSP does not cover private therapy, though many student health plans include some counselling coverage.

Do international students in BC need MSP?

International students studying in BC for six months or more are generally required to enrol in MSP.

Will seeing a counsellor affect my student visa?

Seeing a counsellor is confidential and does not appear on your academic record.

How can international students cope with homesickness?

International students can cope with homesickness by building local connections, keeping routines, and staying in touch with family.

How long does culture shock last for international students?

Culture shock often eases over the first several months as international students build routines and connections, though it varies from person to person.

Is counselling available for international students in languages other than English?

Yes. Some campus services and private clinics in BC offer counselling in other languages. Pacific Maple Psych Centre offers therapy in Punjabi, Cantonese, Mandarin, and Urdu.

What are signs an international student needs support?

Signs include persistent sadness or worry, sleep changes, falling behind, withdrawing, and using substances to cope.

Is it normal for international students to feel lonely?

Yes. Loneliness is very common among international students, especially in the first year.
