Complicated Grief and Prolonged Grief Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Intense, prolonged grief can bring thoughts of wanting to join the person who died. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Grief is a natural response to losing someone we love, and for most people it gradually softens over time, even though the loss is never forgotten. For some, however, grief remains intense, consuming, and disabling long after the death, interfering with daily life and the ability to move forward. This has been called complicated grief, and since 2022 it has been recognized in diagnostic manuals as prolonged grief disorder. In adults, it is typically considered when intense grief continues for at least twelve months after a death, or six months in the ICD-11 classification, along with significant difficulty functioning. Prolonged grief is not a sign of loving too much or grieving wrongly, and it responds to specialized treatment.

What Prolonged Grief Looks Like

  • Intense yearning or longing for the person who died, most days.
  • Preoccupation with thoughts or memories of the person.
  • Feeling as though part of yourself has died.
  • Difficulty accepting that the death happened.
  • Avoiding reminders that the person has died.
  • Intense emotional pain, such as sorrow, anger, or guilt.
  • Difficulty re-engaging with friends, activities, or plans.
  • Emotional numbness or feeling that life is meaningless.
  • Intense loneliness or feeling detached from others.

Everyone grieves differently, and many of these feelings are part of normal grief, especially early on. What sets prolonged grief apart is the intensity, the persistence, and the degree to which it disrupts life. Grief that resurfaces around anniversaries, as described on the page about grief years later, is usually not prolonged grief disorder.

Why It Happens

Prolonged grief is more likely in certain circumstances. Sudden, violent, or traumatic deaths, including suicide, homicide, and accidents, increase risk, as described on the pages about sudden or traumatic death and suicide loss. The death of a child or partner, a very close or dependent relationship, a history of depression or anxiety, previous losses, and limited social support also raise risk. Feelings of guilt or anger, and beliefs such as I should have prevented it, can keep grief stuck.

Researchers describe several processes that can keep grief from softening. Avoiding reminders of the loss can prevent processing, while ruminating about the death can deepen distress. Difficulty imagining a meaningful future without the person can make it hard to re-engage with life. Grief rarely follows set stages, as explained on the page about grief and its stages.

Prolonged grief can affect physical health, sleep, work, and relationships. Friends and family may not know how to help, or may encourage the person to move on, which can increase isolation. Recognizing prolonged grief as a treatable condition can open the door to support.

What It Is Not

  • Not a measure of how much you loved someone. Intense grief reflects many factors, not love alone.
  • Not depression, though the two can occur together. Prolonged grief centres on yearning and the loss.
  • Not a requirement to let go. Healing often involves maintaining a meaningful connection while re-engaging with life.
  • Not something to wait out indefinitely. Specialized treatment can help.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about the loss, grief symptoms, how long they have lasted, and how daily life is affected, and will check for depression, PTSD, and suicide risk. Prolonged grief disorder therapy, developed from research on complicated grief, has strong evidence. It combines elements of cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal therapy, and exposure, helping people revisit the story of the death, reduce avoidance, process guilt and anger, and rebuild a meaningful life while honouring the relationship. Grief-focused cognitive behavioural therapy is also effective. Support groups and connection with others who have experienced similar losses can reduce isolation.

Common Questions About Complicated and Prolonged Grief

When does grief become prolonged grief disorder?

It is considered when intense grief lasts at least twelve months in adults, or six months under ICD-11, and significantly disrupts daily life.

How is prolonged grief different from normal grief?

Normal grief softens over time. Prolonged grief remains intense and consuming, with deep yearning and difficulty re-engaging with life.

Who is at higher risk of complicated grief?

Risk is higher after sudden or violent deaths, the loss of a child or partner, and for people with limited support or past mental health concerns.

How can clinicians tell prolonged grief apart from depression?

They look at the focus. The two can occur together, but prolonged grief centres on yearning and preoccupation with the person who died.

Why can I not accept that my loved one has died?

Difficulty accepting the death is a common feature of prolonged grief, especially after sudden or traumatic losses.

Does prolonged grief mean I loved the person more?

No. The intensity of grief reflects many factors, including circumstances of the death and support, not love alone.

What therapy helps with prolonged grief?

Prolonged grief disorder therapy and grief-focused CBT have strong evidence for helping people move forward.

Do I have to let go of my loved one to heal?

No. Healing often involves keeping a meaningful connection while gradually re-engaging with life.

How can I support someone whose grief is not easing?

Stay present, avoid pressuring them to move on, and gently encourage professional support.

When was prolonged grief disorder recognized as a diagnosis?

It was added to the ICD-11 and to the DSM-5-TR in 2022.
