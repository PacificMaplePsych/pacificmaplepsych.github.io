Why Depression Risk Rises in Perimenopause

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Research shows that the risk of depression rises during perimenopause, the transition leading up to menopause, compared with the years before it. This includes people who have never been depressed before, although those with a history of depression, postpartum depression, or PMDD are at higher risk. Fluctuating estrogen affects brain systems involved in mood, and symptoms such as night sweats and insomnia, along with midlife stresses, add to the strain. Perimenopausal depression can look different from the stereotype, often showing up as irritability, anxiety, low motivation, and feeling flat rather than obvious sadness. It is treatable with therapy, medication, or both.

How Perimenopausal Depression Can Look

  • Irritability and anger rather than sadness, as described on the page about menopause rage.
  • Anxiety and worry, sometimes with panic.
  • Loss of interest and enjoyment, as described on the page about anhedonia.
  • Fatigue and low motivation.
  • Trouble concentrating that overlaps with brain fog.
  • Sleep problems, which can both cause and result from depression.
  • Feeling hopeless, worthless, or like a burden.

Why the Risk Increases

Hormonal Fluctuation

It is not simply low estrogen but the unpredictable rise and fall of hormones that seems to affect mood most. Some people appear more sensitive to these changes, which may explain why a history of PMDD or postpartum depression increases risk, as described on the page about PMDD.

Sleep Disruption

Night sweats and insomnia can lead to chronic sleep loss, a strong risk factor for depression, as described on the page about insomnia during menopause.

Midlife Stress and Change

Caregiving, children leaving home, health concerns, relationship changes, and shifts in identity often cluster in midlife, as described on the page about midlife transitions.

Body and Identity

Changes in the body, fertility, and how others see you can bring grief or loss of confidence.

What It Is Not

Depression in perimenopause is not an inevitable part of aging, and it is not a personal weakness. It is also not always hormonal. Depression has many contributors, and a physician should check for other medical causes, such as thyroid problems. Normal ups and downs are different from depression that lasts most days for two weeks or more and affects daily life, as described on the page about what depression is.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

  • Talk to a physician. They can assess menopausal symptoms, rule out medical causes, and discuss treatment options.
  • Therapy. Cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation, and interpersonal approaches are effective for depression, as described on the page about how depression is treated.
  • Medication. Antidepressants are effective for perimenopausal depression, and some also reduce hot flushes. Hormone therapy may help mood in some people during perimenopause but is not a stand-alone treatment for depression.
  • Treating sleep. Improving sleep often improves mood.
  • Lifestyle supports. Regular exercise, social connection, and limiting alcohol support recovery.

If you notice your mood changing in your 40s or early 50s, especially alongside cycle changes, mentioning both to your physician can help connect the dots. The page about perimenopause and mental health describes the wider transition.

Talking to Your Physician

It can help to prepare for your appointment by noting your mood changes, sleep, cycle changes, and any history of depression, postpartum depression, or premenstrual mood changes. Mentioning both mood and menopausal symptoms helps your physician consider the full picture and discuss treatment options that fit your situation.

Common Questions About Depression in Perimenopause

Does perimenopause cause depression?

Perimenopause increases the risk of depression through hormonal fluctuation, sleep disruption, and midlife stress, even in people never depressed before.

Who is most at risk of depression in perimenopause?

People with a history of depression, postpartum depression, or PMDD have a higher risk of depression in perimenopause.

How can perimenopausal depression show up besides sadness?

Perimenopausal depression often shows up as irritability, anxiety, fatigue, low motivation, and loss of enjoyment, sometimes more than sadness.

Is it depression or just perimenopause?

If low mood, irritability, or loss of interest lasts most days for two weeks or more and affects daily life, it may be depression and deserves assessment.

Can hormone therapy treat depression in perimenopause?

Hormone therapy may help mood for some people in perimenopause, but it is not a stand-alone treatment for depression.

Do antidepressants work for perimenopausal depression?

Yes. Antidepressants are effective for perimenopausal depression, and some also help reduce hot flushes.

Can therapy help with depression during menopause?

Yes. CBT, behavioural activation, and interpersonal therapy are effective for depression during perimenopause and menopause.

Can poor sleep cause depression in perimenopause?

Yes. Chronic sleep loss from night sweats or insomnia is a strong risk factor for depression in perimenopause.

Does depression risk go down after menopause?

For many people, depression risk decreases after menopause once hormone levels settle, though support is important at any stage.

When should I get help for low mood in perimenopause?

Get help for low mood in perimenopause if it lasts two weeks or more, affects daily life, or includes thoughts of hopelessness or suicide.
