How to Prepare for an ADHD or Autism Assessment

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

To prepare for an ADHD or autism assessment, it helps to write down the reasons you are seeking assessment with specific examples, gather records such as school report cards and past assessments, ask someone who knew you as a child to share their observations, list medications and health conditions, and plan for testing days by resting well and eating beforehand. You do not need to prove anything or study for the assessment. The goal is to give the clinician an honest, detailed picture of your experiences across your life, so that the results reflect you accurately. Clinics often send intake questionnaires in advance, and completing them carefully is one of the most useful steps.

How It Works

ADHD and autism assessments usually include:

  • Intake questionnaires. About current concerns, development, health, and mental health history.
  • A clinical interview. A detailed conversation about childhood, school, work, relationships, and daily life.
  • Rating scales. Completed by you and often by a partner, parent, or close friend.
  • Standardized measures. Such as tests of attention, executive functioning, or structured observation of social communication, depending on the questions.
  • A report and feedback session. Where results and recommendations are explained, as described on the page about feedback sessions.

Both ADHD and autism are developmental, so clinicians look for signs that were present from childhood, even if they were masked or went unnoticed. The pages about how ADHD is diagnosed in adults and how autism is diagnosed in adults explain what clinicians look for.

Practical Details

Referrals. Private assessments in BC can usually be booked without a referral, though some benefit plans or public programs require one.

Time involved. Assessments often take several appointments, and the report can take a few weeks afterward.

Cost. Private assessments are not covered by MSP. Fees vary by clinic and assessment type, and some extended health plans cover part of the cost.

Medication. If you take ADHD medication, ask the clinic whether to take it as usual on testing days.

Virtual or in person. Interviews can often be done virtually, while some testing works best in person.

Both conditions. ADHD and autism often occur together, so you can ask whether both can be assessed at once. The page about autism and ADHD together has more detail.

How to Prepare

  • List your reasons with examples. Describe specific situations at school, work, home, and in relationships where you have struggled or noticed differences.
  • Think about childhood. Note what you remember about attention, behaviour, friendships, routines, sensory experiences, and interests.
  • Gather records. School report cards, teacher comments, past assessments, and relevant medical notes.
  • Ask a parent or close person. Someone who knew you as a child may be asked to complete questionnaires or share memories.
  • Note masking and coping strategies. Effort you put into fitting in or compensating is important information.
  • List health information. Medications, sleep, mental health history, and substance use.
  • Plan testing days. Sleep well, eat, bring water and snacks, and wear comfortable clothes. Bring glasses or hearing aids if you use them.
  • Prepare questions. About the process, timing, report, and what happens next.

Try to be honest rather than presenting your best or worst self. Accurate information leads to the most useful results.

Common Questions About Preparing for an ADHD or Autism Assessment

What are the most useful steps before an ADHD or autism assessment?

Write down specific examples of your struggles, gather school and medical records, ask someone who knew you as a child for input, and rest well before testing for an ADHD or autism assessment.

Do I need to study for an ADHD assessment?

No. You do not need to study for an ADHD assessment. The goal is an honest picture of your experiences, not a test to pass.

Why are early-life memories important when preparing for an autism evaluation?

Autism is developmental, so autism assessments look for signs present from childhood, even if they were masked or went unnoticed.

What records should I bring to an ADHD or autism assessment?

Helpful records include school report cards, teacher comments, past assessments, and relevant medical notes for an ADHD or autism assessment.

Who else might be involved in my autism assessment?

A parent, partner, or close friend may be asked to complete questionnaires or share memories as part of your autism assessment.

Should I take my ADHD medication before testing?

Ask the clinic whether to take ADHD medication as usual on testing days, since instructions can differ by assessment.

Why should I mention masking in an autism or ADHD assessment?

Masking and compensating can hide difficulties, so describing the effort involved helps the clinician understand your experience in an autism or ADHD assessment.

Can an ADHD or autism assessment be done virtually?

Interviews for an ADHD or autism assessment can often be done virtually, while some testing works best in person.

What should I do on the day of an ADHD or autism testing session?

On testing day, sleep well, eat beforehand, bring water and snacks, wear comfortable clothes, and bring glasses or hearing aids for your ADHD or autism testing.

What questions are worth asking the clinician during an ADHD or autism assessment?

Useful questions cover the ADHD or autism assessment process, timing, the report, and what happens after the results.
