Supporting Siblings of Neurodivergent Children

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Siblings of neurodivergent children, such as autistic children or children with ADHD or learning differences, often feel a mix of love, pride, protectiveness, frustration, jealousy, embarrassment, and worry. They may notice that their brother or sister gets more attention, feel pressure to be the "easy" child, or take on extra responsibilities. Many also develop empathy, patience, and maturity. Parents can support siblings by explaining their sibling's differences in age-appropriate ways, making space for all of their feelings, spending one-on-one time with them, and protecting their own interests and activities. Siblings do best when they feel seen, informed, and allowed to be children too.

What Siblings May Experience

  • Mixed feelings. Love and loyalty alongside frustration or resentment, sometimes followed by guilt.
  • Feeling overlooked. Parents' time and energy may be focused on appointments and challenges.
  • Pressure to be perfect. Trying not to add to parents' stress by hiding their own problems.
  • Embarrassment. Especially in public or with friends, which may then cause guilt.
  • Worry. About their sibling's future or their parents' stress.
  • Extra responsibility. Helping with care or acting as a protector.
  • Disrupted plans. Outings that change or end early because of a sibling's needs.
  • Positive growth. Empathy, acceptance of differences, and strong family bonds.

Why It Matters

Siblings' experiences shape their wellbeing and their lifelong relationship with their brother or sister. When siblings feel unheard, they may become withdrawn, anxious, or act out to get attention. Some children take on a caretaking role that continues into adulthood, as described on the page about parentification. Small, consistent efforts to support siblings can make a meaningful difference.

Ways to Support Siblings

1. Explain in Age-Appropriate Ways

Give clear, simple information about their sibling's differences and strengths, and update it as they grow. Children often imagine worse explanations when they are not told. Books can help, as described on the page about books for families navigating learning differences and neurodivergence.

2. Welcome All Feelings

Let siblings know that frustration, jealousy, and embarrassment are normal and okay to talk about, without judgment.

3. Protect One-on-One Time

Even short, regular time alone with a parent helps siblings feel valued.

4. Support Their Interests

Make space for their activities, friendships, and achievements, even when schedules are busy.

5. Be Fair, Not Identical

Explain that fairness means each child gets what they need, while also making sure siblings' needs are met.

6. Limit Caregiving Responsibilities

Siblings can help in age-appropriate ways, but they should not carry adult responsibilities.

7. Give Them Tools for Tricky Moments

Help them know what to say to friends or what to do during a sibling's meltdown.

8. Connect With Other Siblings

Sibling support groups or workshops, offered by some community organizations, help children meet others with similar experiences.

When Siblings May Need More Support

Consider extra support if a sibling seems persistently sad, anxious, angry, withdrawn, or is struggling at school, or if family conflict is high. Family therapy can help everyone communicate and feel heard, as described on the page about family therapy. Parents' own wellbeing matters too, as described on the page about what parental burnout is.

What It Is Not

Having a neurodivergent sibling is not a burden that harms children by default. Many siblings describe their relationship as one of the most meaningful in their lives. Their challenges are real, but with support, siblings often thrive.

Common Questions About Supporting Siblings

How does having a neurodivergent sibling affect children?

Having a neurodivergent sibling can bring mixed feelings, extra responsibility, and less attention, but also empathy, maturity, and strong bonds.

How do I explain autism or ADHD to my other children?

Explain autism or ADHD to siblings in simple, age-appropriate language, focusing on strengths and differences, and update the explanation as they grow.

Is it normal for siblings to feel jealous of a neurodivergent child?

Yes. Jealousy is a normal feeling for siblings of neurodivergent children, especially when parents' attention is stretched.

How can I make time for the siblings of my neurodivergent child?

Even short, regular one-on-one time with each child helps siblings of neurodivergent children feel valued.

Should siblings help care for a neurodivergent child?

Siblings can help in age-appropriate ways, but they should not carry adult caregiving responsibilities.

What should siblings do during a meltdown?

Give siblings a simple plan for meltdowns, such as going to a quiet space or getting an adult, so they feel safe and know what to do.

Are there support groups for siblings of autistic children?

Yes. Some community organizations offer sibling support groups or workshops for brothers and sisters of autistic and neurodivergent children.

Can siblings of neurodivergent children develop anxiety?

Some siblings of neurodivergent children develop anxiety or sadness, so it helps to watch for signs and offer support.

How do I balance fairness between a neurodivergent child and their siblings?

Explain that fairness means each child gets what they need, while making sure siblings' needs and interests are also met.

Can family therapy help siblings of neurodivergent children?

Yes. Family therapy can help siblings and parents communicate, share feelings, and feel more connected.
