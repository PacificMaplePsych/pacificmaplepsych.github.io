How to Support Someone Who Self-Harms

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If someone has a serious injury, has taken an overdose, or is in immediate danger, call 911. For support at any time, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline), including for guidance on supporting someone else. Young people can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

Supporting someone who self-harms usually works best when you stay calm, listen without judgment, focus on the person rather than the behaviour, encourage professional support, and look after your own wellbeing. Self-harm means intentionally hurting oneself, often as a way to cope with overwhelming emotions such as distress, numbness, anger, or shame. It is more common than many people realize, especially among teens and young adults. Your reaction can make a real difference to whether the person feels safe talking and seeking help. You do not need to have the perfect words, and you do not have to manage this alone.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

For many people, self-harm is a way to get through unbearable feelings. It may bring brief relief, a sense of control, or a way to feel something when numb. The relief is short-lived, and shame often follows, which can make the cycle continue. The broader picture is described on the page about understanding self-harm.

Self-harm is not usually attention-seeking, and many people hide it carefully. It is also not the same as a suicide attempt, though people who self-harm have a higher risk of suicidal thoughts, so it is important to take it seriously and ask gently. The person may feel embarrassed, afraid of being judged, or worried about losing control over what happens next.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Stay calm. A steady, caring response helps the person feel safe to talk.
  • Listen more than you speak. Ask open questions, such as "What has been going on for you?" and let them share at their own pace.
  • Acknowledge their pain. You might say, "It sounds like things have been really hard."
  • Ask about suicide directly. Asking, "Are you having thoughts of ending your life?" does not plant the idea, and it helps you know how urgent things are. The page about supporting someone with suicidal thoughts explains more.
  • Help with basic wound care. Make sure injuries are cleaned and checked by a health professional if they are deep or may be infected.
  • Explore other coping options together. Skills such as those on the page about DBT skills for emotional regulation can help manage intense feelings.
  • Stay connected. Check in regularly and keep doing ordinary things together.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Shock, anger, or punishment, which can increase shame and secrecy.
  • Demanding they stop immediately or making them promise. Self-harm usually reduces as other coping skills grow.
  • Constantly checking their body, which can damage trust.
  • Labelling it as attention-seeking.
  • Keeping serious risk a secret. If there is a risk of suicide or serious injury, others may need to be involved.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting someone who self-harms can bring fear, sadness, helplessness, and even anger. These feelings are normal. You are not responsible for stopping the behaviour on your own. Talk to someone you trust or a counsellor about your own feelings, set limits on what you can offer, and keep your own routines. Parents and caregivers may find it helpful to get guidance on how to respond at home. You can call or text 9-8-8 yourself for support.

Encouraging Professional Help

Therapy can help people understand what drives self-harm and build safer ways to cope. Approaches such as DBT and CBT have good evidence. Raise the idea gently, offer to help find a therapist or go to an appointment together, and respect their pace where it is safe to do so. A family doctor can check injuries and help with referrals. For young people under eighteen, parents or guardians should be involved in getting care. Creating a plan for difficult moments, as described on the page about safety plans, can also help. If there is any risk of suicide or a serious injury, seek urgent help or call 911.

Common Questions About Supporting Someone Who Self-Harms

What are good first words when a loved one admits to self-harm?

Stay calm, thank them for telling you, acknowledge their pain, and ask what has been going on, without judgment or shock about the self-harm.

Why do people hide self-harm so carefully?

Shame and fear of judgment lead many people to hide self-harm. It is most often a way to cope with overwhelming feelings, not a bid for attention.

Should I ask about suicide if I learn someone is self-harming?

Yes. Self-harm is usually a way to cope rather than a suicide attempt, but people who self-harm have a higher risk of suicidal thoughts, so asking directly helps.

Should I make someone promise to stop self-harming?

Demanding a promise often increases shame and secrecy. Self-harm usually reduces gradually as other coping skills grow.

How can I help with self-harm injuries?

Make sure self-harm injuries are cleaned and checked by a health professional if they are deep, bleeding heavily, or may be infected.

Should I keep someone's self-harm a secret?

If there is a risk of suicide or serious injury from self-harm, others may need to be involved to keep the person safe.

What steps can I take tonight if I discover my teenager has been self-harming?

Parents can stay calm, listen, get injuries checked, involve a doctor or therapist, and get guidance on supporting a teen who self-harms.

What therapy helps people who self-harm?

Approaches such as DBT and CBT have good evidence for helping people who self-harm build safer ways to cope with intense emotions.

How do I cope with my own feelings about someone else's self-harm?

Fear, sadness, and helplessness are common. Talk to someone you trust or a counsellor, set limits, and call 9-8-8 for support about self-harm.

When is self-harm an emergency?

Call 911 if a self-harm injury is serious, if someone has taken an overdose, or if they are in immediate danger.
