What Is Borderline Personality Disorder

If you are having thoughts of suicide or self-harm, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Borderline personality disorder, or BPD, is a mental health condition characterized by intense and rapidly changing emotions, a deep fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, an unstable sense of self, and impulsive behaviour. People with BPD often feel emotions more strongly and for longer than others, and may struggle to return to a calm baseline. BPD is one of the most stigmatized mental health conditions, yet it is also one of the most treatable personality conditions. Long-term studies show that most people with BPD improve significantly over time, and many no longer meet diagnostic criteria after treatment.

The Nine Criteria

A diagnosis requires at least five of the following, forming a long-standing pattern:

  • Intense efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment, described on the page about fear of abandonment.
  • Unstable, intense relationships that swing between idealizing and devaluing, sometimes called splitting.
  • An unstable sense of identity or self-image.
  • Impulsive behaviour in at least two areas, such as spending, substances, driving, or eating.
  • Recurrent suicidal behaviour, threats, or self-harm.
  • Rapid mood shifts lasting hours to a few days.
  • Chronic feelings of emptiness.
  • Intense anger or difficulty controlling anger.
  • Stress-related paranoia or dissociation.

Because different combinations are possible, BPD can look quite different from person to person. The emotional side of BPD is explored in more depth on the page about emotional intensity and BPD.

Why It Happens

BPD develops from a combination of biological sensitivity and environmental factors. Genetics influence emotional reactivity and impulsivity. Many people with BPD experienced childhood trauma, neglect, loss, or an environment where their emotions were invalidated, though not everyone did. The biosocial model developed by Marsha Linehan suggests that a sensitive emotional system combined with an invalidating environment makes it hard to learn how to understand and manage emotions. BPD symptoms often become noticeable in adolescence or early adulthood.

BPD commonly occurs alongside depression, anxiety, PTSD, eating disorders, and substance use. It can be confused with bipolar disorder, complex PTSD, or ADHD, so careful assessment matters. BPD is one of several conditions described on the page about personality disorders.

Living with BPD can be exhausting, both for the person and for those close to them. Many people with BPD describe feeling misunderstood, judged, or dismissed by others, including health professionals. Compassionate, informed care makes a real difference, and many people find that learning about BPD is itself a relief.

Myths About BPD

  • Myth: People with BPD are manipulative. Behaviours usually reflect overwhelming pain and a lack of other coping tools.
  • Myth: BPD cannot be treated. Effective therapies exist, and recovery is common.
  • Myth: BPD only affects women. People of any background can have BPD, though it may be recognized differently.
  • Myth: BPD is the same as bipolar disorder. They are different conditions with different patterns and treatments.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a careful exploration of emotional patterns, relationships, self-image, impulsivity, and history, often over several sessions. Psychotherapy is the main treatment. Dialectical behaviour therapy is the best-known approach, teaching mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal skills, usually through individual therapy and skills groups. Mentalization-based therapy, schema therapy, and transference-focused psychotherapy are also effective. Medication may help with specific symptoms, such as depression or anxiety, but does not treat BPD itself. Safety planning is important when self-harm or suicidal thoughts are present. Family education can help loved ones respond supportively.

Common Questions About Borderline Personality Disorder

What are the nine criteria for BPD?

They include fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, unstable identity, impulsivity, self-harm, mood shifts, emptiness, intense anger, and stress-related paranoia or dissociation.

How many criteria are needed for a BPD diagnosis?

At least five of the nine criteria, forming a long-standing pattern across situations.

Do people with BPD recover?

Yes. Long-term studies show most people improve significantly, and therapies such as DBT speed recovery.

Are people with BPD manipulative?

This is a common myth. Behaviours usually reflect intense pain and a lack of other coping tools.

What causes borderline personality disorder?

BPD develops from a mix of emotional sensitivity and environmental factors, such as trauma or invalidation.

When do BPD patterns usually become noticeable?

Symptoms often become noticeable in adolescence or early adulthood.

Is BPD the same as bipolar disorder?

No. BPD mood shifts are rapid and linked to events, while bipolar disorder involves sustained mood episodes.

What is DBT and how does it help BPD?

Dialectical behaviour therapy teaches mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal skills.

Is there a pill for borderline personality disorder?

No. Medication may help with specific symptoms but does not treat BPD itself. Psychotherapy is the main treatment.

How can families support someone with BPD?

Learn about BPD, validate feelings, set clear boundaries, and consider family education or skills programs.
