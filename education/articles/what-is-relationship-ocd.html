What Is Relationship OCD

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Relationship OCD, often called ROCD, is a form of obsessive-compulsive disorder in which intrusive doubts focus on a romantic relationship. People with ROCD may repeatedly question whether they truly love their partner, whether their partner is right for them, or whether they are attracted enough. They may also fixate on a partner's perceived flaws. These doubts cause intense anxiety and lead to compulsions, such as checking feelings, comparing, and seeking reassurance. ROCD can occur in loving, healthy relationships, and the doubts do not necessarily mean anything is wrong. Relationship OCD is treatable with the same approaches used for other forms of OCD.

What Relationship OCD Looks Like

  • Constant questioning, such as do I really love them or are they the one.
  • Monitoring your feelings for signs of love, attraction, or doubt.
  • Comparing your partner or relationship with others, including exes or friends' partners.
  • Focusing on a partner's perceived flaws in appearance, intelligence, or personality.
  • Seeking reassurance from friends, family, the internet, or your partner.
  • Replaying moments to analyze what you felt.
  • Avoiding intimacy, commitment, or situations that trigger doubt.
  • Intense guilt or anxiety about having these thoughts.
  • Considering ending the relationship to escape the doubt, then feeling distress at the idea.

Many of these rituals are mental compulsions, happening silently in the mind.

Why It Happens

ROCD follows the same cycle as other forms of OCD. An intrusive doubt about the relationship triggers anxiety. A compulsion, such as checking feelings or seeking reassurance, brings brief relief, which reinforces the doubt's importance. Over time, the cycle strengthens and doubts multiply. OCD tends to target what matters most, so people who deeply value their relationship may be especially vulnerable. Relationship OCD shares this pattern with harm OCD and other OCD themes.

Beliefs about relationships can fuel ROCD, such as the idea that true love means never having doubts, always feeling intense attraction, or knowing with complete certainty that a partner is right. Media portrayals of perfect romance add to these expectations. Attachment insecurity, perfectionism, and a personal or family history of OCD or anxiety increase vulnerability.

ROCD differs from general relationship anxiety, though they overlap. In ROCD, doubts are intrusive, repetitive, and accompanied by compulsions, and they often feel ego-dystonic, meaning they go against what the person wants. ROCD can also be confused with genuine concerns about an unhealthy relationship, which is why careful assessment matters.

What It Is Not

  • Not proof that you do not love your partner. Doubts in ROCD reflect OCD, not your true feelings.
  • Not a sign the relationship is wrong. ROCD often occurs in healthy relationships.
  • Not solved by finding certainty. Seeking certainty tends to strengthen doubts.
  • Not the same as legitimate concerns. Real problems such as disrespect or abuse deserve attention on their own terms.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician experienced in OCD will explore doubts, compulsions, and how they affect the relationship and daily life, and will distinguish ROCD from other relationship concerns. Exposure and response prevention is the most effective treatment. It involves gradually facing uncertainty about the relationship, such as writing about feared doubts, while resisting compulsions like checking feelings or seeking reassurance. Cognitive therapy can address unrealistic beliefs about love and certainty. Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people act on their values in the relationship while allowing doubts to be present. Partners can support treatment by learning about OCD and reducing reassurance. Medication may also help some people.

Common Questions About Relationship OCD

What is relationship OCD?

Relationship OCD involves persistent, distressing doubts about a relationship or partner, with compulsions such as checking and reassurance-seeking.

Does relationship OCD mean I do not love my partner?

No. Doubts in ROCD reflect OCD, not your true feelings, and often occur in loving relationships.

Why do I keep focusing on my partner's flaws?

In ROCD, attention can fixate on perceived flaws, driven by anxiety rather than the partner's actual qualities.

Are my relationship doubts OCD or ordinary insecurity?

A clinician can help clarify. ROCD involves intrusive, repetitive doubts with compulsions, while relationship anxiety is broader worry about rejection or security.

Why does reassurance not help with relationship doubts?

Reassurance brings brief relief but reinforces the doubt's importance, so it returns stronger.

Should I break up because of my doubts?

Decisions made to escape OCD doubts are often regretted. A clinician can help separate OCD from genuine concerns.

Why do I keep testing whether I still feel attracted?

This is a form of checking. In ROCD, people may monitor feelings, test attraction, or compare their partner with others to gain certainty.

What might exposure practice involve for relationship doubts?

It might include writing about feared doubts or spending time together without checking feelings, so anxiety decreases over time.

What is the most useful thing a partner can do during treatment?

Partners can learn about OCD, offer support without repeated reassurance, and encourage treatment.

Can relationship OCD be treated?

Yes. Exposure and response prevention, cognitive therapy, and acceptance-based approaches are effective.
