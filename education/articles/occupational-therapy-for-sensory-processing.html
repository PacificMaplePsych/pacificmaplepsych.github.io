Occupational Therapy for Sensory Processing

Occupational therapy for sensory processing helps people understand how they experience sensory input, such as sound, light, touch, movement, and smell, and develop strategies, routines, and environmental changes that make daily life more comfortable and manageable. Occupational therapists, often called OTs, are regulated health professionals who focus on helping people take part in everyday activities, such as self-care, work, school, and leisure. For people who are highly sensitive to sensory input, seek out intense input, or both, sensory-focused occupational therapy can reduce overload, support regulation, and improve participation in daily life. It is commonly used with autistic people and people with ADHD, but it can help anyone with sensory differences.

How Occupational Therapy for Sensory Processing Works

Sensory processing refers to how the brain receives, organizes, and responds to information from the senses. Some people are over-responsive, finding certain input overwhelming, as described on the page about sensory overload. Others are under-responsive, needing more input to notice it, or are sensory seeking, craving movement, pressure, or strong sensations. Many people have a mix. Differences can affect the eight sensory systems, including sight, sound, touch, taste, smell, movement and balance (vestibular), body position (proprioception), and internal body signals (interoception).

An occupational therapist assesses how these differences affect daily life and helps people:

  • Understand their sensory profile and triggers.
  • Develop a sensory diet, a planned set of activities that provide the input the nervous system needs throughout the day.
  • Adjust environments, such as lighting, noise, and seating.
  • Build tools, such as those described on the page about building a sensory toolkit.
  • Improve daily routines, self-care, and executive functioning.
  • Advocate for accommodations at work or school.

Neurodiversity-affirming OTs focus on comfort and participation, not on stopping harmless behaviours such as stimming.

What Sessions Are Like

Occupational therapy usually begins with an assessment. This may include interviews, sensory questionnaires, and observation of daily activities. For children, parents and teachers often provide input. The OT then works with the person to set goals, such as getting through a workday with less overload, sleeping better, or managing grooming tasks more comfortably.

Sessions vary depending on age and goals. With children, sessions often include play-based activities, such as swinging, climbing, or working with textures, in a sensory-rich space. With adults, sessions may focus more on discussion, trying tools, and planning changes at home and work. OTs may visit homes, schools, or workplaces to recommend adjustments. Between sessions, people practise strategies and try out sensory diet activities. The length of therapy varies from a few sessions to several months.

Occupational therapy is often provided privately in BC, and some extended health benefit plans cover part of the cost. Children may be able to access services through school districts or health authorities, and family doctors or paediatricians can help with referrals.

Who It Can Help

Occupational therapy for sensory processing can help autistic children and adults, people with ADHD, people with developmental differences, and those whose sensory sensitivities affect daily life. It can also help people with anxiety, PTSD, or chronic pain who experience heightened sensitivity. Research on sensory approaches is still developing, with more evidence for individualized strategies and environmental changes than for some specific techniques. A good OT will tailor recommendations and review what is working.

Occupational therapy works well alongside psychological therapy, which can address anxiety, burnout, and emotional wellbeing. When sensory differences are part of a broader picture, an autism or ADHD assessment may help clarify needs.

Common Questions About Occupational Therapy for Sensory Needs

What does an occupational therapist do for sensory processing?

An OT assesses how sensory differences affect daily life and helps build strategies, routines, and environmental changes.

What is a sensory diet?

It is a planned set of activities that provide the sensory input a person's nervous system needs throughout the day.

I am in my thirties. Is sensory-focused OT only meant for kids?

No. OTs work with adults as well as children to address sensory needs at home, work, and in daily routines.

What happens in a sensory assessment with an OT?

It may include interviews, questionnaires, and observation of daily activities to understand your sensory profile.

Why would an OT ask whether I notice hunger or needing the bathroom?

These are interoceptive signals, the sense of internal body cues such as hunger, thirst, and heart rate, which can be muted or intense with sensory differences.

Is occupational therapy covered in BC?

It is often private, and some extended health plans cover part of the cost. Children may access services through schools or health authorities.

Will an occupational therapist try to stop my stimming?

A neurodiversity-affirming OT focuses on comfort and participation, not stopping harmless behaviours such as stimming.

Can occupational therapy help with sensory overload at work?

Yes. OTs can recommend tools and workplace changes, and help you advocate for accommodations.

What are sensory seeking behaviours?

They are ways people seek out extra input, such as movement, pressure, or strong sensations, to feel regulated.

Should I see a therapist and an occupational therapist?

They work well together. OTs address sensory and daily living needs, while therapists support emotional wellbeing.
