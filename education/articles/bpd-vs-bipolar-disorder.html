BPD vs. Bipolar Disorder

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Signs of severe mania, such as not sleeping for days, losing touch with reality, or acting in ways that put you at risk, need urgent care, so call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) and bipolar disorder can both involve intense mood changes, impulsivity, and difficulties in relationships, which is why they are often confused. The key difference is timing and pattern. In bipolar disorder, mood changes come in distinct episodes of depression and mania or hypomania that typically last days to weeks or longer, and they often occur without a clear trigger. In BPD, emotions shift quickly, often within hours, and are usually set off by events in relationships, such as feeling rejected or criticized. BPD also involves an unstable sense of self and intense fear of abandonment. The two conditions can occur together, and getting the right diagnosis matters because treatments differ.

How the Mood Changes Differ

Duration

Bipolar episodes last at least several days for hypomania and at least a week for mania, and depressive episodes last two weeks or more. In BPD, intense emotions often rise and fall within hours or a day.

Triggers

Bipolar mood episodes can be triggered by stress or sleep loss but often seem to arrive on their own. BPD emotions are typically reactive to interpersonal events, such as a delayed text or a perceived slight.

The Type of Mood

Mania or hypomania involves elevated or irritable mood, decreased need for sleep, racing thoughts, and increased energy, as described on the page about understanding hypomania. BPD more often involves shifts between anger, anxiety, despair, and emptiness, rather than sustained high energy with little sleep.

Between Episodes

Many people with bipolar disorder feel relatively stable between episodes. In BPD, emotional sensitivity and relationship difficulties tend to be more constant.

Other Features of Each

BPD commonly includes fear of abandonment, unstable relationships, an unstable sense of identity, chronic emptiness, black-and-white thinking, self-harm, and impulsivity, as described on the page about what borderline personality disorder is.

Bipolar disorder is defined by mood episodes. It includes bipolar I, with at least one manic episode, and bipolar II, with hypomania and depression, as described on the pages about bipolar I disorder and bipolar II disorder. Bipolar disorder has a strong genetic component.

Why They Get Confused

Both conditions can involve impulsivity, irritability, suicidal thoughts, and relationship strain. Bipolar II in particular, with short hypomanic periods, can look similar to BPD. People may also have both conditions, along with trauma, ADHD, or substance use, which can complicate the picture. Misdiagnosis in either direction can lead to treatment that does not fit.

How Assessment Helps

A careful assessment looks at the timeline of mood changes, triggers, sleep, family history, relationships, and sense of self. Mood tracking over several weeks can clarify patterns, as described on the page about mood tracking for bipolar disorder. Input from people who know you well can also help.

How Treatment Differs

Bipolar disorder is usually treated with mood-stabilizing medication prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist, alongside therapy and routines that protect sleep, as described on the page about treatment options for bipolar disorder. BPD is primarily treated with psychotherapy, such as dialectical behaviour therapy, as described on the page about how borderline personality disorder is treated. When both are present, care usually combines both approaches.

Common Questions About BPD and Bipolar Disorder

Which matters more for telling BPD and bipolar disorder apart, timing or triggers?

Both matter. Bipolar episodes last days to weeks and often arise without a clear trigger, while BPD emotions shift within hours and are usually set off by relationship events.

Can you have both BPD and bipolar disorder?

Yes. BPD and bipolar disorder can occur together, and care then usually combines medication for bipolar disorder with therapy for BPD.

Why is BPD sometimes mistaken for bipolar II?

BPD is sometimes mistaken for bipolar II because both involve mood shifts, impulsivity, and irritability, but BPD mood changes are shorter and usually triggered by relationship events.

How long do mood swings last in BPD compared to bipolar disorder?

In BPD, mood swings often last hours, while bipolar mood episodes last at least several days, and depressive episodes two weeks or more.

Does BPD involve mania?

No. BPD does not involve mania. Mania, with high energy and little need for sleep, is a feature of bipolar disorder.

Why do mood stabilizers help bipolar disorder more than BPD?

Bipolar disorder involves mood episodes that respond well to mood-stabilizing medication, while BPD is mainly treated with psychotherapy such as DBT, with medication only for specific symptoms.

What triggers mood changes in BPD?

Mood changes in BPD are often triggered by relationship events, such as feeling rejected, criticized, or abandoned.

Can mood tracking help tell BPD and bipolar disorder apart?

Yes. Tracking mood, sleep, and triggers over several weeks can help a clinician tell BPD and bipolar disorder apart.

Is BPD genetic like bipolar disorder?

Both have genetic influences, but bipolar disorder is strongly heritable, while BPD reflects a mix of temperament and life experiences.

Should a psychiatrist or psychologist assess whether I have BPD or bipolar disorder?

Either can. A psychologist or psychiatrist can assess BPD and bipolar disorder by reviewing your mood history, triggers, sleep, relationships, and family history.
