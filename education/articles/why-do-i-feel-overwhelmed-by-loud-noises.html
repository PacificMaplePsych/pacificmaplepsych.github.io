Why Do I Feel Overwhelmed by Loud Noises

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

The blender, a crying baby on the bus, a restaurant where everyone is talking at once, a coworker's music leaking from their headphones. For some people these sounds fade into the background. For others they feel almost physically painful, crowding out every other thought until the only goal left is to make the noise stop or get away from it. Afterward, it can take hours to feel settled again.

Sensitivity to noise is more common than many people realize, and it is not a character flaw or a sign of being "difficult." It can come from the ears, the nervous system, stress, past experiences, or differences in how the brain processes sensory information.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Hearing-related conditions. Hyperacusis is a condition where everyday sounds are experienced as uncomfortably or painfully loud. Tinnitus, a ringing or buzzing in the ears, often comes with sound sensitivity. Misophonia involves strong emotional reactions, often anger or disgust, to specific sounds such as chewing or breathing. An audiologist can help assess these.

Migraine. Sensitivity to sound is a common feature of migraine, both during attacks and sometimes between them.

Stress and poor sleep. A tired or overloaded nervous system has less capacity to filter out background noise. Many people find they become more noise sensitive during stressful periods or when feeling on edge all the time.

Trauma. After frightening experiences, the brain can become highly alert to sudden sounds, leading to a strong startle response or to specific sounds acting as reminders of the past.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD find that competing sounds make it very hard to filter and focus, even though others with ADHD actually need background noise to concentrate.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Sensory processing differences are a core part of autism, and noise sensitivity is one of the most commonly reported. It is not about hearing better in a technical sense. It is about how the brain filters, prioritizes, and responds to incoming sound.

For many autistic people, the brain does less automatic filtering of background noise. Sounds that others tune out, such as fluorescent light buzzing, a ticking clock, or distant conversations, remain in the foreground. Several sounds at once can feel chaotic rather than blending into a general hum. Unpredictable noises, like a sudden alarm or a dropped dish, can feel jarring and physically uncomfortable.

A few patterns suggest autistic sensory processing may be involved:

  • Noise sensitivity that has been present since childhood, often alongside sensitivities to light, textures, or smells.
  • Overload that builds across the day, so the same sound is tolerable in the morning and unbearable in the evening.
  • Responses to overload such as shutting down, becoming unable to speak, or having a meltdown, rather than feeling fearful.
  • Busy social settings being exhausting partly because of the noise, a pattern that also shows up as distress in crowds.
  • Other autistic traits, such as differences in social communication, a strong preference for routine, or deep interests.

Noise sensitivity on its own does not mean someone is autistic. When it is part of a broader, lifelong pattern like this, though, it is worth exploring.

What Can Help

  • Use sound tools without guilt. Noise-cancelling headphones, musician's earplugs, or low-level earplugs can reduce load while still letting you hear conversation.
  • Plan for recovery. Build quiet time after noisy events, especially in the evening when capacity tends to be lower.
  • Shape your environment. Soft furnishings, rugs, and a quieter workspace can cut background noise significantly. Many workplaces will consider reasonable accommodations.
  • Avoid total silence long-term if you have hyperacusis, since an audiologist may recommend gradual sound exposure to help recalibrate sensitivity.

Common Questions About Noise Sensitivity

Is sensitivity to loud noises a sign of autism?

It can be one sign, particularly when it has been present since childhood and appears alongside other sensory sensitivities and differences in social communication or routine. On its own, noise sensitivity has many possible causes, so a broader assessment is needed before drawing conclusions.

What is the difference between hyperacusis and misophonia?

Hyperacusis is when everyday sounds feel physically uncomfortable or painfully loud. Misophonia is a strong emotional reaction, often anger or disgust, to specific trigger sounds such as chewing, sniffing, or tapping, regardless of how loud they are.

Why does noise bother me more at the end of the day?

Sensory processing draws on limited capacity. As the day goes on, fatigue and accumulated input reduce your ability to filter sound, so noises that were tolerable earlier can feel overwhelming by evening. This is especially common in autism.

Can stress make me more sensitive to sound?

Yes. When the nervous system is under strain, its ability to filter background input drops, and sounds can feel louder and more intrusive. Many people notice their noise sensitivity improves when stress and sleep improve.

Is it bad to wear noise-cancelling headphones all the time?

Using them to manage overload is reasonable and often very helpful. However, for people with hyperacusis, avoiding all everyday sound can sometimes increase sensitivity over time. An audiologist can advise on a balanced approach.

What does an autistic meltdown from noise overload look like?

A meltdown is an involuntary response to overwhelming input, not a tantrum or a choice. It may involve crying, shouting, pacing, or intense distress. Some people shut down instead, becoming quiet, withdrawn, or unable to speak until the overload passes.

Can ADHD cause noise sensitivity too?

Many people with ADHD find competing sounds very distracting and hard to filter. Others find some steady background noise helps them focus. The pattern varies widely, which is one reason a careful assessment looks at the full picture.

Why do sudden noises make me jump so much?

A strong startle response can reflect a nervous system that is on high alert, which is common after trauma and during high anxiety. Some autistic people also find unpredictable sounds especially jarring. The context and other symptoms help clarify the cause.

Can I ask my employer for accommodations for noise sensitivity?

Yes. Common accommodations include a quieter workspace, permission to use headphones, flexible work locations, or advance notice of loud events such as fire drills. Documentation from an assessment can support these requests.

Should I see an audiologist or a psychologist about noise sensitivity?

If you notice ringing in your ears, hearing changes, or physical pain from sound, start with an audiologist or doctor. If noise sensitivity is part of a lifelong pattern with other sensory or social differences, a psychologist can assess for autism or ADHD. Sometimes both are useful.
