What Is a Perinatal Mood and Anxiety Disorder (PMAD)

Perinatal mood and anxiety disorders, often called PMADs, are mental health conditions that occur during pregnancy or within the first year after birth. The term is an umbrella that includes several conditions, such as perinatal depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, post-traumatic stress disorder, and bipolar-related mood changes. PMADs are among the most common complications of pregnancy and the postpartum period. They can affect birthing parents, and non-birthing parents can experience similar difficulties. PMADs are not a sign of weakness or bad parenting, and they are treatable. Early support helps both parents and babies.

What PMADs Include

  • Perinatal depression. Persistent low mood, loss of interest, guilt, and exhaustion beyond typical adjustment, during pregnancy or after birth.
  • Perinatal anxiety. Persistent worry, tension, and physical anxiety, often about the baby, described on the page about postpartum anxiety.
  • Perinatal OCD. Intrusive, distressing thoughts, often about harm coming to the baby, with checking or avoidance, as described on the page about scary intrusive thoughts about your baby.
  • Perinatal PTSD. Trauma responses after a frightening pregnancy or birth, explored on the page about birth trauma.
  • Bipolar mood episodes. The postpartum period carries increased risk of mania or depression for people with bipolar disorder.
  • Postpartum psychosis. A rare but serious emergency involving confusion, hallucinations, or delusions, usually within weeks of birth.

Common signs across PMADs include difficulty sleeping even when possible, feeling overwhelmed, not feeling bonded with the baby, irritability, and feeling unlike yourself.

Why They Happen

PMADs arise from a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors. Hormonal shifts during pregnancy and after birth, combined with severe sleep deprivation, affect mood and stress systems. The enormous identity and relationship changes of becoming a parent add emotional strain. Risk factors include a personal or family history of depression, anxiety, or bipolar disorder, previous PMADs, pregnancy complications, a traumatic birth, pregnancy loss, infertility, limited support, relationship stress, financial pressure, and a baby with health concerns.

Many new parents feel pressure to be happy and grateful, which can make it hard to admit struggling. Some fear being judged as unfit parents. In reality, PMADs are common and treatable, and reaching out is an act of care for both parent and baby. Non-birthing parents can also experience depression and anxiety, as described on the page about paternal postnatal depression.

What PMADs Are Not

  • Not the baby blues. Baby blues are mild mood changes in the first two weeks that pass on their own. PMADs are more intense or longer-lasting.
  • Not a sign of bad parenting. PMADs can affect loving, capable parents.
  • Not only postpartum. Symptoms can begin during pregnancy.
  • Not something to push through alone. Treatment helps, and early support matters.

How They Are Assessed and Treated

Family doctors, midwives, obstetricians, and public health nurses can screen for PMADs during pregnancy and after birth. Treatment depends on the specific condition and severity. Psychotherapy, including cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy, is effective for perinatal depression and anxiety. Exposure and response prevention helps with perinatal OCD. Trauma-focused therapy helps after birth trauma. Some medications are considered compatible with pregnancy and breastfeeding and may be recommended. Practical support, such as help with night feeds and protected sleep, and peer support groups for new parents reduce vulnerability. Postpartum psychosis requires emergency care.

Common Questions About Perinatal Mood and Anxiety Disorders

What does PMAD stand for?

PMAD stands for perinatal mood and anxiety disorder, an umbrella term for mental health conditions during pregnancy or after birth.

Which conditions are included in PMADs?

They include perinatal depression, anxiety, OCD, PTSD, bipolar-related episodes, and postpartum psychosis.

How common are perinatal mood and anxiety disorders?

They are among the most common complications of pregnancy and the first year after birth.

How are PMADs different from the baby blues?

Baby blues are mild and pass within about two weeks. PMADs are more intense, longer-lasting, and affect daily life.

Can PMADs start during pregnancy?

Yes. Symptoms can begin during pregnancy, not only after birth.

Which postpartum symptoms need emergency care?

Confusion, hallucinations, or unusual beliefs after birth can signal postpartum psychosis, a rare emergency that requires immediate medical care.

Who is at higher risk of a PMAD?

Risk is higher with a history of depression or anxiety, previous PMADs, traumatic birth, pregnancy loss, and limited support.

Can non-birthing parents experience perinatal depression?

Yes. Partners can also develop depression and anxiety during pregnancy and after birth.

What treatments help perinatal mood and anxiety disorders?

Therapies such as CBT and interpersonal therapy, practical support, peer groups, and sometimes medication are effective.

Where can I get help for a PMAD in British Columbia?

Speak with your family doctor, midwife, or public health nurse, or call 8-1-1 for advice at any time.
