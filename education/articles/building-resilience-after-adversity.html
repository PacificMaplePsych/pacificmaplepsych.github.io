Building Resilience After Adversity

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Building resilience after adversity means developing the skills, relationships, and habits that help you adapt, recover, and find meaning after difficult experiences, such as loss, illness, job loss, relationship breakdown, or trauma. Resilience is not about being unaffected or pushing through without feeling pain. Resilient people still struggle, grieve, and feel afraid. What helps is having ways to cope, people to lean on, and a sense that you can take steps forward. Research suggests resilience is not a fixed trait. It can be strengthened at any age, and it often grows through facing challenges with support.

Why Resilience Can Be Built

Researchers who study resilience describe it as ordinary rather than rare. Many people recover well from hardship, especially when they have protective factors, such as supportive relationships, a sense of purpose, flexible thinking, emotional regulation skills, and access to practical resources. These factors can be developed. Adversity can also affect the body and mind for a while, bringing stress, sleep problems, or low mood. The broader effects of difficult experiences are explored on the page about trauma.

Building resilience does not erase what happened, but it can help people carry it differently. Some people also experience what researchers call post-traumatic growth, such as deeper relationships, a changed sense of priorities, or greater appreciation for life, alongside ongoing pain. Growth is not required, and it should never be forced, but it is possible.

Strategies to Try

1. Stay Connected

Supportive relationships are one of the strongest protective factors. Reach out to people you trust, even briefly. Ideas for rebuilding connection are on the page about building social connection.

2. Allow Your Feelings

Naming emotions, such as sadness, anger, or fear, and letting them be present helps them move rather than build up.

3. Look After the Basics

Sleep, regular meals, movement, and time outdoors support the body's ability to cope with stress.

4. Focus on What You Can Control

Adversity often brings a sense of helplessness. Identifying small, concrete actions within your control restores a sense of agency.

5. Practise Flexible Thinking

Notice all-or-nothing thoughts, such as "nothing will ever get better," and consider more balanced perspectives, such as "this is very hard right now, and I have gotten through hard things before."

6. Treat Yourself With Compassion

Self-compassion, described on the page about compassion-focused therapy, is linked with greater resilience than harsh self-criticism.

7. Find Meaning

Reflect on what matters to you now, what you have learned, or how you might help others. Meaning can come slowly and does not require seeing the adversity as positive.

8. Build Coping Skills

Slow breathing, grounding, journalling, and problem-solving are practical tools. The page about stress management techniques offers more.

9. Set Small Goals

Achievable goals, such as a short walk or one task a day, build confidence and momentum.

Common Obstacles

The pressure to be resilient can itself feel heavy, as if struggling means failing. Resilience includes struggling and asking for help. Ongoing hardships, such as financial strain, discrimination, chronic illness, or caregiving, can make recovery harder, and individual strategies cannot fix systemic problems. Practical support and advocacy matter too. Some people isolate themselves after adversity, which can deepen distress. Reaching out, even in small ways, helps. Major life changes after adversity, such as a move or new role, can add stress, as described on the page about coping with major life transitions.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If distress continues for a long time after adversity, if you experience flashbacks, nightmares, persistent low mood, anxiety, or hopelessness, or if daily life feels unmanageable, therapy can help. Trauma-informed therapy, grief counselling, and other approaches can support recovery. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Building Resilience

Is resilience about bouncing back as if nothing happened?

No. Resilience means adapting and recovering after adversity with the help of skills, relationships, and supports, not being unaffected by pain.

Can resilience be learned, or are some people just born with it?

Research suggests resilience can be strengthened at any age by building protective factors such as relationships, coping skills, and purpose.

What are the strongest protective factors for resilience?

Supportive relationships, a sense of purpose, flexible thinking, emotional regulation skills, and practical resources are key resilience factors.

Does being resilient mean I should not feel upset after a setback?

No. Resilient people still struggle and grieve after a setback. Resilience includes feeling pain and asking for help.

Can people grow in positive ways after adversity, even while still hurting?

Post-traumatic growth describes positive changes some people experience after adversity, such as deeper relationships, alongside ongoing pain.

How can self-compassion build resilience after adversity?

Self-compassion is linked with greater resilience than self-criticism, since it helps people recover from setbacks without harsh judgment.

How can I build resilience when hardships keep piling up?

Focus on small actions within your control, lean on support, and seek practical help, since ongoing adversity needs more than individual coping.

Why does isolating myself after hard times make things worse?

Isolation after adversity cuts off one of the strongest supports for resilience, so reaching out even briefly helps.

How can flexible thinking help me cope with adversity?

Flexible thinking helps replace all-or-nothing thoughts after adversity with more balanced views, which supports coping and hope.

When is it time to get help if I am not recovering after adversity?

If distress after adversity lasts a long time or brings flashbacks, persistent low mood, or hopelessness, therapy can help.
