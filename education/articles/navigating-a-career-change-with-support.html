Navigating a Career Change With Support

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Navigating a career change with support means combining practical planning with attention to the emotional side of change, such as fear, uncertainty, and questions about identity. Career changes happen for many reasons, including burnout, layoffs, shifting values, health, family needs, or a desire for more meaning. Even welcome changes can bring anxiety and self-doubt. Support from friends, mentors, career professionals, and sometimes a therapist can help people make clearer decisions, cope with setbacks, and adjust to a new chapter. There is no single right path, and many people change careers more than once.

Why Support Helps

Work often shapes identity, routine, social connection, and financial security. Changing careers can shake all of these at once. People may feel exhilarated about possibilities and terrified of making the wrong choice. Some grieve the loss of a role that once defined them, especially after an unexpected job loss. This experience is explored on the page about identity loss after a life transition.

Uncertainty can trigger worry, overthinking, and avoidance. Support helps by offering perspective, practical information, and encouragement. Talking decisions through can reduce the sense of being stuck in your own head. Support also helps people notice when fear, perfectionism, or old beliefs are driving decisions rather than their values.

Strategies to Try

1. Clarify Your Values and Needs

Reflect on what matters most in work, such as meaning, flexibility, stability, creativity, or helping others. Consider what has drained you in past roles and what energized you.

2. Explore Before Committing

Informational interviews, volunteering, short courses, or side projects can test a new direction before a full leap. Small experiments reduce risk and build confidence.

3. Plan the Practical Side

Consider finances, training, timelines, and job markets. A realistic plan, including a financial buffer if possible, eases stress.

4. Build a Support Team

Mentors, career counsellors, peers who have made similar changes, and supportive friends can offer guidance and encouragement. In BC, WorkBC provides free employment services, including career planning, for eligible residents.

5. Expect Mixed Emotions

Excitement, grief, fear, and relief can all show up. Naming these feelings helps you respond with care rather than self-criticism.

6. Break the Change Into Steps

Large goals can feel overwhelming. Breaking them into weekly actions, such as updating a resume or contacting one person, makes progress more manageable.

7. Look After Your Wellbeing

Transitions can disrupt routines. Protecting sleep, movement, social connection, and time for rest supports clear thinking.

8. Be Patient With the Adjustment

Starting over can mean feeling like a beginner again. Many people experience self-doubt in the first months of a new role. Confidence usually grows with time.

Common Obstacles

Fear of failure or of making the wrong choice can lead to endless research without action. Setting a deadline for a decision, or choosing a small next step, can help move forward. Some people struggle with what others will think, especially if family expectations or cultural values emphasize stability or a particular path. Talking openly about your reasons can help, and so can accepting that not everyone will understand.

Financial pressure can limit options, making it important to plan realistically and consider gradual transitions. Perfectionism can make it hard to accept that a new path may involve uncertainty. Worry about the future can also build ahead of time, as described on the page about anticipatory anxiety. Midlife career changes can bring extra questions about time and purpose, as explored on the page about midlife transitions.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If anxiety about the change feels overwhelming, if you feel stuck for a long time, if a job loss has brought low mood or hopelessness, or if burnout is making decisions hard, therapy can help. A therapist can help you explore values, manage anxiety and self-doubt, grieve losses, and plan steps forward. Career counselling can complement therapy with practical guidance. If you have thoughts of suicide after a job loss or during a difficult transition, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Career Change and Mental Health

Why do I feel so anxious about changing careers?

Career changes affect identity, routine, and security, so uncertainty can trigger worry and self-doubt.

How do I know if a career change is right for me?

Reflect on your values, test new directions with small experiments, and talk decisions through with trusted people.

Is it normal to grieve my old job even though I chose to leave?

Yes. Leaving a role can bring loss of identity, routine, and connections, even when the change is positive.

What free career support is available in BC?

WorkBC offers free employment services, including career planning, for eligible residents.

How can I test a new career before quitting my current job?

Informational interviews, volunteering, courses, and side projects can help you explore without a full leap.

Why do I keep researching options without making a decision?

Fear of the wrong choice can lead to overthinking. Setting a deadline or taking a small step can help.

How do I handle family members who disapprove of my career change?

Share your reasons openly, and accept that not everyone will understand. Support from others can help.

Is it too late to change careers in midlife?

No. Many people change careers in midlife, often bringing valuable experience to new roles.

Why do I feel like an impostor in my new career?

Starting over can bring self-doubt. Confidence usually grows as you gain experience in the new role.

How is therapy different from career counselling during a job change?

Career counselling offers practical guidance, while therapy helps with emotions, values, anxiety, and identity. They often work well together.
