Understanding Ambiguous Loss

Ambiguous loss is a loss that lacks clarity or closure. The term was developed by family therapist Pauline Boss to describe situations where a person is gone in one way but not another. Because there is no clear ending, the usual rituals and understanding that support grief may be missing. People may feel stuck, confused, or unsure whether they are allowed to grieve at all. Ambiguous loss is not a sign that someone is grieving wrongly. It is a particularly difficult kind of loss, and naming it can bring relief and direction.

What Ambiguous Loss Looks Like

Pauline Boss described two main types:

  • Physically present but psychologically absent. A loved one is still here but changed, for example through dementia, brain injury, addiction, serious mental illness, or chronic illness. This is often described as grieving someone who is still alive.
  • Physically absent but psychologically present. A loved one is missing or gone without clear answers, for example after a disappearance, estrangement, adoption, immigration, or a family member lost to incarceration or distance.

Common experiences include:

  • Confusion about whether to hope or to let go.
  • Grief that comes and goes without resolution.
  • Guilt about grieving someone who is still alive.
  • Feeling misunderstood by others who do not see it as a loss.
  • Exhaustion, anxiety, or low mood.
  • Uncertainty about roles, such as spouse or caregiver.

Why It Is So Hard

Most people cope with loss by understanding what happened, marking it with rituals, and gradually adjusting. Ambiguous loss interrupts this process. Without a clear ending, the mind keeps searching for resolution. Hope and grief can exist at the same time, pulling in opposite directions. Communities often do not recognize ambiguous losses, so support and rituals may be absent. This is sometimes called disenfranchised grief. The situation may also continue for years, as with dementia or a family estrangement, creating ongoing strain.

Caregivers of loved ones with progressive illness often experience both ambiguous loss and anticipatory grief, grieving changes as they happen and anticipating further loss. The exhaustion of caregiving can compound this, as described on the page about caregiver burnout.

Ambiguous loss can also affect whole families. Family members may see the situation differently, with one holding on to hope while another has already begun to grieve. These differences can lead to conflict or misunderstanding. Talking openly about how each person understands the loss can reduce blame and help families support one another.

What It Is Not

  • Not a failure to cope. Feeling stuck is a normal response to a loss without closure.
  • Not something that requires choosing between hope and grief. Holding both is often part of coping.
  • Not less valid than grief after a death. Ambiguous losses can be just as painful.
  • Not always solved by finding answers. Resilience often means learning to live with uncertainty.

How It Is Supported

Therapy for ambiguous loss focuses on naming the loss, making room for mixed feelings, and building resilience within uncertainty rather than seeking closure that may not come. Pauline Boss describes the value of both-and thinking, such as recognizing that a loved one is both here and gone. Therapy can help people find meaning, adjust roles and identity, and create personal rituals that honour what has been lost. Support groups for caregivers, families of missing persons, or people affected by estrangement can reduce isolation. Taking care of your own health and connections is also important.

Common Questions About Ambiguous Loss

Can a loss count as grief when nobody has died?

Yes. Ambiguous loss describes a loss without clarity or closure, such as when a loved one is present but changed, or absent without clear answers.

Who developed the idea of ambiguous loss?

Family therapist Pauline Boss developed the concept through her work with families of missing persons and people with dementia.

Is it normal to grieve someone with dementia who is still alive?

Yes. Grieving the changes in a loved one with dementia is a common form of ambiguous loss.

Can estrangement from family be an ambiguous loss?

Yes. When a family member is alive but absent from your life, the loss can feel unresolved and difficult to grieve.

Why do I feel stuck when I am grieving a loss without closure?

Without a clear ending, the mind keeps searching for resolution, and hope and grief can pull in opposite directions.

What is both-and thinking in ambiguous loss?

It means holding two truths at once, such as a person being both here and gone, rather than forcing a single answer.

How is ambiguous loss different from anticipatory grief?

Ambiguous loss centres on unclear presence or absence, while anticipatory grief looks ahead to an expected loss. They often occur together.

Can immigration cause ambiguous loss?

Yes. Leaving behind family, community, and homeland can bring unresolved grief for people and places that still exist.

How can families cope with ambiguous loss together?

Talking openly about different views of the loss, sharing roles, and creating meaningful rituals can reduce conflict and isolation.

What does counselling focus on when there may never be closure?

Counselling helps name the loss, hold mixed feelings, and build resilience while living with uncertainty.
