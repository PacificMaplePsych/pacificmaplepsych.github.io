Why Do I Struggle to Make Eye Contact

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You know you are "supposed" to look people in the eye. Maybe a parent reminded you as a child, or a manager once said you seemed disengaged. But when you try, it feels too intense, like staring into a bright light, or you lose track of what the person is saying because all your effort goes into holding their gaze. So you look at their mouth, their shoulder, or somewhere just past them, and hope no one notices.

Struggling with eye contact is common and has many possible explanations. Some are about culture and personality, some about anxiety or past experiences, and some about how your brain processes social and sensory information.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Culture and upbringing. Norms around eye contact vary widely. In many cultures and families, sustained eye contact with elders or authority figures is considered disrespectful. What reads as "avoidant" in one setting is polite in another.

Shyness and introversion. Some people simply find direct gaze a bit exposing, particularly with strangers, and become more comfortable as they get to know someone.

Social anxiety. When you fear being judged, meeting someone's eyes can feel like inviting scrutiny. Looking away reduces that sense of exposure. Social anxiety often comes with other physical signs, such as sweaty palms before conversations, blushing, or a shaky voice.

Shame and low self-worth. People carrying deep shame or a sense of being unworthy sometimes avoid eye contact because being seen feels uncomfortable at a deeper level.

Low mood. Depression can reduce eye contact along with energy, facial expressiveness, and interest in social connection.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD find their gaze wanders because their attention does, or they look away to think while talking.

When It Looks More Like Autism

For many autistic people, eye contact is not avoided out of fear or shyness but because it is genuinely uncomfortable or cognitively demanding. People describe it as intense, intrusive, or even physically unpleasant. Others explain that looking at someone's eyes makes it harder to listen, because processing the face and the words at the same time takes more effort than doing either alone.

Autistic people often learn to manage expectations by looking near the eyes, at the forehead or mouth, or by making brief, scheduled glances. This is one form of masking, the effort of hiding natural traits to fit in, and it can be quietly exhausting over a full day of interactions.

A few patterns suggest autism may be part of the picture:

  • Eye contact has felt uncomfortable since childhood, including with people you know and trust, not just strangers.
  • You listen and understand better when you are not looking at someone's face.
  • There are other differences in social communication, such as finding small talk confusing or taking things literally.
  • You also experience sensory sensitivities, like being overwhelmed by loud noises.

It is important to say that reduced eye contact does not mean an autistic person is uninterested, dishonest, or not paying attention. Often it is exactly the opposite: looking away is how they pay attention.

What Can Help

  • Notice when it is hardest. Is it with strangers, authority figures, everyone, or when you are tired? The pattern helps point toward the likely cause.
  • Use comfortable alternatives. Looking at the bridge of the nose or glancing periodically works well for many people and reads as engaged to others.
  • Tell people what works for you if you are comfortable doing so, for example "I listen better when I'm not looking at you."
  • Work on the anxiety if fear is the driver. Gradually practising eye contact in low-pressure settings can build comfort when social anxiety is involved.

Common Questions About Eye Contact

Does avoiding eye contact always mean someone is autistic?

No. Culture, shyness, social anxiety, shame, depression, and ADHD can all affect eye contact. It is more suggestive of autism when it has been uncomfortable since childhood, happens with familiar people too, and appears alongside other social and sensory differences.

Why does eye contact feel physically uncomfortable for some autistic people?

Many autistic people experience direct gaze as intense sensory and social input, sometimes described as overwhelming or intrusive. Processing a face and listening at the same time can also demand extra effort, so looking away frees up capacity to understand the conversation.

Can social anxiety make eye contact hard even with friends?

Social anxiety usually makes eye contact hardest with strangers, authority figures, or people whose opinion matters a lot. With close friends it often eases. If it is equally hard with everyone, including trusted people, other explanations such as autism may be worth considering.

Is it rude not to make eye contact during conversations?

Norms vary a lot between cultures and individuals. In many places, lower eye contact is polite or neutral. Where more eye contact is expected, briefly explaining your preference or using comfortable alternatives usually prevents misunderstandings.

What is masking eye contact in autism?

Masking eye contact means forcing or simulating it to meet social expectations, for example by looking at the forehead or counting glances. It can help someone fit in but often takes significant effort and can contribute to exhaustion after social interactions.

Can trauma or shame make it hard to look people in the eye?

Yes. People who carry deep shame or who were harshly criticized may find being looked at uncomfortable, because it feels exposing. Working on those underlying feelings in therapy often makes eye contact easier over time.

Can depression reduce how much eye contact I make?

It can. Depression often lowers energy, facial expressiveness, and interest in social connection, and eye contact may drop as part of that. If reduced eye contact appeared along with low mood, depression may be the more relevant explanation.

Is there a way to seem engaged without holding eye contact?

Yes. Looking near the eyes, nodding, facing the person, and responding to what they say all signal engagement. Many people will not notice the difference, and those who know you can be told what works best for you.

Should I practise making more eye contact?

It depends on the reason. If anxiety is the barrier, gradual practice can build comfort. If eye contact is uncomfortable because of autistic sensory or processing differences, forcing it may cost a lot of energy, and alternatives that feel comfortable are often a better choice.

Would an autism assessment look at eye contact?

Eye contact is one of many things an adult autism assessment considers, alongside social communication, sensory experiences, routines, interests, and developmental history. No single behaviour determines the outcome. The overall lifelong pattern is what matters.
