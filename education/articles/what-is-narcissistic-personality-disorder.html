What Is Narcissistic Personality Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) is a personality disorder involving a long-standing, pervasive pattern of grandiosity, a strong need for admiration, and limited empathy for others. Beneath the outward confidence, many people with NPD have fragile self-esteem that is highly sensitive to criticism. The term "narcissist" is widely used in everyday conversation, often loosely, but NPD is a specific clinical diagnosis that only a qualified professional can make. Understanding what it is, and what it is not, helps people make sense of difficult relationships without labelling everyone who behaves selfishly.

What Narcissistic Personality Disorder Looks Like

  • An inflated sense of importance or entitlement to special treatment.
  • Preoccupation with success, power, status, or ideal love.
  • A need for constant admiration and attention.
  • Difficulty recognizing or caring about others' feelings and needs.
  • Exploiting others to achieve goals.
  • Envy of others, or believing others envy them.
  • Arrogant or dismissive behaviour, especially when not receiving admiration.
  • Strong reactions, such as anger or withdrawal, to criticism or perceived slights.

Clinicians sometimes describe different presentations. Grandiose narcissism is more openly boastful and dominant. Vulnerable or covert narcissism is quieter, marked by hypersensitivity, resentment, and feeling underappreciated. At work, NPD may show up as taking credit or reacting badly to feedback. In relationships, partners may feel used, dismissed, or manipulated.

Why It Happens

NPD likely develops from a combination of genetic temperament and early experiences. Theories point to childhood environments involving excessive praise without realistic feedback, conditional approval, neglect, or harsh criticism, each of which can make a stable sense of self difficult to build. Grandiosity may protect against underlying shame or feelings of inadequacy. Personality patterns become established by early adulthood and tend to be enduring, though they can change with sustained effort and treatment.

People with NPD rarely seek help for narcissism itself. They more often come to therapy because of depression, anxiety, relationship breakdowns, work problems, or a crisis that threatens their self-image. Their distress in those moments is real.

Relationships with someone who has strong narcissistic traits often follow a recognizable pattern. Early on, the person may be charming and attentive. Over time, partners, friends, or colleagues may notice that their needs are rarely considered, that criticism is met with anger or coldness, and that conversations tend to return to the other person. Many people describe feeling that they are always adjusting to keep the peace, which can erode their confidence and sense of self.

What It Is Not

  • Not everyone who is selfish or confident. Healthy self-esteem and occasional self-centredness are normal.
  • Not something to diagnose in others from a distance. Labelling a partner or boss can oversimplify complex situations. What matters most is whether a relationship is safe and respectful.
  • Not the same as emotional abuse, though they can overlap. Abusive behaviour, including gaslighting, is harmful whatever the diagnosis.
  • Not bipolar mania. Grandiosity during mania is episodic, while NPD is a stable pattern.
  • Not borderline personality disorder, though both involve fragile self-esteem and relationship difficulties.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

NPD is diagnosed by a psychologist or psychiatrist through a detailed assessment of long-standing patterns across relationships and settings. Treatment is challenging but possible. Approaches such as psychodynamic therapy, schema therapy, and other longer-term therapies can help people develop more stable self-esteem, tolerate criticism, and improve empathy and relationships. Co-occurring depression, anxiety, or substance use are often addressed too. For people affected by someone with narcissistic traits, therapy can help with boundaries, self-trust, and recovery, particularly after an unhealthy relationship.

Common Questions About Narcissistic Personality Disorder

What are the main traits of narcissistic personality disorder?

Main traits include grandiosity, a strong need for admiration, entitlement, limited empathy, and sensitivity to criticism, forming a long-standing pattern across settings.

What is covert or vulnerable narcissism?

Vulnerable narcissism is a quieter presentation marked by hypersensitivity, resentment, and feeling underappreciated, rather than open boasting.

Is everyone who seems self-centred a narcissist?

No. Self-centred behaviour is common. Narcissistic personality disorder is a specific diagnosis involving an enduring, pervasive pattern that causes significant problems.

Can people with narcissistic personality disorder change?

Change is possible, especially with long-term therapy and motivation, though it can be challenging because traits are deeply ingrained.

Where do narcissistic patterns tend to come from?

It likely develops from genetic temperament combined with early experiences such as conditional approval, excessive praise, neglect, or harsh criticism.

Why do people with narcissistic traits react so strongly to criticism?

Beneath outward confidence, self-esteem is often fragile. Criticism can threaten that self-image, triggering anger, withdrawal, or defensiveness.

Should I try to diagnose my partner as a narcissist?

It is usually more helpful to focus on how the relationship affects you and whether it is safe and respectful than on diagnosing someone else.

Why do people with strong narcissistic traits usually come to therapy?

They rarely seek help for narcissism itself but may come for depression, anxiety, or relationship and work problems.

Which long-term therapies are used for NPD?

Longer-term approaches such as psychodynamic therapy and schema therapy can help build stable self-esteem, improve empathy, and change relationship patterns.

How can I recover after a relationship with someone with narcissistic traits?

Therapy can help rebuild self-trust, process hurt, set boundaries, and understand patterns that made the relationship hard to leave.
