Working Through Jealousy in a Relationship

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Working through jealousy in a relationship usually involves understanding what the feeling is signalling, calming the urge to act on it right away, and talking openly with your partner about needs and fears. Jealousy is a normal emotion that arises when people feel a valued relationship might be threatened. Occasional, mild jealousy is common. When it becomes intense, constant, or leads to checking, accusing, or controlling behaviour, it can damage trust and cause distress for both partners. Jealousy often says more about fears of loss or not being enough than about what a partner is actually doing. With awareness and support, it can become more manageable.

Why Working Through Jealousy Helps

Jealousy combines several feelings, including fear, insecurity, anger, and sadness. It can be triggered by real events, such as secrecy or a past betrayal, or by imagined threats, such as a partner spending time with a colleague. Past experiences shape how strongly people react. Someone who was betrayed before, or who grew up with inconsistent care, may be more sensitive to signs of possible loss. Anxious attachment and low self-esteem can intensify jealousy.

Jealous thoughts can spiral, with people imagining worst-case scenarios and seeking reassurance or evidence. Checking a partner's phone or asking repeated questions may bring brief relief but often increases anxiety and strains the relationship. Working through jealousy helps break this cycle, reduce distress, and build trust based on communication rather than surveillance.

Strategies to Try

1. Name the Feeling

Notice when jealousy shows up and what it feels like in your body. Naming it, for example "I am feeling jealous and scared," can create a pause before reacting.

2. Look for the Fear Underneath

Ask what the jealousy is protecting. Is it fear of being left, replaced, or not being enough? Understanding the deeper need helps you respond to it directly.

3. Check the Facts

Distinguish between what you know and what you are imagining. Writing down the thought and the evidence for and against it can reduce catastrophic thinking.

4. Delay Acting on Urges

When you feel the urge to check, accuse, or question, wait for twenty or thirty minutes and use a calming strategy. Urges often ease with time.

5. Share Feelings Without Blame

Talk to your partner calmly, using I statements, such as "I felt anxious when I did not hear from you, and I would love a quick message when plans change." This invites reassurance and closeness rather than defensiveness.

6. Agree on Relationship Expectations

Couples differ in what feels comfortable around friendships, social media, and time apart. Talking openly about boundaries can reduce misunderstandings.

7. Build Your Own Security

Investing in friendships, interests, and self-worth outside the relationship reduces the sense that everything depends on it.

8. Rebuild Trust After Betrayal

When jealousy follows a real betrayal, rebuilding trust takes time and consistent actions from both partners. Couples counselling often helps.

Common Obstacles

Reassurance can feel essential in the moment, but constant reassurance may keep jealousy going, much like in relationship anxiety. Some people find their jealous thoughts are intrusive and persistent, and feel compelled to check or analyze. This can overlap with relationship OCD, which benefits from specific treatment. Partners may also feel frustrated or controlled by repeated questions, which can lead to withdrawal and increase insecurity further. Naming this cycle together helps.

It is also important to recognize when jealousy becomes controlling. Monitoring a partner's phone, restricting who they see, or using threats are harmful behaviours. If you are experiencing controlling jealousy from a partner, the signs of domestic abuse page and our abuse resources can help.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If jealousy feels overwhelming, causes frequent conflict, leads to checking or controlling behaviour, or is linked to past trauma, therapy can help. Individual therapy can address insecurity, attachment patterns, and anxious thinking. Couples counselling can help partners communicate, rebuild trust, and understand each other's needs. If you ever feel unsafe in your relationship, call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Jealousy in Relationships

Is some jealousy normal in a relationship?

Yes. Occasional, mild jealousy is common. It becomes a problem when it is intense, constant, or leads to controlling behaviour.

Why do I feel jealous even though my partner has done nothing wrong?

Jealousy often reflects fears of loss or not being enough, shaped by past experiences, rather than a partner's actions.

Does checking my partner's phone make jealousy better?

It may bring brief relief but usually increases anxiety and damages trust over time.

What words can I use to admit my jealousy to my partner calmly?

Use calm I statements, describe your feeling and need, and avoid accusations.

How can I stop acting on jealous urges?

Pause, name the feeling, wait twenty or thirty minutes, and use a calming strategy before deciding what to do.

Can jealousy come from past betrayal?

Yes. Being betrayed before can make people more sensitive to signs of possible loss.

When does jealousy become controlling?

Monitoring a partner, restricting who they see, or using threats are controlling behaviours that cause harm.

Does anxious attachment make jealousy in relationships stronger?

Often, yes. Anxious attachment can intensify jealousy in relationships because of a heightened fear of abandonment and need for reassurance.

My jealousy started after my partner cheated. Can that trust come back?

Rebuilding trust takes time, honesty, and consistent actions from both partners, often with couples counselling.

Can couples counselling help with jealousy?

Yes. It can help partners communicate, understand each other's needs, and rebuild trust.
