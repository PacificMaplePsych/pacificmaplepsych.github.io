What Is Claustrophobia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Claustrophobia is an intense, persistent fear of enclosed or confined spaces, such as elevators, small rooms, tunnels, airplanes, crowded trains, or MRI machines. People with claustrophobia may feel panicky, short of breath, or terrified of being trapped or running out of air, and they often go to great lengths to avoid such places. It is a type of specific phobia and is one of the more common phobias. Claustrophobia can develop after a frightening experience, such as being stuck in an elevator, or without a clear cause. It responds very well to exposure-based therapy, and many people improve within a relatively short course of treatment.

Signs of Claustrophobia

  • Panic in enclosed spaces, with a racing heart, sweating, trembling, or dizziness
  • Fear of suffocating or not being able to breathe
  • Fear of being trapped or unable to escape
  • Checking for exits whenever entering a room
  • Avoiding elevators, tunnels, flights, or crowded places
  • Difficulty with medical procedures, such as MRI scans
  • Anticipatory anxiety before situations that might feel confining

Why It Happens

  • Past experiences. Being trapped, locked in, or stuck in a small space can create a lasting link between enclosed spaces and danger.
  • Learning. Seeing a parent react fearfully to enclosed spaces can shape fears.
  • Sensitivity to body sensations. People who notice breathing changes may interpret them as signs of suffocation.
  • Panic. A panic attack in an enclosed space can lead to fear of similar places.

Avoidance then keeps the fear going, as described on the page about what a specific phobia is.

The Role of Safety Behaviours

People with claustrophobia often develop habits to feel safer, such as always taking the stairs, sitting near exits, keeping doors open, or avoiding the middle of rows in theatres or planes. These behaviours bring short-term relief, but they also prevent the brain from learning that enclosed spaces are usually safe and that anxiety passes on its own. Over time, the list of avoided places can grow, limiting travel, work, and social life. Therapy gradually reduces these safety behaviours as part of exposure, helping people build genuine confidence in situations they once avoided. Recognizing your own safety behaviours is often a useful first step, even before formal treatment begins.

What It Is Not

Claustrophobia is different from agoraphobia, which involves fear of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable during panic, often across many settings, as described on the page about agoraphobia. The two can overlap. Some discomfort in a stuck elevator or crowded space is normal. Claustrophobia is persistent, intense, and leads to avoidance that affects daily life.

How It Is Treated

Exposure Therapy

Gradual, planned exposure to enclosed spaces is the most effective treatment. A person might start by imagining a small space, then stand in a closet with the door open, then closed, then ride an elevator one floor, building up step by step. The page about how exposure therapy treats phobias explains the approach.

Cognitive Strategies

Therapy helps identify and test fearful thoughts, such as "I will run out of air," and builds confidence in coping.

Interoceptive Exposure

Practising sensations such as breathlessness in a safe way can reduce fear of those sensations.

Calming Skills

Slow breathing can help in the moment, but therapy aims to reduce fear rather than rely on these strategies alone.

Claustrophobia and MRI Scans

If you need an MRI, tell the imaging team ahead of time. Options may include an open or wide-bore scanner, music, a mirror, staying in contact with staff, or medication prescribed by your physician. Short-term therapy before a scan can also help, as described on the page about overcoming a fear of needles and medical procedures.

Common Questions About Claustrophobia

Is claustrophobia a type of specific phobia?

Yes. Claustrophobia, an intense fear of enclosed spaces, is a type of specific phobia and one of the more common ones.

What causes claustrophobia?

Claustrophobia can be caused by frightening past experiences, learning from others, sensitivity to body sensations, or panic in a confined space.

What are symptoms of claustrophobia?

Symptoms of claustrophobia include panic, a racing heart, sweating, fear of suffocating or being trapped, and avoiding enclosed spaces.

Is claustrophobia the same as agoraphobia?

No. Claustrophobia is fear of enclosed spaces, while agoraphobia is fear of situations where escape or help might be difficult during panic.

Does exposure therapy work for claustrophobia?

Yes. Gradual exposure therapy is the most effective treatment for claustrophobia, often combined with cognitive strategies.

How quickly can people overcome claustrophobia?

Many people overcome claustrophobia within a relatively short course of exposure-based therapy, though the pace varies.

How can I get through an MRI with claustrophobia?

Tell the imaging team, ask about open or wide-bore scanners, use music or contact with staff, and discuss medication with your physician.

Can claustrophobia start suddenly?

Claustrophobia can start suddenly after a frightening experience or develop gradually without a clear cause.

Do children develop claustrophobia too?

Yes. Claustrophobia can begin in childhood or adulthood, and children respond well to gentle, gradual exposure-based therapy.

Can therapy for claustrophobia be done virtually?

Yes. Much of the planning and some exposure practice for claustrophobia can be done virtually, with real-world practice between sessions.
