What Is Codependency

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Codependency is a relationship pattern in which a person consistently prioritizes someone else's needs, feelings, or problems over their own, often to the point of neglecting themselves. The term originally described partners and family members of people with alcohol or drug addiction, who often organized their lives around managing the other person's behaviour. It is now used more broadly to describe relationships where one person's sense of worth and stability depends heavily on caring for, pleasing, or controlling another. Codependency is not a formal diagnosis, but it describes real patterns that can cause distress. With awareness and support, people can build more balanced relationships.

What Codependency Looks Like

  • Feeling responsible for other people's emotions and problems.
  • Difficulty saying no or setting limits, even when overwhelmed.
  • Putting others' needs first and ignoring your own.
  • Feeling anxious, guilty, or empty when you are not needed.
  • Trying to fix, rescue, or control others' behaviour.
  • Staying in relationships that are harmful or unbalanced.
  • Covering for someone's drinking, spending, or other behaviour.
  • Losing track of your own interests, opinions, or identity.
  • Resentment that builds from giving more than you receive.

Why It Happens

Codependent patterns often develop in childhood. Growing up with a parent who struggled with addiction, mental illness, or chronic illness may lead a child to take on caretaking roles, as described on the page about parentification. In families where love felt conditional, or where a child's needs were ignored, people may learn that their worth comes from being helpful. Emotional neglect, trauma, or unpredictable environments can teach people to focus on others' moods as a way to stay safe, a pattern similar to the fawn response.

In adulthood, these patterns may continue in romantic relationships, friendships, or work, particularly in helping professions. Codependency can be reinforced when a partner relies on the person's caretaking, such as in relationships affected by addiction. Over time, codependency can lead to exhaustion, resentment, anxiety, depression, and a sense of lost identity.

Codependency can also make it hard to leave unhealthy relationships. A person may feel that they cannot abandon someone who needs them, or may fear who they would be without the caretaking role. Recognizing these fears is an important step in making choices that protect wellbeing.

People with codependent patterns are often described as kind, reliable, and generous, and these are genuine strengths. The difficulty arises when giving is driven by fear or obligation rather than choice, and when there is little room left for one's own needs. Many people find that as they care for themselves more, their care for others becomes more sustainable and less resentful.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as caring deeply. Healthy care includes caring for yourself too.
  • Not a diagnosis. It describes a pattern rather than a mental illness.
  • Not a reason for blame. Codependency usually develops as an adaptation to difficult circumstances.
  • Not the same as interdependence. Healthy relationships involve mutual support with room for individuality.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Change often begins with recognizing the pattern and reconnecting with your own needs, feelings, and interests. Learning to set and hold boundaries, explored on the page about boundary issues, is central. Therapy can help people understand where codependent patterns came from, reduce guilt about self-care, and build self-worth that does not depend on being needed. Approaches such as cognitive behavioural, schema, and family systems therapy are often used. Support groups, including groups for families of people with addiction, can reduce isolation. Couples therapy can help partners rebalance responsibilities and build healthier interdependence.

Common Questions About Codependency

Where did the term codependency come from?

It originally described partners and families of people with addiction, and is now used more broadly for caretaking relationship patterns.

Is codependency a mental health diagnosis?

No. It is not a formal diagnosis, but it describes real patterns that can cause distress.

How do I know if I am codependent?

Signs include putting others first at your own expense, difficulty saying no, and feeling responsible for others' feelings.

What causes codependency?

It often develops in childhood, especially in families affected by addiction, illness, neglect, or conditional love.

How is codependency different from being caring?

Healthy caring includes looking after yourself. Codependency involves neglecting your own needs to manage others.

Can codependency happen in friendships and work?

Yes. Codependent patterns can appear in any close relationship, including friendships and helping professions.

Why do I feel empty when no one needs me?

If your sense of worth depends on being needed, times without caretaking can feel uncomfortable or empty.

What is the difference between codependency and interdependence?

Interdependence involves mutual support with room for individuality. Codependency involves imbalance and loss of self.

How can I stop being codependent?

Notice the pattern, reconnect with your needs, practise boundaries, and seek therapy or support groups.

Are there support groups for codependency?

Yes. Groups for families of people with addiction and codependency-focused groups offer peer support.
