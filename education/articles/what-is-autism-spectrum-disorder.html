What Is Autism Spectrum Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Autism spectrum disorder, often simply called autism, is a lifelong neurodevelopmental difference that affects how a person communicates, relates to others, processes sensory information, and experiences the world. Autistic people often have distinct patterns of focused interests, a preference for routine and predictability, and ways of thinking that differ from the neurotypical majority. Autism is described as a spectrum because it looks very different from person to person. Some autistic people need substantial daily support, while others live independently and may not be identified until adulthood. Autism is not an illness to be cured. Understanding it helps autistic people and those around them build lives that fit.

What Autism Looks Like

The diagnosis is based on two broad areas. The first involves social communication and interaction, which may include:

  • Differences in conversation, such as preferring direct communication or finding small talk tiring.
  • Different use of eye contact, facial expression, or gesture.
  • Difficulty reading unspoken social rules or others' intentions.
  • Finding friendships and group settings effortful, even when connection is wanted.

The second involves restricted or repetitive patterns, which may include:

  • Deep, intense interests that bring joy and expertise.
  • A strong need for routine, with distress when plans change unexpectedly.
  • Repetitive movements or sounds, often called stimming, which help with regulation.
  • Sensory differences, such as being very sensitive to noise, light, or certain textures, or seeking out certain sensations.

Many autistic people also describe strengths, such as attention to detail, honesty, pattern recognition, deep focus, and loyalty. Challenges often come from environments that are not designed for autistic needs rather than from autism itself.

Why It Happens

Autism has strong genetic roots, and it often runs in families. Research shows that differences in brain development begin before birth and shape how information is processed throughout life. Large studies have found no link between vaccines and autism. Parenting does not cause autism. Rates of diagnosis have increased over recent decades, largely because of broader criteria, greater awareness, and better recognition in girls, adults, and people who mask their traits.

Autism often co-occurs with other conditions, including ADHD, anxiety, depression, sleep difficulties, and gastrointestinal problems. Many autistic adults experience exhaustion from years of trying to fit in, and some experience overwhelm that leads to meltdowns and shutdowns.

Autistic experience also changes across a lifetime. A child who struggled with transitions at school may, as an adult, build routines and careers around their strengths. Stress, major life changes, and burnout can make support needs rise again for a while. Seeing autism as lifelong but not fixed in its effects helps families and individuals plan for different stages.

What It Is Not

  • Not a disease or something that needs fixing. Autism is a different way of being, and support focuses on wellbeing rather than normalizing.
  • Not a lack of empathy. Many autistic people feel deeply but may express or read emotions differently.
  • Not caused by parenting or vaccines.
  • Not only a childhood condition. Autistic children become autistic adults, and many are diagnosed later in life, as described on the page about late diagnosis.
  • Not the same in everyone. Knowing one autistic person means knowing one autistic person.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

Assessment is carried out by a psychologist, psychiatrist, or paediatrician with training in autism. It usually involves developmental history, interviews, questionnaires, and observation, and may include input from family or teachers. For children, support may include speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, and school accommodations. For adults, neurodiversity-affirming therapy can help with anxiety, burnout, identity, and relationships. Practical adjustments, such as sensory-friendly spaces, clear communication, and predictable routines, often make a big difference at home, school, and work.

Common Questions About Autism Spectrum Disorder

Why is autism called a spectrum?

Autism looks very different from person to person, in support needs, strengths, and challenges, so it is described as a spectrum rather than a single profile.

What are the two main areas used to diagnose autism?

Diagnosis looks at differences in social communication and interaction, and restricted or repetitive patterns of behaviour, interests, or sensory experience.

Do vaccines cause autism?

No. Large, well-designed studies have found no link between vaccines and autism.

Is autism caused by parenting?

No. Autism has strong genetic roots and involves differences in brain development that begin before birth.

What is stimming and why do autistic people do it?

Stimming is repetitive movement or sound, such as rocking or hand flapping, that helps with regulation, focus, or expressing emotion.

Do autistic people feel empathy?

Yes. Many autistic people feel emotions deeply but may express them or read others' cues differently.

Why are more people being diagnosed with autism now?

Broader criteria, greater awareness, and better recognition in girls and adults account for much of the increase.

What conditions commonly occur alongside autism?

ADHD, anxiety, depression, sleep problems, and gastrointestinal issues are common alongside autism.

Who can diagnose autism in British Columbia?

Assessments are done by trained psychologists, psychiatrists, or paediatricians. Children may access publicly funded assessment, while many adults seek private assessment.

What does neurodiversity-affirming support mean?

It means support that respects autistic ways of being and focuses on wellbeing and meeting needs rather than making someone appear neurotypical.
