What If Only One Partner Wants Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If only one partner wants therapy, you still have options. You can invite your partner in a low-pressure way, suggest a single consultation rather than an open-ended commitment, or begin individual therapy focused on the relationship. Individual work can help you understand your own patterns, communicate more effectively, and decide what you need, and changes in one partner often shift the dynamic between both. Reluctance is common and often comes from fear of being blamed, feeling exposed, or doubting that therapy works. If you are unsure whether you want to stay in the relationship, or if there is abuse or fear, a different approach may be needed.

How It Works

Understanding reluctance. Common reasons include worry that the therapist will take sides, discomfort talking about feelings, past negative experiences, cost or time concerns, or fear of what might come up. Asking about these concerns with curiosity can open the door.

Inviting your partner. Explain why therapy matters to you, using "I" statements rather than criticism, and frame it as working on "us" rather than fixing them. Suggest trying one or two sessions and deciding together afterward.

Starting on your own. Individual therapy for relationship patterns can help you understand your reactions, attachment patterns, and communication habits. Some partners join later once they see the benefits.

When one partner is unsure about staying. Discernment counselling, a short and focused process, can help couples where one person is leaning out decide whether to work on the relationship.

Practical Details

Choosing a therapist. If you start individually, look for someone experienced with relationships. If your partner may join later, ask whether the therapist can see you both or would refer you to a separate couples therapist, since some prefer not to switch from individual to couples work.

Confidentiality. What you share in individual therapy is confidential, within legal limits. Your partner is not informed unless you choose to share.

Safety. If your partner's reluctance is part of a pattern of control, intimidation, or abuse, couples therapy may not be appropriate. Individual support and safety planning are a better first step. See the abuse resources page and the escape planning page.

Virtual options. Some reluctant partners find a first virtual session less intimidating, as described on the page about virtual couples therapy.

Fees. Individual and couples sessions may have different fees. Ask the clinic and check your extended health plan.

How to Approach It

  • Pick a calm moment. Avoid raising therapy during or right after an argument.
  • Share your reasons. Focus on wanting to feel closer or argue less, not on what your partner is doing wrong.
  • Listen to their concerns. Take them seriously, and offer information about what sessions are like, such as the page about the first couples therapy session.
  • Offer choices. Let them help choose the therapist, format, or timing.
  • Start for yourself if needed. Your own wellbeing and growth matter, regardless of your partner's decision.

Communication tools on the page about navigating conflict styles may also help in the meantime.

Common Questions About When Only One Partner Wants Therapy

What can I do if my partner does not want couples therapy?

You can invite your partner gently, suggest a single session, or start individual therapy focused on the relationship on your own.

Can individual therapy help my relationship if my partner will not attend?

Yes. Individual therapy can help you understand your patterns and communicate differently, which often shifts the relationship dynamic.

Why might a partner be reluctant to try therapy?

A partner may be reluctant because of fear of blame, discomfort with feelings, cost, past experiences, or doubt that therapy works.

How do I ask my partner to try couples therapy?

Choose a calm moment, use I statements, frame couples therapy as working on the relationship, and suggest trying one or two sessions.

What is discernment counselling for a partner who is unsure?

Discernment counselling is a short process for couples where one partner is unsure about staying, helping them decide whether to pursue therapy.

Can my individual therapist later see us as a couple?

Some therapists can, while others prefer to refer you to a separate couples therapist to stay balanced. Ask your individual therapist about their approach.

Will my partner find out what I say in individual therapy?

No. What you share in individual therapy is confidential within legal limits, and your partner is not informed unless you choose to share.

Is couples therapy appropriate if my partner is controlling?

Couples therapy may not be safe when a partner is controlling or abusive. Individual support and safety planning are usually a better first step.

Would a virtual session help a reluctant partner?

Some reluctant partners find a first virtual couples session less intimidating than going to an office in person.

Should I start therapy even if my partner never joins?

Yes. Your own wellbeing and growth matter, and individual therapy can help regardless of whether your partner joins.
