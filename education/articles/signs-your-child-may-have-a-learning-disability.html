Signs Your Child May Have a Learning Disability

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Signs that a child may have a learning disability include ongoing difficulty learning to read, write, spell, or do math despite good teaching and effort, a noticeable gap between how capable they seem and their schoolwork, and growing frustration, avoidance, or low confidence around school. Signs vary by age. In young children, they may include trouble learning letters, sounds, rhymes, or numbers. In older children, reading may stay slow, written work may be much weaker than spoken ideas, and math facts may not stick. Every child learns at their own pace, but difficulties that persist for months despite extra support are worth looking into. Early identification makes support more effective.

Signs by Age

Preschool and Kindergarten

  • Difficulty learning the alphabet, letter sounds, or numbers
  • Trouble rhyming or recognizing that words start with the same sound
  • Difficulty following simple directions or learning routines
  • Delayed speech or language

Early Elementary

  • Slow progress in learning to read or decode words
  • Frequent guessing at words
  • Difficulty remembering sight words or spelling
  • Trouble with counting, number sense, or basic math facts
  • Awkward or effortful handwriting

Later Elementary and Middle School

  • Slow, effortful reading and difficulty understanding what was read
  • Written work much shorter or weaker than spoken ideas
  • Continued difficulty with math facts and multi-step problems
  • Difficulty organizing assignments or studying
  • Avoiding homework or reading

Teens

  • Reading taking much longer than peers
  • Difficulty with essays, note-taking, or foreign languages
  • Strong in some subjects and struggling in others
  • Low confidence, anxiety, or disengagement from school

Emotional and Behavioural Signs

Children with learning difficulties often feel discouraged. They may say "I am stupid," avoid tasks, act as the class clown, have stomach aches before school, or become upset during homework. These signs can be mistaken for behaviour problems or lack of motivation, as described on the page about signs your child may need mental health support.

What It Is Not

A learning disability is not a reflection of intelligence, effort, or parenting. Many children with learning disabilities are bright, as described on the page about why a child may struggle to read even though they are bright. Not every child who struggles has a learning disability either. Attention difficulties, anxiety, language disorders, vision or hearing problems, and gaps in instruction can all affect learning.

Why Early Identification Matters

Learning difficulties that go unaddressed tend to have a widening effect over time. A child who struggles to decode words in grade one may, by grade four, be falling behind in science and social studies because they cannot read the material, even if they understand the concepts. Early identification allows support to begin while skills are still developing and before a child concludes that they are not smart. It also helps teachers and families understand behaviour, such as avoidance or frustration, in a more compassionate way.

What to Do Next

  1. Talk with the teacher. Share what you are noticing and ask what they see in class.
  2. Check vision and hearing. Rule out sensory problems.
  3. Ask about school support. Many schools offer early intervention for reading or math.
  4. Track progress. Note whether skills improve with extra help.
  5. Consider an assessment. If difficulties persist, a psychoeducational assessment can identify strengths, challenges, and recommendations, as described on the page about what a specific learning disorder is.

After an assessment, the page about school supports in BC after a psychoeducational assessment explains how results can be used.

Common Questions About Signs of Learning Disabilities

What might a learning disability look like in a school-aged child?

In a school-aged child, a learning disability may look like ongoing difficulty with reading, writing, or math despite good teaching, a gap between ability and schoolwork, and frustration around school.

Can a learning disability be spotted before kindergarten?

Some early signs of a learning disability, such as difficulty learning letters, sounds, rhymes, or numbers, can appear before kindergarten, though diagnosis usually comes later.

Can a learning disability look like a behaviour problem?

Yes. Children with learning disabilities may avoid tasks, act out, or seem unmotivated, which can be mistaken for behaviour problems.

At what age can a learning disability be diagnosed?

Learning disabilities are often identified in early elementary school, though they can be assessed at any age, including adulthood.

Should I wait to see if my child grows out of learning difficulties?

Learning difficulties that persist for months despite extra support are worth looking into, since early help is more effective.

Who can diagnose a learning disability?

A psychologist can diagnose a learning disability through a psychoeducational assessment.

Is a learning disability the same as ADHD?

No. ADHD and learning disabilities are different, though they often occur together and can both affect learning.

Can anxiety cause learning difficulties?

Anxiety can affect concentration and performance, and children with learning disabilities often develop anxiety about school.

What should I do if I think my child has a learning disability?

Talk with the teacher, check vision and hearing, ask about school support, track progress, and consider a psychoeducational assessment.

Can a child with a learning disability succeed in school?

Yes. With the right instruction, accommodations, and encouragement, children with learning disabilities can succeed in school.
