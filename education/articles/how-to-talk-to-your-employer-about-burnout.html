How to Talk to Your Employer About Burnout

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Talking to your employer about burnout usually goes best when you prepare, focus on the impact on your work, and come with a few specific requests, such as adjusting deadlines, clarifying priorities, or reducing a particular workload. You do not have to share personal health details to have this conversation. Many managers would rather know early than find out when someone is already exhausted or leaving. The conversation may feel uncomfortable, but it can lead to changes that make work more sustainable. Planning ahead can make it feel more manageable and help you stay clear under pressure.

Why Speaking Up Helps

Burnout often grows from workplace conditions such as heavy workloads, unclear expectations, lack of control, and too little support. Individual coping can only go so far when these conditions stay the same. Raising the issue gives your employer a chance to help address the causes. It also reduces the strain of hiding how you are feeling, which itself takes energy.

Speaking up early usually leads to more options. When exhaustion has become severe, choices may narrow to time off or leaving. When concerns are raised sooner, small adjustments are often enough. Understanding the warning signs, described on the page about preventing burnout, can help you choose a good time to act.

Strategies to Try

1. Get Clear on What Is Happening

Before the conversation, note what has changed. Which tasks or situations drain you most? How has your work been affected, for example missed deadlines, longer hours, or difficulty concentrating? Specific examples help your manager understand.

2. Decide What to Share

You can describe the situation in work terms, such as "My workload has been unsustainable and it is affecting my focus," without naming a diagnosis. If you have a medical note or are receiving treatment, you can decide whether and how much to share. Human resources can explain how health information is handled.

3. Come With Requests

Think about what would help most. Options might include adjusting deadlines, reassigning some tasks, clarifying priorities, reducing meetings, flexible hours or remote work, protected focus time, a temporary reduction in hours, or time off. Having two or three ideas makes it easier to move toward solutions.

4. Choose the Right Time and Format

Request a private meeting rather than raising it in passing or during a busy moment. Some people prefer to send a short email first explaining that they would like to discuss workload.

5. Use Clear, Calm Language

You might say: "I want to do my best work, and I have noticed that the current workload is making that hard. I have been working late most nights and feeling run down. Could we look at priorities together, and consider moving the report deadline?" Focusing on shared goals often makes the conversation feel collaborative.

6. Agree on Next Steps

Before ending, summarize what was agreed and when you will check in again. A brief follow-up email can help keep everyone on the same page.

Common Obstacles

Many people fear being seen as weak, uncommitted, or replaceable. These fears are understandable, especially in competitive or insecure workplaces. Framing the conversation around sustainable performance can help. If your manager is part of the problem, you might speak with another leader, human resources, or an employee assistance program. Some people struggle with feeling guilty or like a burden, which can be linked with people-pleasing. Practising what to say with a friend or therapist can help.

Sometimes a conversation does not go well. A manager may dismiss the concerns or offer changes that do not help. In that case, you can ask for a follow-up meeting, involve human resources, keep a record of what was discussed, and learn about your rights. In BC, employment standards, human rights protections, and workplace health and safety rules may apply, and a union representative or employment lawyer can advise on specific situations. If burnout is severe, a doctor can assess whether medical leave is needed.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If you feel exhausted most days, detached from your work, or hopeless, or if stress is affecting your sleep, health, or relationships, professional support can help. Burnout can overlap with depression and anxiety, as explained on the page about burnout versus depression. A doctor can check your physical health and discuss time off. Therapy can help you recover, set boundaries, and plan conversations or career decisions. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Talking to Your Employer About Burnout

Do I have to tell my manager I have a diagnosis to discuss burnout?

No. You can describe the impact of workload in work terms and decide whether to share any health information.

What should I ask my boss for when I am burning out?

Consider adjusted deadlines, clearer priorities, reassigned tasks, flexible hours, fewer meetings, or time off.

How do I start the conversation with my manager about exhaustion?

Request a private meeting, describe specific changes you have noticed, and propose a few practical solutions.

Will my employer think less of me if I say I am struggling?

Some people worry about this, but many managers prefer knowing early. Framing it around sustainable performance can help.

What if my manager is the main source of my burnout?

You might speak with another leader, human resources, or an employee assistance program.

Should I send an email before meeting about my workload?

A short email asking to discuss workload can help your manager prepare and make the meeting feel less sudden.

What should I do if my employer dismisses my concerns?

Ask for a follow-up, involve human resources, keep notes, and learn about your rights under BC employment rules.

Can a doctor recommend time off for burnout?

Yes. A doctor can assess your health and advise whether medical leave may help.

How can I practise what to say to my employer?

Rehearsing with a friend or therapist, or writing out key points, can help you feel calmer and clearer.

Is it better to quit than to ask for changes at work?

It depends on your situation. Raising concerns first often opens options, but leaving can be the right choice when change is not possible.
