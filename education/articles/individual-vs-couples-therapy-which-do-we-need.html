Individual vs. Couples Therapy: Which Do We Need

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Couples therapy is usually the better fit when the main concern is the relationship itself, such as recurring conflict, communication problems, distance, trust after a betrayal, or a major decision you need to make together. Individual therapy is usually better when one person's own difficulties, such as depression, anxiety, trauma, or long-standing personal patterns, are the main concern, even if they affect the relationship. Many couples benefit from both, either at the same time or one after the other. If there is abuse or fear in the relationship, individual support comes first. If you are unsure, an initial consultation can help you decide.

How Each Works

Couples therapy. Both partners attend most sessions. The therapist focuses on patterns between you, such as how conflicts start and end, how needs are expressed, and how you reconnect, using approaches like the Gottman Method or emotionally focused therapy. The page about how couples therapy works has more detail.

Individual therapy. One person meets with a therapist to explore their own thoughts, feelings, history, and patterns. This can include how they show up in relationships, as described on the page about individual therapy for relationship patterns.

Both together. Some couples attend couples therapy while one or both partners also see an individual therapist. Often these are different therapists, so each relationship stays clear and balanced.

Practical Details

Signs couples therapy may fit.

  • The same arguments keep repeating.
  • You feel distant, lonely, or disconnected from each other.
  • Trust has been damaged.
  • You are facing a big decision together, such as having children or moving.
  • Both partners are willing to attend and work on the relationship.

Signs individual therapy may fit first.

  • One partner is struggling with depression, anxiety, trauma, or substance use.
  • Personal history, such as attachment patterns, is driving reactions.
  • One partner is unwilling to attend, as explored on the page about when only one partner wants therapy.
  • You are unsure whether you want to stay in the relationship and need space to think.
  • There is abuse, coercive control, or fear of a partner.

Safety. Couples therapy is not recommended when one partner is abusive or controlling, because joint sessions can increase risk. See the abuse resources page.

Fees and benefits. Couples and individual sessions may have different fees, and coverage varies. Check your extended health plan.

How to Decide Together

  • Name the main concern. Is it mostly about how you relate, or about one person's wellbeing?
  • Ask what each of you hopes for. Shared goals point toward couples work.
  • Be honest about readiness. Couples therapy works best when both partners are willing to participate.
  • Consider sequencing. Sometimes individual work first makes couples therapy more productive later.
  • Book a consultation. A therapist can help you decide, as described on the page about initial consultations for couples therapy.

Common Questions About Choosing Individual or Couples Therapy

How do we know if we need individual or couples therapy?

Couples therapy fits when the relationship itself is the main concern. Individual therapy fits when one person's own difficulties are the main focus.

Can couples therapy help if one partner has depression?

Couples therapy can help a relationship affected by depression, but the partner with depression usually also benefits from individual treatment.

Is it common for partners to combine couples sessions with their own individual therapy?

Yes. Many partners attend couples therapy while also seeing an individual therapist, often a different clinician to keep each relationship balanced.

Should I start with individual therapy before couples therapy?

Sometimes. Individual therapy first can help with personal concerns or uncertainty about the relationship, making later couples work more productive.

Why might a therapist suggest individual sessions instead of joint couples work?

A therapist may suggest individual sessions when one partner is abusive or controlling, because joint couples sessions can increase risk, or when personal concerns need attention first.

What are signs we would benefit from couples therapy?

Repeating arguments, feeling disconnected, damaged trust, a big shared decision, and both partners being willing are signs couples therapy may help.

Can individual therapy improve my relationship?

Yes. Individual therapy can help you understand your reactions and relationship patterns, which often improves how you relate to a partner.

Should my couples therapist also be my individual therapist?

Often not. Many clinicians prefer that individual and couples therapy be with different therapists so the couples work stays balanced.

What if only one of us wants couples therapy?

If only one partner wants couples therapy, individual therapy focused on the relationship is a useful place to start.

How can a consultation help us choose between individual and couples therapy?

In a consultation, a therapist can hear both perspectives and recommend individual therapy, couples therapy, or a combination for your situation.
