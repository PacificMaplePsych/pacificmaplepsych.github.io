The History of Borderline Personality Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) has a long and complicated history. Physicians described people with intense, changeable emotions, impulsivity, and fear of rejection as far back as ancient Greece, but the condition was given many different names over the centuries. The term "borderline" was introduced in 1938, when it described people thought to sit on the border between neurosis and psychosis. BPD was officially recognized as a diagnosis in 1980. Since then, research has transformed understanding of the condition, and effective treatments such as dialectical behaviour therapy have shown that many people with BPD recover. That long history of confusion helps explain why BPD is still often misunderstood and stigmatized.

Early Descriptions

Writers in ancient medicine described patterns that resemble BPD. Aretaeus of Cappadocia, a Greek physician usually dated to the first or second century, wrote about people whose mood could swing between anger, melancholy, and agitation, who changed their minds readily, and who at times withdrew from others and wished to die. Modern historians caution against diagnosing people from ancient texts, but these accounts show that intense, shifting emotional states have long been recognized.

Over the following centuries, people with these patterns were described under labels such as melancholia, hysteria, and various forms of "insanity." Many of these labels carried judgment and were applied disproportionately to women.

How the Term "Borderline" Emerged

In 1938, American psychoanalyst Adolph Stern described a "borderline group" of patients who did not fit neatly into the categories of the time. They struggled with deep insecurity, sensitivity to criticism, and intense reactions in relationships, and they often did poorly in traditional psychoanalysis. The word "borderline" reflected the belief that they were on the border between neurosis and psychosis.

In the decades that followed, clinicians including Otto Kernberg described "borderline personality organization," and John Gunderson and colleagues developed clearer descriptions that could be studied in research. This work helped move BPD from a vague label toward a defined pattern of emotional instability, unstable relationships and self-image, and impulsivity.

Recognition as a Diagnosis

Borderline personality disorder was first included in the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-III) in 1980. Formal criteria made research possible, but the name stuck, even though the "border with psychosis" idea is no longer how BPD is understood. Some clinicians and people with lived experience prefer terms such as emotionally unstable personality, or emphasize the links with trauma, as described on the page about BPD and complex PTSD.

The Turning Point in Treatment

For many years, BPD was widely believed to be untreatable, and people with the diagnosis were sometimes turned away by clinicians. That changed in the late 1980s and 1990s. Psychologist Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT), and her treatment manual, Cognitive-Behavioral Treatment of Borderline Personality Disorder, was published in 1993. In 1991, she published the first randomized controlled trial showing that a psychotherapy could help people with BPD. Linehan later spoke publicly about her own history of severe emotional suffering.

Other evidence-based approaches followed, including mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, schema therapy, and good psychiatric management. Long-term studies then found that most people with BPD improve substantially over time, as described on the page about whether people recover from BPD.

Why the History Still Matters

Old ideas about BPD being untreatable, manipulative, or attention-seeking still linger, even though research has moved on. These beliefs can affect how people are treated in health care and how they see themselves, as explained on the page about BPD stigma and common myths. Understanding the history makes it easier to see BPD for what current evidence shows it to be: a serious but treatable condition involving intense emotional pain, from which many people build lives they value.

Common Questions About the History of BPD

When was borderline personality disorder first described?

Patterns resembling borderline personality disorder were described in ancient Greek medicine, but the term "borderline" was introduced in 1938.

What did Adolph Stern describe in 1938 about borderline patients?

In 1938, Adolph Stern described a borderline group of patients who did not fit existing categories, which led to the term borderline personality disorder.

What was the border in borderline personality disorder originally thought to be?

Early clinicians thought people with borderline personality disorder sat on the border between neurosis and psychosis, an idea that is no longer used.

When was BPD added to the DSM?

Borderline personality disorder was added to the DSM in 1980, in the third edition, known as DSM-III.

What role did Marsha Linehan play in BPD treatment?

Psychologist Marsha Linehan developed dialectical behaviour therapy for borderline personality disorder, publishing her treatment manual in 1993 and the first randomized trial in 1991.

Was BPD once thought to be untreatable?

Yes. For many years, BPD was widely believed to be untreatable, but research since the 1990s shows that effective treatments exist.

Is BPD linked to the old diagnosis of hysteria?

Some patterns once labelled hysteria overlap with what is now called BPD, and both labels were applied disproportionately to women.

Why do some people want to rename borderline personality disorder?

Some people want to rename borderline personality disorder because the term is outdated and stigmatizing, suggesting alternatives such as emotionally unstable personality.

What treatments exist for BPD today?

Treatments for BPD today include DBT, mentalization-based treatment, transference-focused psychotherapy, schema therapy, and good psychiatric management.

Why does the history of BPD matter today?

The history of BPD matters because outdated beliefs about it being untreatable still fuel stigma, even though most people improve with time and treatment.
