Why Do I Feel Like a Burden to Others

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Feeling like a burden can be linked with thoughts of suicide. If you are having those thoughts, or feel others would be better off without you, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Feeling like a burden usually means you believe your needs, problems, or presence cost others more than you give back. This belief can grow during illness, unemployment, or times when you rely on others more than usual. It can also come from early experiences of being made to feel like too much, or from low self-worth. Depression strongly amplifies this feeling, making it seem like an obvious truth when it is usually a distortion. The people around you often see things very differently.

People describe hesitating to ask for help, apologizing constantly, hiding how they feel so they do not worry anyone, or withdrawing from friends because they believe they are dragging others down.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Illness or disability. Needing more care or support can make people worry about the impact on loved ones, even when those loved ones are glad to help.

Life setbacks. Job loss, financial difficulty, or a breakup can shift roles and make someone feel less able to contribute.

Family patterns. Children who were told they were a nuisance, or whose needs were treated as inconvenient, often carry that belief into adulthood.

Low self-worth. People who feel unworthy of love may assume others only tolerate them.

Constant guilt. Those who feel guilty all the time may see their needs as a source of harm to others.

Aging. Older adults sometimes worry about becoming dependent on family, particularly after health changes or retirement.

When It Looks More Like Depression

Depression changes how people see themselves and their relationships, drawing attention to evidence of failure and filtering out evidence of being valued. A sense of burdensomeness is common in depression and deserves particular care, because research links it with suicidal thinking. Signs that depression may be involved include:

  • Persistent low mood, emptiness, or hopelessness about the future.
  • Feeling worthless or excessively guilty.
  • Feeling disconnected from everyone around you and pulling away.
  • Loss of interest in things you used to enjoy.
  • Changes in sleep, appetite, or energy.
  • Thoughts that others would be better off without you, or wishing you could disappear.

Feeling like a burden often leads to withdrawal, which reduces the very connections that could challenge the belief. Loved ones usually want to help and often feel closer when they are allowed to. Depression is treatable, and as it lifts, the sense of burdensomeness usually eases too.

What Can Help

  • Test the belief gently. Ask a trusted person how they actually feel about supporting you. Their answer is often surprising.
  • Remember relationships are mutual. Most relationships go through seasons of giving and receiving. Needing support now does not erase what you have offered or will offer.
  • Stay connected. Keep small contacts going, even brief messages, rather than withdrawing.
  • Find ways to contribute. Small acts, such as listening to a friend or helping a neighbour, can remind you of your value.
  • Share the thought. Telling someone "I have been feeling like a burden" opens space for support and reassurance.
  • Get help if the feeling is heavy. Persistent burdensomeness is a sign to reach out to a professional.

Common Questions About Feeling Like a Burden

Why do I always feel like I am bothering people?

This feeling often comes from early experiences, low self-worth, or depression. It can lead to apologizing constantly and avoiding asking for help, even when others are happy to support you.

Could feeling like a burden to my family be depression talking?

It is common in depression. Depression distorts how people see their value to others, making burdensomeness feel true even when loved ones see things very differently.

Why is feeling like a burden linked to suicide risk?

Research links perceived burdensomeness with suicidal thinking because it can create a belief that others would be better off without you. If you have these thoughts, call or text 9-8-8.

How do I ask for help without feeling like a burden?

Be specific about what you need, remember that relationships involve giving and receiving, and notice that most people feel good when they can help someone they care about.

Do my loved ones really see me as a burden?

Usually not. People who feel like a burden often underestimate how much others value them. Asking directly, or noticing how others respond to you, can challenge the belief.

Can chronic illness make me feel like a burden to my family?

Yes. Needing more care can bring worry about the impact on loved ones. Talking openly with family and seeking support for yourself can ease this feeling.

Why do I hide my feelings so I don't worry anyone?

Hiding feelings can seem like protecting others, but it often increases isolation. Sharing even a little can bring closeness and support.

Can childhood experiences make me feel like too much for people?

Yes. Being told you were a nuisance, or having your needs treated as inconvenient, can create a lasting belief that you are a burden.

How can I support someone who says they feel like a burden?

Take it seriously, listen without arguing, tell them you value them, and ask directly about suicidal thoughts. Encourage professional help and share crisis resources such as 9-8-8.

Can therapy help if I feel like a burden?

Yes. Therapy can address depression, challenge beliefs about burdensomeness, rebuild self-worth, and strengthen connections, which usually eases the feeling over time.
