Insomnia During Menopause

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Insomnia during menopause is very common. Many people in perimenopause and menopause have trouble falling asleep, wake frequently during the night, or wake too early and cannot get back to sleep. Night sweats and hot flushes are a major cause, but hormonal changes can also affect sleep directly, and anxiety, low mood, and a busy mind at night add to the problem. Once poor sleep becomes a pattern, worry about sleep and habits such as spending long hours in bed can keep insomnia going even after other symptoms improve. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is recommended as a first-line treatment and works well for menopausal insomnia.

Why Menopause Disrupts Sleep

Night Sweats and Hot Flushes

Sudden waves of heat can wake you repeatedly, often followed by chills and difficulty settling again.

Hormonal Changes

Estrogen and progesterone influence sleep and body temperature regulation. Progesterone has a calming effect, so falling levels can make sleep lighter.

Mood and Anxiety

Anxiety, low mood, and racing thoughts become more common in perimenopause, as described on the page about anxiety in perimenopause, and they make it harder to fall and stay asleep.

Other Sleep Disorders

The risk of sleep apnea rises after menopause, and restless legs can also disrupt sleep. Loud snoring, gasping, or daytime sleepiness should be checked by a physician.

Learned Sleeplessness

After many bad nights, the bed can become linked with frustration and alertness, as described on the page about what insomnia is.

How Poor Sleep Affects Mental Health

Ongoing sleep loss affects mood, irritability, concentration, and stress tolerance. It can contribute to menopause rage, brain fog, and depression, and depression in turn disrupts sleep, as described on the page about how sleep and mental health affect each other.

What Helps

CBT for Insomnia

CBT-I is a structured therapy, usually six to eight sessions, that retrains the body's sleep system. It includes stimulus control, adjusting time in bed to build sleep drive, changing unhelpful beliefs about sleep, and relaxation strategies. It has strong evidence in people going through menopause, as described on the page about CBT for insomnia.

Managing Night Sweats

A cool bedroom, layered bedding, breathable sleepwear, and a fan can help. CBT and clinical hypnosis also have evidence for reducing how much hot flushes bother people, as described on the page about CBT for hot flushes and night sweats.

Medical Options

A physician can discuss hormone therapy, non-hormonal medications, and assessment for sleep apnea or other conditions.

Helpful Habits

Keep a consistent wake time, get morning light, limit alcohol and late caffeine, and wind down before bed, as described on the page about how to wind down before bed when your mind is busy.

What It Is Not

Menopausal insomnia is not something you simply have to put up with, and it is not a sign of weakness. It is also not always caused by hormones alone. Because several factors are often involved, a combined approach usually works best. If you feel very sleepy during the day, fall asleep while driving, or snore loudly, see a physician promptly.

Partners and Shared Beds

Night sweats and frequent waking can also disrupt a partner's sleep, which may lead to frustration on both sides. Some couples find it helps to use separate blankets, adjust room temperature, or sleep apart temporarily on difficult nights. These changes do not mean the relationship is in trouble. Talking openly about what helps both partners rest can reduce tension and protect the relationship during a demanding period.

Common Questions About Menopause and Insomnia

Why is it so hard to sleep during menopause?

Sleep problems during menopause often come from night sweats, hormonal changes, anxiety, and habits that develop after many bad nights.

How common are sleep problems during the menopause transition?

Sleep problems are among the most common complaints during the menopause transition, affecting many people in perimenopause and after menopause.

Does CBT for insomnia work during menopause?

Yes. CBT for insomnia is a first-line treatment with strong evidence for improving sleep during menopause.

How do night sweats affect sleep?

Night sweats can wake you repeatedly and make it hard to settle again, leading to fragmented sleep during menopause.

Can menopause cause sleep apnea?

The risk of sleep apnea rises after menopause. Loud snoring, gasping, or daytime sleepiness should be checked by a physician.

Does reducing night sweats improve menopausal insomnia?

Often, yes. Reducing night sweats, whether through hormone therapy, other medication, or a cooler bedroom, can improve menopausal insomnia.

How does poor sleep in menopause affect mood?

Poor sleep in menopause can increase irritability, anxiety, low mood, and brain fog.

What can I do about waking at 3 a.m. during menopause?

If you wake during menopause and cannot sleep, get up after about 20 minutes, do something calm elsewhere, and return when sleepy.

Can menopausal insomnia improve even while hot flushes continue?

Yes. CBT for insomnia can improve menopausal sleep within weeks, even while hot flushes and other symptoms continue.

Should I see a doctor about insomnia during menopause?

Yes. A physician can check for sleep apnea and other causes and discuss treatments for insomnia during menopause.
