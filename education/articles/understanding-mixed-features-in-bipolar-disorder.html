Understanding Mixed Features in Bipolar Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Mixed states can carry a higher risk of suicide because low mood is combined with agitation and impulsivity. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 if you are in immediate danger.

Mixed features describe a mood episode in which symptoms of depression and symptoms of mania or hypomania occur at the same time, or in rapid alternation within the same period. Someone might feel hopeless and worthless while also restless, agitated, sleepless, and full of racing thoughts. Mixed features can occur in bipolar I, bipolar II, and sometimes in depression. They are often distressing and confusing, and they are important to recognize because they can change treatment and increase risk.

What Mixed Features Look Like

  • Low mood or despair combined with racing thoughts and fast speech.
  • Feeling exhausted but unable to sleep, with high energy or agitation at night.
  • Irritability, anger, or a short fuse alongside hopelessness.
  • Restlessness, pacing, or feeling unable to settle.
  • Impulsive decisions while feeling low, such as spending or risky behaviour.
  • Rapid shifts in mood within a day.

At home, loved ones may notice someone who seems both miserable and wired. At work, concentration and relationships may suffer. People often describe it as feeling trapped inside a body that will not slow down while their mind feels dark.

Why It Happens

Bipolar disorder involves disruptions in the brain systems that regulate mood, energy, sleep, and activity. In mixed states, these systems appear to be dysregulated in opposite directions at once. Sleep loss, stress, substance use, and some medications, including antidepressants used without mood stabilizers in people with bipolar disorder, can contribute. Mixed features are also more common during transitions between episodes and in people with more frequent mood episodes.

What It Is Not

  • Not simply a bad mood with anxiety. Anxiety and depression often coexist, but mixed features involve clear manic or hypomanic symptoms such as decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, or racing thoughts.
  • Not the same as rapid mood swings in daily life. Emotional shifts that happen within hours in response to relationships, described on the page about emotions that feel intense and fast-changing, point more toward emotion regulation patterns.
  • Not ADHD alone. Restlessness and racing thoughts can occur in ADHD, but ADHD is lifelong rather than episodic.
  • Not a sign that treatment has failed. Mixed features are a recognized part of bipolar disorder and can be addressed.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A physician, psychiatrist, or psychologist assesses mixed features by exploring current symptoms, past episodes, sleep, and family history, often using mood charts. The page on dramatic mood swings explains how bipolar episodes are distinguished from other mood changes. Treatment usually involves medication prescribed and monitored by a physician or psychiatrist, and antidepressants may be used cautiously or avoided. Psychological support focuses on safety planning, stabilizing sleep and routine, mood tracking, reducing substance use, and early warning signs. Family involvement can help loved ones recognize changes early.

If you notice mixed symptoms, it is important to tell your prescriber promptly, since medication adjustments may be needed.

Common Questions About Mixed Features in Bipolar Disorder

What does it mean when a doctor says my episode has mixed features?

Mixed features means symptoms of depression and symptoms of mania or hypomania are present during the same episode, such as hopelessness combined with agitation and racing thoughts.

What does a mixed episode feel like?

People often describe feeling dark, hopeless, or worthless while also restless, wired, irritable, and unable to sleep, which can be exhausting and frightening.

Why are mixed states considered higher risk?

The combination of low mood with energy, agitation, and impulsivity can increase the risk of self-harm and suicide, so prompt support and safety planning are important.

Do mixed states only happen in bipolar I?

Yes. Mixed features can occur in bipolar I, bipolar II, and sometimes in depressive episodes without a bipolar diagnosis.

Can antidepressants trigger a mixed state?

In some people with bipolar disorder, antidepressants without a mood stabilizer can trigger hypomania, mania, or mixed features, which is why prescribers monitor closely.

How is a mixed episode different from anxiety and depression together?

Anxiety with depression involves worry and tension. Mixed features include manic or hypomanic signs such as decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, or racing thoughts.

Does lack of sleep make mixed features worse?

Yes. Sleep loss can trigger or worsen mood episodes, including mixed states. Protecting sleep is a key part of managing bipolar disorder.

What should I do if I notice mixed symptoms?

Contact your prescriber promptly, use your safety plan, reduce stimulation and substances, and involve trusted people. If you are at risk, call or text 9-8-8 or call 911.

How is mixed features treated?

Treatment usually involves medication prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist, along with therapy focused on safety, sleep, routine, and early warning signs.

Can mood tracking help spot mixed episodes early?

Yes. Tracking mood, sleep, energy, and irritability daily can reveal early changes, helping you and your care team respond sooner.
