What Is Emotional Regulation

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Emotional regulation is the set of skills that help people notice, understand, and manage their emotions. It includes being able to recognize what you feel, tolerate uncomfortable emotions, calm down after being upset, and express feelings in ways that fit the situation. Emotional regulation does not mean controlling or eliminating emotions. It means having some influence over how intense they become, how long they last, and how you act on them. Everyone struggles with regulation at times, especially when tired or stressed. For some people, emotions feel intense and hard to manage most of the time. Emotional regulation skills can be learned and strengthened throughout life.

What Emotional Dysregulation Looks Like

  • Emotions that feel too big to handle or shift quickly.
  • Difficulty calming down after being upset.
  • Reacting strongly and later regretting it.
  • Outbursts of anger, tears, or panic.
  • Shutting down, going numb, or withdrawing.
  • Using food, alcohol, spending, or scrolling to escape feelings.
  • Difficulty identifying what you are feeling.
  • Avoiding situations because of how they might make you feel.

Dysregulation can look like too much emotion, such as explosive reactions, or too little, such as numbness. Both can reflect difficulty managing emotional intensity.

Why It Happens

Emotional regulation develops through childhood as caregivers help children name feelings, soothe distress, and learn coping. When caregivers were consistently responsive, children gradually internalize these skills. When emotions were dismissed, punished, or overwhelming at home, children may not have learned how to manage them. Temperament also matters, as some people are born with more reactive emotional systems.

Several conditions are linked with emotional dysregulation, including ADHD, autism, PTSD, depression, anxiety, and borderline personality disorder, described on the page about emotional intensity and BPD. Stress, lack of sleep, hunger, pain, and hormonal changes can lower anyone's capacity to regulate. The idea of the window of tolerance describes the zone in which people can manage emotions effectively, and this zone can shrink under stress or after trauma. Relying on strategies such as emotional suppression can seem to work in the short term but often makes emotions harder to manage over time.

Emotional regulation also happens between people. Children first learn to calm down through co-regulation, when a caring adult helps them settle. Adults continue to regulate with the help of others, for example by talking with a friend or being comforted by a partner. Healthy relationships are an important resource for managing emotions, not a sign of weakness.

Regulation looks different across ages and situations. A toddler may need a hug to calm down, a teen may need space and a trusted adult to talk to, and an adult may need a walk or time to think. Knowing what helps you personally, and planning for it, is an important part of building these skills.

What It Is Not

  • Not suppressing or ignoring feelings. Healthy regulation involves acknowledging emotions, not pushing them away.
  • Not never feeling upset. Everyone experiences strong emotions, and that is normal.
  • Not a fixed trait. Regulation skills can be learned at any age.
  • Not a sign of weakness when it is hard. Many factors affect how easily emotions can be managed.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Many approaches teach regulation skills. Dialectical behaviour therapy offers structured skills in mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps people notice how thoughts shape emotions and practise reappraisal, looking at a situation from a different angle. Acceptance and commitment therapy teaches making room for emotions while acting on values. Everyday strategies include naming emotions, slow breathing, grounding through the senses, taking a pause before reacting, physical activity, and protecting sleep. Building a sense of safety and support in relationships helps too. With practice, many people find emotions become more manageable and less frightening.

Common Questions About Emotional Regulation

Does managing emotions mean never feeling upset?

No. Emotional regulation is the ability to notice, understand, and manage emotions, including how intense they become and how you act on them.

Why do my reactions feel so much bigger than other people's?

This may reflect emotional dysregulation, which is difficulty managing emotional intensity, leading to strong reactions, trouble calming down, or shutting down.

Should I try to control or hide strong feelings?

Not usually. Healthy regulation involves acknowledging emotions and influencing how you respond, rather than suppressing or eliminating them.

What conditions are linked with emotional dysregulation?

ADHD, autism, PTSD, depression, anxiety, and borderline personality disorder are commonly linked with dysregulation.

Why do I calm down faster when someone I trust is with me?

This is co-regulation, calming with the help of another person, such as a parent soothing a child or friends comforting each other.

How can I calm down quickly when upset?

Slow breathing, grounding through the senses, stepping away briefly, and naming the emotion can help.

Can changing how I see a situation change how I feel?

Yes. Reappraisal means looking at a situation from a different angle, which can change how intensely you feel about it.

How does DBT teach emotional regulation?

DBT offers structured skills in mindfulness, emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and interpersonal effectiveness.

Why is it harder to manage emotions when I am tired?

Sleep loss, hunger, and stress reduce the brain's capacity to regulate emotions, making reactions stronger.

Can adults learn emotional regulation skills?

Yes. Regulation skills can be learned and strengthened at any age with practice and support.
