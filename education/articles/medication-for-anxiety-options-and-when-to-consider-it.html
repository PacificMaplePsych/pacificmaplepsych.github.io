Medication for Anxiety: Options and When to Consider It

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

This page offers general information only. Decisions about starting, changing, or stopping any medication are made with a physician or psychiatrist. Do not stop a prescribed medication suddenly without medical advice. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or symptoms that feel new or severe, seek medical care or call 911.

Medication for anxiety is prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist and may be considered when anxiety is moderate to severe, long-lasting, significantly interferes with daily life, or has not improved enough with therapy alone. The most commonly prescribed options for anxiety disorders are certain antidepressant medications, which are taken daily and work gradually over several weeks. Other medications are sometimes used for short-term relief or specific situations. Medication does not cure anxiety, but it can reduce symptoms enough to make daily life and therapy more manageable. Many people do best with a combination of medication and therapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy.

The Main Types of Anxiety Medication

Antidepressants. Certain antidepressants are the most common first-line medications for generalized anxiety, panic disorder, social anxiety, and related conditions. Despite the name, they are prescribed for anxiety as well as depression. They usually take two to six weeks to start helping, with fuller benefits over a few months.

Short-term or as-needed medications. Some fast-acting medications may be prescribed for short periods or specific situations, such as severe panic. Because some of these can cause dependence with regular use, physicians generally use them cautiously and for limited periods.

Medications for physical symptoms. Some medications that reduce physical symptoms such as a racing heart or trembling are occasionally used for performance-related anxiety.

Other options. For some people, other medications may be considered if first-line options have not helped. These decisions are made by a physician or psychiatrist based on individual circumstances.

When Medication May Be Considered

Medication is often considered when:

  • Anxiety is severe or persistent and makes work, relationships, or daily tasks very hard.
  • Panic attacks are frequent or lead to avoiding many situations, as explained on the page about panic attacks and panic disorder.
  • Anxiety occurs alongside depression.
  • Therapy has not helped enough on its own, or access to therapy is limited.
  • Someone prefers to try medication, alone or with therapy.

For mild to moderate anxiety, therapy is usually recommended first. Cognitive behavioural therapy has strong evidence for anxiety disorders and teaches skills that last. A clear assessment, described on the page about how anxiety is diagnosed, helps guide the choice.

What to Discuss With Your Doctor

  • Your main symptoms and how they affect your life.
  • Other health conditions and medications, including supplements.
  • Alcohol, cannabis, and other substance use.
  • Pregnancy, plans for pregnancy, or breastfeeding.
  • Past experiences with medication, including side effects.
  • Any history of bipolar disorder or elevated mood in you or your family.
  • Your preferences and concerns, including worries about side effects or dependence.

What to Expect

Physicians often start with a low dose and adjust gradually. Some people feel a little more anxious or unsettled in the first week or two before benefits appear, and it helps to know this in advance. Side effects vary but can include stomach upset, sleep changes, or headaches, and many ease over time. Follow-up appointments check how the medication is working. If one medication does not help or causes troublesome side effects, another may be tried.

When anxiety has improved and stayed stable for a while, a physician may discuss gradually reducing medication. Stopping suddenly can cause discontinuation symptoms, so changes should always be guided by a doctor. Healthy habits, described on the page about lifestyle changes that reduce anxiety, support any treatment. The types of anxiety disorders are explained on the page about anxiety disorders.

Common Questions About Anxiety Medication

What medications are commonly prescribed for anxiety disorders?

Certain antidepressants are the most common first-line medications for anxiety disorders, with other options used in specific situations.

Why would my doctor prescribe an antidepressant for anxiety?

Certain antidepressants are effective for anxiety disorders as well as depression and are often the first medication considered for anxiety.

How long does anxiety medication take to start working?

Daily anxiety medications such as antidepressants usually take two to six weeks to start helping, with fuller benefits over a few months.

When should I consider medication for my anxiety?

Medication may be considered when anxiety is severe, persistent, interferes with daily life, occurs with depression, or has not improved with therapy alone.

Should I try CBT before asking my doctor about anxiety medication?

For mild to moderate anxiety, CBT is usually recommended first. Many people with more severe anxiety benefit from both therapy and medication.

Can anxiety medication make me feel worse at first?

Some people feel slightly more anxious or unsettled in the first week or two of anxiety medication before benefits appear.

Are fast-acting anxiety medications safe to take every day?

Some fast-acting anxiety medications can cause dependence with regular use, so physicians generally prescribe them cautiously and for limited periods.

Why might my doctor ask about past highs in mood before prescribing for anxiety?

A history of elevated mood can change which anxiety medication is safest, so doctors ask about it along with other health conditions, medications, substance use, and pregnancy plans.

Can I stop my anxiety medication when I feel better?

Do not stop anxiety medication suddenly. A physician can guide a gradual reduction when anxiety has been stable for a while.

Do I still need therapy if I take anxiety medication?

Medication can reduce anxiety symptoms, while therapy teaches skills that last, so many people benefit from both.
