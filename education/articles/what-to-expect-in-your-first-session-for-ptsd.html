What to Expect in Your First Session for PTSD

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency. If you are experiencing violence at home, see the abuse resources page.

In your first therapy session for PTSD, the therapist will focus on getting to know you, understanding your current symptoms, such as flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance, feeling on edge, or changes in mood, and making sure you feel as safe as possible. You will usually not be asked to describe the trauma in detail. The therapist may ask a general question about what happened, and you can share as much or as little as you choose. They will explain how PTSD works, discuss treatment options, and agree on goals with you. A trauma-informed therapist will move at your pace and check in about how you are feeling throughout the session.

What to Expect

  1. Introductions and consent. Confidentiality, its limits, and how the therapist works.
  2. Current symptoms. Intrusive memories, nightmares, avoidance, startle responses, sleep, concentration, and mood. A standardized questionnaire may be used.
  3. A general sense of the trauma. Usually just enough to understand the type of event, without details.
  4. Safety and stability. Questions about current safety, self-harm, substance use, and support.
  5. Impact on daily life. Work, relationships, and activities you have stopped doing.
  6. Explaining PTSD and treatment. Why symptoms persist and how therapy can help.
  7. Grounding skills. The therapist may teach a simple technique to use if you feel overwhelmed.

Practical Details

Treatment approaches. Trauma-focused therapies such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, EMDR, and trauma-focused CBT have strong evidence, as described on the pages about cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure and how PTSD is treated.

Pacing. Trauma processing usually begins after a few sessions of assessment, education, and coping skills. For complex trauma, more time may be spent on stabilization first, as noted on the page about complex PTSD.

Control. You can pause, take a break, or say no to a question at any time. A trauma-informed therapist will respect this, as explained on the page about trauma-informed therapy.

Virtual or in person. Both can work well. Some people feel safer at home, while others prefer a separate space.

Medication. Some medications can help with PTSD symptoms and are prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist.

Funding. Fees and coverage vary. WorkSafeBC, ICBC, or crime victim assistance programs may fund therapy in some situations.

How to Prepare

  • Note your main symptoms. And how they affect your daily life.
  • Decide what you are comfortable sharing. It is okay to say "I am not ready to talk about that yet."
  • Plan for comfort. Bring water or a grounding object, and sit where you feel safest.
  • Arrange support afterward. Plan something calming or have someone to talk to.
  • Write down questions. About the therapist's training, approach, and what to expect.
  • Be gentle with yourself. Symptoms may feel stirred up for a day or two, which is common.

Common Questions About the First PTSD Therapy Session

What does a trauma therapist focus on at the very first PTSD appointment?

In the first PTSD session, the therapist asks about current symptoms, safety, and daily life, explains PTSD and treatment, and agrees on goals with you.

Will I be asked to describe the traumatic event in detail right away?

No. In a first PTSD session, you usually only share a general sense of the trauma, and you decide how much to say.

When does trauma processing usually start in PTSD therapy?

Trauma processing usually starts after a few sessions of assessment, education, and coping skills, and later for complex trauma.

Can I stop or take a break during a PTSD session?

Yes. You can pause, take a break, or decline a question at any time in a PTSD session, and a trauma-informed therapist will respect this.

What therapies work best for PTSD?

Cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, EMDR, and trauma-focused CBT have strong evidence for treating PTSD.

Is it normal to feel worse after a first PTSD session?

Yes. PTSD symptoms may feel stirred up for a day or two after a first session, which is common. Planning calming activities can help.

What is a grounding skill for PTSD?

A grounding skill for PTSD is a simple technique, such as noticing your surroundings or slow breathing, that helps you feel present when overwhelmed.

Is virtual therapy effective for PTSD?

Yes. Virtual therapy can be effective for PTSD, and some people feel safer attending trauma sessions from home.

Who might pay for PTSD therapy in BC?

Depending on the situation, WorkSafeBC, ICBC, crime victim assistance programs, or extended health benefits may help pay for PTSD therapy in BC.

What helps people with PTSD feel ready for a first trauma therapy visit?

Note your PTSD symptoms, decide what you are comfortable sharing, bring something grounding, and plan support for after your first appointment.
