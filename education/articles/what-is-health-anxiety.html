What Is Health Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

New, severe, or changing physical symptoms, such as chest pain, sudden weakness, trouble breathing, or a severe headache, should always be checked by a doctor. Call 911 in an emergency.

Health anxiety is persistent, excessive worry about having or developing a serious illness. People with health anxiety often notice normal body sensations, such as a headache, a skipped heartbeat, or a mole, and fear they signal something dangerous. They may check their bodies, search online, or seek repeated reassurance from doctors, which brings only brief relief. Clinically, health anxiety often corresponds to illness anxiety disorder, or to somatic symptom disorder when physical symptoms are prominent. Health anxiety can be exhausting and isolating, but it is very treatable.

What Health Anxiety Looks Like

  • Constant worry that you are seriously ill, despite reassurance.
  • Frequently checking your body, such as feeling for lumps or taking your pulse.
  • Googling symptoms and finding the worst possible explanations.
  • Seeking reassurance from doctors, family, or friends, then doubting it.
  • Avoiding doctors, hospitals, or health news out of fear.
  • Interpreting ordinary sensations as signs of disease.
  • Worry that jumps from one illness to another.
  • Feeling anxious after hearing about someone else's illness.
  • Difficulty enjoying life because of preoccupation with health.

Why It Happens

Health anxiety often begins after a personal or family health scare, the illness or death of someone close, or a period of stress. Childhood experiences with serious illness, either one's own or a family member's, can increase vulnerability. People with a tendency toward anxiety, a heightened awareness of body sensations, or difficulty tolerating uncertainty are more likely to develop health anxiety.

Several processes keep health anxiety going. Paying close attention to the body makes normal sensations feel more noticeable and alarming. Anxiety itself causes physical symptoms, such as a racing heart, tension, and stomach upset, which can then seem like evidence of illness. Thinking patterns such as catastrophizing turn small concerns into frightening possibilities. Checking, searching, and reassurance-seeking reduce anxiety briefly but prevent learning that uncertainty is tolerable, so worry returns.

Online health information can make health anxiety worse. Search results often list rare but serious conditions alongside common explanations, and it is easy to focus on the most frightening possibilities. Some people spend hours researching, which increases rather than reduces worry.

Health anxiety can also affect relationships. Loved ones may tire of repeated reassurance, or feel worried and frustrated in turn. Some families unintentionally strengthen the cycle by answering every question or accompanying the person to many appointments. Learning together how health anxiety works can help everyone respond in ways that support recovery.

What It Is Not

  • Not imagined concern. The fear and distress are real.
  • Not a reason to avoid medical care. Regular care with one trusted provider is recommended.
  • Not solved by more tests. Reassurance from testing often fades quickly.
  • Not the same as somatic symptom disorder, though they overlap. The page on health anxiety and somatic symptom disorder explains the difference.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about health worries, checking and reassurance behaviours, avoidance, and how daily life is affected. A physician can ensure appropriate medical care and help coordinate a plan that avoids unnecessary testing. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most effective treatment. It helps people understand the anxiety cycle, reduce checking and searching, test catastrophic beliefs, and build tolerance for uncertainty. Mindfulness-based approaches can help people notice sensations without reacting with fear. Scheduling regular check-ins with a trusted doctor, rather than seeking care whenever anxiety spikes, can reduce reassurance-seeking. Medication may help some people, particularly when anxiety is severe.

Common Questions About Health Anxiety

How do I know if my health worries have become health anxiety?

When worry about illness is persistent, excessive, and leads to checking, searching, or avoidance that affects daily life.

Is health anxiety the same as hypochondria?

Hypochondria is an older term. Today, clinicians use terms such as illness anxiety disorder or health anxiety.

Why do clear test results only calm me for a day or two?

Reassurance eases worry briefly, but anxiety returns and often shifts to a new concern, keeping the cycle going.

Does searching symptoms online make health anxiety worse?

Often, yes. Search results can highlight rare, serious conditions, which increases fear rather than easing it.

Could my racing heart be caused by the worry itself?

Often, yes. Anxiety causes real sensations such as a racing heart or stomach upset, which can feed health worries.

Why did my fear of illness start after a friend got sick?

Someone else's illness, a personal health scare, stressful periods, and news about disease are common triggers for health anxiety.

What helps reduce feeling for lumps or taking my pulse?

Notice when you check, gradually reduce how often, and use therapy to build tolerance for uncertainty.

How often should I see a doctor when health worries are high?

Regular, planned visits with one trusted provider support appropriate care while reducing reassurance-seeking.

What can loved ones say instead of constant reassurance?

Offer empathy, avoid repeated reassurance, and encourage the person to seek therapy for the anxiety.

What treatment works best for health anxiety?

Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most effective treatment, often combined with mindfulness and coordinated medical care.
