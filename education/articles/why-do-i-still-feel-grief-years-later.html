Why Do I Still Feel Grief Years Later

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

It has been years since they died. Most days you get on with life, and people around you assume you have moved on. Then a song, a smell, a date on the calendar, or a moment you wish they could see brings it all back. Sometimes the grief feels almost as fresh as it did at the start, and you wonder if something is wrong with you for still hurting this much.

Grief does not follow a set timeline. For most people, it changes shape over time rather than disappearing completely. Still feeling grief years later is usually a reflection of how much the person mattered.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Ongoing bonds. Many people keep an inner connection with someone who has died. Missing them and thinking of them is part of that continuing relationship, not a failure to let go.

Anniversaries and milestones. Birthdays, holidays, weddings, graduations, and the anniversary of the death can bring grief back strongly, as can reaching the age the person was when they died.

New losses. A new loss or major life change can stir up older grief that had settled.

Grief that was put on hold. If you had to stay strong for others, return to work quickly, or could not grieve fully at the time, feelings may surface later.

Depression. Grief can lead to depression, especially if you feel hopeless about the future or have lost interest in most things.

Trauma. A sudden, violent, or traumatic death can leave distressing memories that keep grief raw, sometimes with difficulty stopping thoughts about the past.

When It Looks More Like Prolonged Grief

Prolonged grief disorder is a recognized condition in which grief remains intense and disabling long after a loss, generally a year or more for adults. It affects a minority of bereaved people and is different from the ordinary waves of grief that continue for years.

Signs of prolonged grief include:

  • Intense longing or preoccupation with the person most days.
  • Difficulty accepting that the death happened.
  • Feeling that part of yourself died with them, or that life has no meaning.
  • Avoiding reminders of the loss, or being unable to engage with life.
  • Intense emotional pain, such as bitterness, anger, or guilt, that does not ease.
  • Emotional numbness or feeling alone and detached from others.

The difference between prolonged grief and ordinary lasting grief is not how long you miss the person, but whether grief is preventing you from living your life.

What Can Help

  • Expect waves. Knowing grief can return around dates and events helps you plan and be gentle with yourself.
  • Create rituals. Marking anniversaries with a meaningful activity can honour the person and give grief a place.
  • Talk about them. Sharing memories with people who knew them keeps the connection alive.
  • Allow joy alongside grief. Enjoying life does not betray the person you lost.
  • Seek support if stuck. If grief is keeping you from living, specialized support can help.

Common Questions About Long-Lasting Grief

Is it normal to still grieve years after a death?

Yes. Many people continue to feel grief for years, especially around anniversaries and milestones. Grief usually changes shape over time rather than disappearing completely.

How is prolonged grief disorder different from grief that lasts for years?

Ordinary grief can last for years but still lets you live your life. Prolonged grief disorder is when grief remains intense and disabling, typically for a year or more after a loss in adults, with persistent longing, difficulty accepting the death, and trouble engaging with life.

Why does grief come back on anniversaries?

Anniversaries, birthdays, and holidays are strong reminders of the person and of the loss. It is common for grief to intensify around these times, even many years later.

What are continuing bonds in grief?

Continuing bonds is the idea that people often maintain an inner connection with someone who has died, through memories, rituals, or conversations. This is a healthy part of grief for many people.

Can old grief resurface after a new loss?

Yes. A new loss or major change can bring earlier grief back to the surface. Past losses that were not fully processed may feel especially strong.

Does moving forward years after a loss mean I have forgotten them?

No. Many people feel guilt when they notice joy or moving forward, as if it means forgetting. Enjoying life is not a betrayal and can coexist with love and grief.

How does traumatic loss affect how long grief lasts?

Sudden, violent, or traumatic deaths can combine grief with trauma symptoms, such as intrusive memories and avoidance, which can make grief more intense and longer lasting.

Can grief turn into depression years later?

Grief can lead to depression at any point, especially when it comes with hopelessness, loss of interest, and changes in sleep or appetite. Depression is treatable, even alongside grief.

Will the pain of grief ever go away?

For most people, the sharp pain of grief softens over time, though missing the person may continue. Many describe grief becoming something they carry rather than something that overwhelms them.

Is there treatment for prolonged grief?

Yes. Grief-focused therapies, including approaches designed specifically for prolonged grief, have good evidence. They help people process the loss, reduce avoidance, and reconnect with life.
