Why Do I Struggle to Read Facial Expressions

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Someone tells you afterward that your friend looked upset the whole evening, and you had no idea. A manager's expression during a meeting leaves you unsure whether they were pleased or annoyed. You find yourself watching faces carefully, trying to work out what people mean, and still missing things that others seem to pick up instantly. Sometimes you only realize later, when the situation has already moved on.

Reading faces is a skill most people use without thinking, so struggling with it can feel confusing and isolating. There are several possible reasons, and understanding which one fits can make social life feel much less like guesswork.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Anxiety. When you are anxious in social situations, attention tends to turn inward, toward how you are coming across. That leaves less attention for the other person's face, and anxiety can also bias you toward seeing neutral expressions as disapproving.

Tiredness or overload. Reading expressions takes mental effort. When you are exhausted, stressed, or in a noisy, busy environment, subtle cues are easier to miss.

Vision or hearing changes. Uncorrected vision problems make fine facial details harder to see, and hearing loss can push attention toward decoding words rather than watching faces. A check with an eye doctor or audiologist can rule these out.

ADHD. Distractibility can mean glancing away at the moment an expression changes, so cues are missed rather than misread.

Trauma or difficult early relationships. People who grew up around unpredictable adults sometimes become very alert to anger but less confident reading other emotions, or they avoid looking closely at faces altogether.

Face blindness. Prosopagnosia is difficulty recognizing who someone is by their face. It is different from reading emotions, though the two can occur together.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Many autistic people find facial expressions harder to read intuitively. This is a difference in how social information is processed, not a lack of caring about others. Autistic people often care a great deal about getting social interactions right, which is one reason many rehearse conversations beforehand and replay them afterward.

Signs that autism may be part of the picture include:

  • The difficulty has been present since childhood, rather than appearing recently.
  • Looking at faces, especially eyes, feels uncomfortable or distracting, as described in Why Do I Struggle to Make Eye Contact.
  • You rely on words more than tone or expression, and sometimes take things more literally than intended.
  • You have learned to read faces through deliberate study and practice, which works but is tiring.
  • Social interactions leave you drained, even when they go well.
  • You also notice sensory sensitivities, a strong preference for routine, or deep focused interests.

Research also suggests that communication difficulties between autistic and non-autistic people go both ways. Many autistic people read each other well, while non-autistic people often misread autistic expressions too. The challenge is often a mismatch in styles rather than a one-sided deficit.

What Can Help

  • Ask directly. Simple questions like "How are you feeling about this?" or "Was that okay?" give you clear information and most people appreciate them.
  • Use other cues. Words, tone of voice, context, and what someone does afterward often carry as much information as a face.
  • Tell people close to you. Letting friends or a partner know that you may miss unspoken signals invites them to say things out loud.
  • Reduce the load. Quieter settings, one-on-one conversations, and breaks between social events leave more capacity for reading others.
  • Check the anxious interpretation. If you tend to see disapproval everywhere, ask yourself what else an expression could mean.

Common Questions About Reading Facial Expressions

Why can't I tell what people are feeling from their faces?

Difficulty reading faces can come from anxiety, fatigue, vision or hearing issues, ADHD, past trauma, or autism. When it has been lifelong and comes with other social or sensory differences, an autism assessment may help clarify it.

Do autistic people have trouble reading facial expressions?

Many autistic people find it harder to read expressions intuitively and may rely more on words, context, or learned rules. This reflects a difference in processing, not a lack of empathy or interest in others.

Can social anxiety make me misread people's faces?

Yes. Social anxiety tends to direct attention inward and bias interpretation, so neutral or ambiguous faces are more likely to be read as critical or annoyed.

Do non-autistic people misread autistic facial expressions too?

Often, yes. Researchers call this the double empathy problem: misunderstandings between autistic and non-autistic people run both ways. Each group can find the other's expressions and communication harder to read, rather than the difficulty belonging to autistic people alone.

Is difficulty reading faces the same as face blindness?

No. Face blindness, or prosopagnosia, is difficulty recognizing who a person is from their face. Difficulty reading facial expressions involves understanding emotions. The two can occur together but are separate.

Can ADHD affect how well I read facial expressions?

ADHD can make it easier to miss expressions because attention shifts away at key moments. People with ADHD can usually read faces when focused, but may miss cues in fast or busy conversations.

Can you learn to read facial expressions better as an adult?

Many people improve through practice, feedback from trusted people, and paying attention to context and tone. It helps to combine this with asking directly when you are unsure, rather than relying on faces alone.

Why does looking at people's faces feel tiring?

For some people, especially autistic people, processing faces takes deliberate effort rather than happening automatically. Doing this through a whole conversation or day can be mentally exhausting.

How should I tell people I have trouble reading expressions?

A simple explanation works well, such as saying you sometimes miss unspoken cues and it helps when people say how they feel out loud. Most people respond positively to clear requests like this.

Would difficulty with facial expressions come up in an adult autism assessment?

Social communication, including how you read and use facial expressions, is one of the areas explored in an autism assessment, alongside sensory experiences, routines, interests, and developmental history.
