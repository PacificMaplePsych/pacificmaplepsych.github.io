How to Support an Anxious Child

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If your child talks about wanting to die or harm themselves, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) or call 911 in an emergency. Children and teens can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

Supporting an anxious child usually works best when parents stay calm, validate the child's feelings, encourage them to face fears in small steps, and gently reduce how much the family accommodates the anxiety. Anxiety is one of the most common mental health concerns in children. It can look like worry, clinginess, stomach aches, avoidance of school or activities, trouble sleeping, or meltdowns. It is natural for loving parents to want to remove anything that frightens their child, but avoiding fears often makes anxiety grow. Parents can help children learn that they are braver and more capable than their anxiety tells them.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

For an anxious child, the brain's alarm system goes off too easily. Situations that seem minor to adults, such as a new classroom, a birthday party, or being apart from a parent, can feel truly frightening. Children may not have words for anxiety and may express it through their bodies, such as headaches or nausea, or through behaviour, such as anger or refusing to go somewhere. The broader picture is described on the page about anxiety in children and teens.

Anxiety can run in families, and anxious parents may notice their own worries rising, as explored on the page about worrying about your child all the time. Children also learn from watching how adults handle fear, which means parents have real influence.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Stay calm and warm. Your calm presence helps your child's nervous system settle.
  • Validate feelings. Say "I can see you feel scared. That makes sense" before problem-solving.
  • Express confidence. Add "and I know you can handle this," which shows belief in their ability.
  • Encourage small brave steps. Break feared situations into manageable steps, such as staying at a party for ten minutes, then longer.
  • Praise bravery. Notice effort, not just outcomes.
  • Teach calming skills. Slow breathing, naming feelings, and grounding can help children ride out anxious moments.
  • Keep routines. Predictable sleep, meals, and activities support calm.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Constant reassurance. Answering the same worried question over and over can keep anxiety going. One calm answer, then a shift, often helps more.
  • Avoiding all fears. Letting children skip school or activities because of anxiety brings short-term relief but makes fears grow.
  • Dismissing feelings. Saying "there is nothing to be scared of" can leave children feeling misunderstood.
  • Pushing too hard too fast. Big leaps can overwhelm. Small steps build confidence.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting an anxious child can be stressful and draining, especially if you also experience anxiety. Watching your child struggle is hard. Looking after your own sleep, stress, and support helps you stay calm and steady. If your own anxiety is high, getting support for yourself can also help your child, since children often take cues from parents. Agreeing on a shared approach with co-parents or caregivers makes consistency easier.

Encouraging Professional Help

Consider professional support if anxiety is frequent or intense, lasts for months, interferes with school, friendships, sleep, or family life, or leads to frequent physical complaints without a medical cause. The page about signs your child may need mental health support can help. Cognitive behavioural therapy has strong evidence for childhood anxiety, and parent-focused programs, which teach parents to reduce accommodation and support brave behaviour, can be very effective, sometimes even without the child attending sessions. Practical guidance for parents is also described on the page about parent coaching. A family doctor can check for physical causes of symptoms. If your child talks about self-harm or suicide, seek help right away.

Common Questions About Supporting an Anxious Child

What daily habits help parents of an anxious child?

Stay calm, validate your child's feelings, express confidence in them, encourage small brave steps, and teach simple calming skills.

Should I keep reassuring my child when they are anxious?

Constant reassurance can keep child anxiety going. One calm answer followed by a shift in focus often helps more.

Is it okay to let my anxious child skip things they fear?

Avoidance brings short-term relief but often makes a child's fears grow. Small, supported brave steps help more.

What should I say to my child when they feel anxious?

Validate first, such as saying you can see they feel scared, then add that you know they can handle it.

Are stomach aches a common sign of anxiety in kids?

Anxiety often shows up in children's bodies as stomach aches or headaches. A doctor can rule out other causes.

Does a parent modelling calm help an anxious child?

Children learn from how adults handle fear, so getting support for your own anxiety can also help your child.

Which family routines can accidentally feed a child's anxiety?

Accommodation means changing family routines to avoid a child's fears. Gradually reducing it is a key part of treating child anxiety.

Can an anxious child improve if only the parents attend sessions?

Yes. Programs that teach parents to reduce accommodation and support brave behaviour can be very effective for child anxiety.

What signs suggest an anxious child should see a therapist?

If a child's anxiety is intense, lasts months, or interferes with school, friendships, sleep, or family life, professional support can help.

What therapy works best for anxious children?

Cognitive behavioural therapy has strong evidence for childhood anxiety, often with parents involved in the work.
