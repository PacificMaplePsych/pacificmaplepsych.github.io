What Is a Treatment Plan

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

A treatment plan is a shared outline, created by you and your therapist, that describes what you want to work on, the goals you are aiming for, the approaches you will use, and how you will tell whether things are improving. It usually develops over the first few sessions after an initial assessment and is reviewed and updated as therapy goes on. Treatment plans can be formal written documents or more informal agreements, depending on the therapist and setting. Either way, they help make therapy focused, collaborative, and easier to evaluate.

How It Works

Creating a treatment plan usually follows these steps:

  1. Assessment. In the first session or two, the therapist learns about your concerns, history, current situation, and strengths. The page about initial consultations describes this early stage.
  2. Clarifying concerns. Together you identify the main problems to address, such as panic attacks, conflict at home, or low mood.
  3. Setting goals. You agree on specific, meaningful goals, such as "attend social events without leaving early," "sleep through the night most nights," or "argue with my partner without shouting."
  4. Choosing approaches. The therapist suggests methods suited to your goals, such as CBT, exposure work, emotion-focused therapy, or skills training, and explains why.
  5. Deciding how to measure progress. This may include questionnaires, tracking specific behaviours, or regular check-ins about how things are going.
  6. Reviewing and updating. Plans are revisited every few weeks or months and adjusted as goals are met or priorities change.

Practical Details

What a plan usually includes. Main concerns, goals, approaches, frequency of sessions, how progress will be measured, and sometimes other supports such as medication through a physician, groups, or family involvement.

Your role. A good treatment plan is collaborative. Your preferences, values, and practical limits shape it. You can ask questions, suggest changes, or disagree.

Formal versus informal plans. Some settings require written plans, while in others the plan is discussed in conversation and noted in records. You can ask your therapist to write down goals if that helps you.

Plans for crises. If safety is a concern, a plan may include steps for difficult moments, such as a safety plan and crisis contacts.

Plans change. New concerns can come up, and goals may shift. A plan is a guide, not a contract.

Plans also help you judge timelines and progress, as discussed on the pages about how long therapy usually takes and how to know if therapy is working.

How to Prepare

  • Think about what you want to be different. Imagine life when therapy has helped. What would you be doing more or less of?
  • Be specific. Concrete goals, such as "go to the grocery store alone," are easier to track than broad ones such as "feel better."
  • Share practical limits. Budget, schedule, and preferences about approach matter.
  • Ask questions. Ask why an approach is recommended and what it will involve.
  • Revisit regularly. Bring up goals during sessions, especially if something changes.

Common Questions About Treatment Plans

What does a therapy treatment plan include?

A treatment plan usually includes your main concerns, goals, the therapy approaches used, session frequency, and how progress will be measured.

Who creates the treatment plan in therapy?

You and your therapist create the treatment plan together, based on your concerns, goals, preferences, and practical limits.

When is a treatment plan made?

A treatment plan usually develops over the first few sessions, after an initial assessment of your concerns and history.

What makes a good goal in a treatment plan?

Specific, meaningful goals, such as going to the grocery store alone, are easier to track in a treatment plan than broad goals.

Can I change my treatment plan?

Yes. Treatment plans are reviewed and updated as goals are met, priorities shift, or new concerns arise.

Is a treatment plan always written down?

Some treatment plans are formal written documents, while others are discussed and noted in records. You can ask for written goals.

How is progress measured in a treatment plan?

Progress may be measured with questionnaires, tracking specific behaviours, or regular check-ins about treatment plan goals.

Can a treatment plan include medication or other supports?

Yes. A treatment plan can include medication through a physician, groups, family involvement, or other supports alongside therapy.

Does a treatment plan include what to do in a crisis?

If safety is a concern, a treatment plan may include a safety plan and crisis contacts for difficult moments.

What should I think about before making a treatment plan?

Consider what you want to be different, make goals specific, and share practical limits such as budget and schedule for your treatment plan.
