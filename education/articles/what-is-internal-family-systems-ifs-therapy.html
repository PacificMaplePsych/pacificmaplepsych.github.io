What Is Internal Family Systems (IFS) Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Internal Family Systems (IFS) therapy is an approach that views the mind as made up of different parts, such as a critical part, an anxious part, or a part that carries old hurt, along with a core Self that is naturally calm, curious, and compassionate. Rather than trying to get rid of difficult feelings, IFS helps people get to know their parts, understand what each is trying to do, and heal the painful experiences some parts carry. IFS was developed by psychologist Richard Schwartz in the 1980s out of family systems therapy. Research on IFS is promising but still early, and many people find its non-judgmental approach to inner conflict helpful.

How IFS Therapy Works

IFS is based on the idea that it is normal to have many parts, and that each part has a positive intention, even when its behaviour causes problems. Most people already talk this way, as in "part of me wants to go, but part of me wants to stay home." IFS describes three broad types of parts:

  • Exiles are parts that carry pain, shame, or fear from past experiences, often from childhood. Other parts try to keep them out of awareness.
  • Managers are protective parts that try to keep life under control and prevent pain, for example through planning, perfectionism, people-pleasing, or self-criticism.
  • Firefighters are protective parts that react when pain breaks through, often with urgent behaviours such as bingeing, numbing, or lashing out.

IFS also describes the Self, a core that is not a part, marked by qualities such as calm, curiosity, compassion, clarity, and confidence. In therapy, the goal is to help the Self lead, so that parts feel understood and can relax their extreme roles. When protective parts trust the Self, people can safely approach exiles, witness their pain, and help them let go of burdens they have carried. The approach fits well with a trauma-informed way of working, since protectors are never forced aside.

What Sessions Are Like

An IFS session often begins with noticing a feeling, thought, or body sensation related to a current difficulty. The therapist helps you focus on it as a part and get curious about it, for example by asking what it wants you to know, what it is afraid would happen if it stopped, and how old it seems. Many people describe parts visually or as sensations in the body. Sessions are usually conversational and exploratory, with moments of quiet inner focus, often with eyes closed if that feels comfortable.

IFS is usually practised without trance, but parts work is also a long-standing technique in clinical hypnotherapy, and many hypnotherapists integrate IFS-style parts work into their sessions. The related approach is explained on the page about hypnotherapy. The pace is guided by your parts. If a protector is not ready to let you approach a painful memory, the therapist respects that and gets to know the protector first. The length of therapy varies, from a few months for a specific concern to longer-term work for complex trauma.

Who It Can Help

IFS is used for anxiety, depression, trauma, complex PTSD, shame, self-criticism, relationship patterns, and inner conflict. Early studies, including small trials, have shown promising results for conditions such as depression and PTSD, but research is still limited, and more rigorous studies are needed. Many people are drawn to IFS because it treats every part of them with respect, including the parts they feel ashamed of.

IFS may be less suitable for people who want a highly structured, skills-based approach, and for some conditions, such as OCD or severe eating disorders, other treatments have stronger evidence and may be needed first or alongside IFS. People in crisis or with severe dissociation may need stabilization before deeper parts work.

Common Questions About IFS Therapy

What does parts mean in Internal Family Systems therapy?

In IFS, parts are different sides of your personality, such as a critical part or an anxious part, each with its own feelings and positive intention.

What are exiles, managers, and firefighters in IFS?

In IFS, exiles carry old pain, managers try to prevent pain through control, and firefighters react urgently when pain breaks through.

What is the Self in IFS therapy?

In IFS, the Self is a calm, curious, compassionate core that is not a part and can help parts feel understood and heal.

Who developed Internal Family Systems therapy?

IFS was developed by psychologist Richard Schwartz in the 1980s, drawing on family systems therapy.

How strong is the evidence for Internal Family Systems therapy so far?

Early IFS research, including small trials for depression and PTSD, is promising, but the evidence is still limited and more studies are needed.

Is IFS the same as hypnotherapy?

No. IFS is usually practised without trance, but IFS-style parts work is often integrated into hypnotherapy, where parts work has a long history.

What happens in a typical IFS session?

You notice a feeling or sensation, focus on it as a part, and get curious about what it wants and fears, guided by the IFS therapist.

Will IFS therapy force me to relive painful memories?

No. IFS respects protective parts and only approaches painful memories when those parts feel ready.

Can IFS help with a harsh inner critic?

Yes. IFS helps you understand what your critical part is trying to protect, so it can soften its extreme role.

Who might need a different approach than IFS?

People wanting structured skills training, or those with OCD or severe eating disorders, may benefit more from other treatments first or alongside IFS.
