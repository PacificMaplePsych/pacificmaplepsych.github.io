Why Do I Feel Caught Between Two Cultures

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Feeling caught between two cultures usually means you are navigating different sets of values, expectations, and ways of belonging, often those of your family's heritage and those of the wider society you live in. Many people who immigrated, grew up as children of immigrants, or live in multicultural families describe feeling not fully at home in either world. This experience is common and can be a source of richness and strength, but it can also bring stress, loneliness, and conflict, particularly around family expectations, relationships, and identity.

People describe speaking one way at home and another at work or school, feeling judged by family for being too Canadian and by peers for being too traditional, or feeling like a translator, not just of language, but of worlds.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Different family expectations. Values around career, marriage, independence, and caring for parents can differ sharply between cultures, putting people in the middle.

Language gaps. Being more fluent in one language than your parents or grandparents can create distance in how deeply you can share with them.

Discrimination and racism. Experiences of exclusion or stereotyping can make it harder to feel you belong in the wider culture.

Loneliness after moving. Newcomers often face isolation while building new networks, as described on the page about feeling lonely after moving to a new city.

Relationship choices. Partners from different backgrounds, or choices that differ from family hopes, can intensify the pull between cultures.

Shifting identity. People can move between identities depending on setting, and may feel like a different person depending on who they are with.

When It Looks More Like Acculturation Stress

Acculturation is the process of adapting to a new culture while holding onto aspects of one's original culture. The stress that can come with it is sometimes called acculturation stress, and it can affect newcomers as well as later generations. Signs that the pull between cultures is becoming heavy include:

  • Ongoing conflict with family about values, relationships, or life choices.
  • Guilt about disappointing parents, or resentment about their expectations.
  • A sense of not belonging anywhere, or not fitting in socially.
  • Feeling you must hide parts of yourself in each setting.
  • Stress, anxiety, or low mood linked to these tensions.
  • Exhaustion from constantly switching between cultural expectations.

Research suggests that people who are able to integrate both cultures, drawing on each rather than choosing one, often report better wellbeing. Integration does not mean resolving every tension. It means finding a sense of self that includes both backgrounds, on your own terms. Family members may be navigating their own losses and fears as well, which can help explain some of the pressure.

What Can Help

  • Name the tension. Recognizing that you are holding two sets of values can reduce self-blame.
  • Find your own blend. Consider which values from each culture matter most to you, rather than choosing one side.
  • Connect with others who understand. Friends, community groups, or online spaces with people of similar backgrounds can reduce isolation.
  • Communicate with family gradually. Explaining your choices with respect for their values, over time, often works better than one big confrontation.
  • Honour your heritage in your own way. Food, language, celebrations, and stories can keep you connected, even if your life looks different from your parents'.
  • Look after your wellbeing. Stress from cultural conflict is real and deserves care.

Common Questions About Feeling Caught Between Two Cultures

Why do I feel like I don't belong in either culture?

When you carry two sets of values and experiences, you may feel slightly different from people in each group. This is common for immigrants and children of immigrants and does not mean you do not belong.

What is acculturation stress?

Acculturation stress is the strain that can come from adapting to a new culture while maintaining ties to your original one, including language barriers, family conflict, and discrimination.

Is it common for children of immigrants to feel caught between cultures?

Yes. Children of immigrants often navigate family expectations shaped by one culture while growing up in another, which can create tension and questions about identity.

How do I handle conflict with my parents about cultural expectations?

Try to understand the values behind their expectations, share your perspective respectfully and gradually, and look for areas of compromise. Support from a therapist can help with difficult conversations.

Why do I feel guilty for choosing a different path than my family expected?

Guilt often reflects love and a wish not to disappoint family. It can coexist with knowing your choice is right for you. Exploring both feelings can bring some peace.

What does it mean to be bicultural?

Being bicultural means identifying with and navigating two cultures. Many bicultural people draw strength from both, though it can also involve stress and switching between norms.

Can discrimination make me feel like I don't fit into Canadian culture?

Yes. Experiences of racism and exclusion can make it harder to feel a sense of belonging, even for people born and raised in Canada.

How can I stay connected to my heritage while living a different life?

Language, food, traditions, stories, and community connections can keep heritage alive. You can choose the parts that feel meaningful to you.

Does code-switching between cultures affect mental health?

Code-switching is a valuable skill, but constantly adjusting to different expectations can be tiring and may leave people feeling they cannot be fully themselves.

Can therapy help with cultural identity struggles?

Yes. Therapy, particularly with a culturally responsive therapist, can help you explore identity, navigate family dynamics, and build a sense of self that includes both cultures.
