Why Autistic and ADHD Children Often Struggle With Sleep

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Sleep problems are much more common in autistic children and children with ADHD than in other children. They may take a long time to fall asleep, wake often during the night, wake very early, or seem to need less sleep than expected. Reasons include differences in the body's internal clock and melatonin production, sensory sensitivities, difficulty winding down, anxiety, and, for some children, the effects of ADHD medication. Poor sleep can in turn worsen attention, behaviour, and emotional regulation, creating a cycle. Approaches that work best are tailored to each child, combining predictable routines, sensory-friendly environments, and support for parents, with medical input when needed.

How Sleep Problems Can Look

  • Taking a long time to fall asleep, sometimes an hour or more
  • Frequent or long night wakings, sometimes with play or talking
  • Very early waking and difficulty returning to sleep
  • Resistance to bedtime routines or distress at transitions
  • Restlessness or needing to move before sleep
  • Daytime tiredness that shows up as hyperactivity or meltdowns rather than sleepiness

Why It Happens

Body Clock Differences

Some autistic children and children with ADHD produce melatonin later in the evening or in a less regular pattern, which shifts their natural sleep time later or makes it less stable.

Sensory Sensitivities

Noise, light, scratchy pyjamas, bedding textures, or temperature can make it hard to relax or stay asleep, as described on the page about sensory overload.

Busy Minds and Bodies

Children with ADHD may find it hard to switch off, with racing thoughts or a need to move. Many autistic children need extra time to process the day.

Transitions and Change

Moving from a preferred activity to bed is a transition, and transitions can be hard for many neurodivergent children. Changes in routine can disrupt sleep for days.

Anxiety

Anxiety is common in autistic children and children with ADHD and often peaks at bedtime, as described on the page about anxiety in children and teens.

Medication and Health

Stimulant medication can delay sleep for some children, and other health conditions, such as gastrointestinal problems, may affect sleep.

What Can Help

  • A predictable routine. Use the same steps in the same order each night, with a visual schedule if helpful, as described on the page about building an autism-friendly daily routine.
  • Transition warnings. Give countdowns or timers before bedtime starts.
  • Sensory-friendly sleep spaces. Consider blackout curtains, white noise, comfortable fabrics, weighted blankets if suitable, and a comfortable temperature.
  • Wind-down activities. Choose calm, preferred activities without screens in the last hour.
  • Movement earlier in the day. Physical activity and daylight support sleep.
  • Gradual changes. Make one change at a time and give it a few weeks.
  • Gentle, consistent responses to night waking, as described on the page about bedtime resistance and night waking.

Supporting Parents

Ongoing sleep disruption is exhausting for parents too. Sharing night duties where possible and asking for support helps parents stay patient and well.

Medical and Professional Support

A physician or paediatrician can check for medical causes, review medication timing, and discuss whether melatonin is appropriate, since it has more evidence for autistic children and children with ADHD than for children in general. An occupational therapist can help with sensory needs, as described on the page about occupational therapy for sensory processing. Psychologists can support parents with behavioural sleep strategies and address anxiety. If you suspect your child is autistic or has ADHD, an assessment can help you understand their needs, including sleep. The page about ADHD and sleep problems explains more about ADHD in particular.

Common Questions About Sleep in Autistic and ADHD Children

Why do autistic children have trouble sleeping?

Autistic children often have trouble sleeping because of body clock differences, sensory sensitivities, anxiety, and difficulty with transitions.

Why do children with ADHD struggle to fall asleep?

Children with ADHD may struggle to fall asleep because of a later body clock, a busy mind, restlessness, or stimulant medication.

Are sleep problems common in autism and ADHD?

Yes. Sleep problems are much more common in autistic children and children with ADHD than in other children.

Does melatonin help autistic children sleep?

Melatonin has evidence for helping some autistic children and children with ADHD fall asleep. Talk to a physician before using it.

Can ADHD medication affect sleep?

Yes. Stimulant ADHD medication can delay sleep for some children. A physician can review timing or dosage.

Do weighted blankets help children sleep?

Some children find weighted blankets calming, though research is mixed. Check safety guidance and suitability with a health professional.

How can I make bedtime easier for my autistic child?

Use a predictable visual routine, transition warnings, a sensory-friendly room, and calm activities to make bedtime easier for an autistic child.

Does a bad night of sleep worsen ADHD symptoms in children?

Yes. Poor sleep can worsen attention, hyperactivity, and emotional regulation in children with ADHD.

Who can help with sleep problems in neurodivergent children?

A physician, occupational therapist, and psychologist can each help with sleep problems in neurodivergent children.

Should my child be assessed if they have severe sleep problems?

If severe sleep problems come with attention, social, or sensory differences, an autism or ADHD assessment can help clarify your child's needs.
