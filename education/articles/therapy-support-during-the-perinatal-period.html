Therapy Support During the Perinatal Period

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency. Confusion, hallucinations, or unusual beliefs after birth need emergency care. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse any time.

Therapy support during the perinatal period, which covers pregnancy and the first year after birth, helps parents cope with the emotional changes and challenges of this time. It can address anxiety, low mood, intrusive thoughts, difficult birth experiences, pregnancy loss, fertility struggles, relationship changes, and adjusting to a new identity as a parent. Perinatal mood and anxiety disorders are common, and therapy is an effective treatment, often alongside practical support and, when needed, medication prescribed by a physician. Seeking support is not a sign of failure. It is a way of caring for yourself and your family.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps with anxious or depressive thinking, worry about the baby, and patterns such as avoidance or reassurance-seeking. It has good evidence for perinatal depression and anxiety.

Interpersonal therapy. This approach focuses on role changes, relationships, and support, which are central in the transition to parenthood. It has strong research support for perinatal depression.

Exposure and response prevention. For perinatal OCD, which often involves distressing intrusive thoughts about harm coming to the baby, this specialized form of CBT helps reduce compulsions and fear.

Trauma-focused therapy. For people who experienced a traumatic birth or earlier trauma, trauma-focused approaches can reduce distress and flashbacks.

Other approaches. Acceptance and commitment therapy, compassion-focused therapy, and emotion-focused therapy can help with self-criticism, guilt, and identity changes. Grief counselling supports people after pregnancy loss, stillbirth, or infant loss.

Couples and family support. Parenthood changes relationships. Couples counselling can help partners communicate, share the load, and reconnect.

Medication. For moderate to severe symptoms, a physician may recommend medication. Many options are considered compatible with pregnancy or breastfeeding, and decisions weigh benefits and risks.

Practical and peer support. Help with sleep, meals, and household tasks, as well as parent groups and peer support, reduce strain and isolation.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

Planning starts with understanding what you are experiencing. Common concerns include postpartum depression, postpartum anxiety, OCD, and trauma, which are part of the broader group of perinatal mood and anxiety disorders. Severity, safety, and past mental health history shape choices. Practical factors matter too, such as sleep, feeding, childcare for appointments, and whether virtual sessions would be easier. Many perinatal therapists welcome babies in sessions. Preferences about medication, and whether you are pregnant or breastfeeding, are considered with your physician. Care may involve your family doctor, midwife, obstetrician, public health nurse, and therapist working together.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Many parents notice improvements within several weeks of starting therapy, such as less worry, better sleep when possible, more enjoyment of the baby, and feeling more like themselves. Progress can be uneven, since sleep deprivation, feeding challenges, and life changes affect mood. Setbacks are common and do not mean therapy is not working. Treatment length varies from a few months to longer, depending on needs. Some people continue therapy through the first year or return later, such as during a subsequent pregnancy.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Protecting sleep where possible, such as sharing night feeds, makes a real difference. Accepting practical help, eating regularly, and getting outside can support mood. Connecting with other parents reduces isolation. Being honest with your care team about how you are feeling, including intrusive thoughts, helps them support you. Remember that intrusive thoughts are common in the perinatal period and do not mean you want to act on them. Treat yourself with patience, since adjusting to parenthood takes time.

Common Questions About Perinatal Therapy

What does the perinatal period include?

It includes pregnancy and the first year after birth.

What can therapy help with during pregnancy and after birth?

Therapy can help with anxiety, low mood, intrusive thoughts, birth trauma, loss, relationship changes, and adjusting to parenthood.

Which therapies work best for perinatal depression?

CBT and interpersonal therapy both have strong evidence for perinatal depression.

Can I bring my baby to therapy sessions?

Many perinatal therapists welcome babies in sessions, and virtual sessions can also make attending easier.

Will my therapist and doctor talk together about whether I need medication while expecting?

With your consent they can coordinate. Medication decisions rest with a physician, who helps weigh benefits and risks, and many options are considered compatible.

Is it safe to tell a perinatal therapist about the frightening thoughts I get about my newborn?

Yes. Perinatal therapists hear about intrusive thoughts often. They are common, usually unwanted, and sharing them helps you get the right support.

Can therapy help after a traumatic birth?

Yes. Trauma-focused approaches can reduce distress and flashbacks related to a difficult birth.

Is there support after pregnancy loss?

Yes. Grief counselling and specialized support can help after miscarriage, stillbirth, or infant loss.

How soon will I feel better with perinatal therapy?

Many parents notice improvement within several weeks, though progress can be uneven.

Can partners join perinatal therapy?

Yes. Couples sessions can help partners communicate, share responsibilities, and reconnect.
