What to Expect From Perinatal and Hormonal Mental Health Support

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or you feel confused or are seeing or hearing things others do not, seek help right away. Call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline), call 911, or go to the nearest emergency department. You can also call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse at HealthLink BC.

Perinatal and hormonal mental health support is therapy focused on emotional changes connected to reproductive and hormonal life stages. This includes pregnancy, the postpartum period, pregnancy loss, fertility challenges, premenstrual mood changes such as PMDD, and perimenopause. You can expect a non-judgmental space to talk about what you are experiencing, practical strategies for managing anxiety, low mood, intrusive thoughts, or irritability, and coordination with your medical providers when helpful. These concerns are common and treatable, and seeking support is a sign of care for yourself and your family, not a sign of failure.

What to Expect

The first session. Your therapist will ask about your current symptoms, your pregnancy, birth, or cycle history, sleep, support at home, and your goals. They will also ask about safety, which is routine.

Understanding what is happening. Many people are relieved to learn that conditions such as postpartum depression, postpartum anxiety, and postpartum OCD are well recognized, and that scary intrusive thoughts do not mean you will act on them.

Therapy approaches. Cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy have good evidence for perinatal depression and anxiety. Therapy may also include emotion-focused work, self-compassion, processing a difficult birth, and adjusting to changes in identity and relationships.

Practical problem-solving. Sessions often address sleep, sharing the load with a partner, asking for help, and setting realistic expectations.

Practical Details

Timing. You can seek support during pregnancy, at any point after birth, during fertility treatment, after a loss, or during perimenopause.

Virtual sessions. Many new parents find virtual sessions easier to fit around feeding and naps. You generally need to be in BC for virtual sessions with a BC clinician.

Babies in sessions. Some therapists are comfortable with a young baby being present during sessions, so it is worth asking.

Medication and medical care. Therapists do not prescribe. If medication may help, including during pregnancy or breastfeeding, a family doctor, obstetrician, midwife, or psychiatrist can discuss options. Hormonal symptoms, such as those in perimenopause, also benefit from medical assessment.

Partners. Partners can sometimes join a session, and couples support may help with adjustment. The page about therapy support during the perinatal period has more detail.

Fees and coverage vary, so check your extended health plan and ask the clinic directly.

How to Prepare

  • Note your main symptoms. When they started and what makes them better or worse.
  • Track mood if it seems linked to your cycle. A simple daily log over two cycles can help clarify patterns such as PMDD.
  • List medications and medical providers. Including any hormonal treatments.
  • Plan the logistics. Arrange childcare or a quiet space, or ask if your baby can be present.
  • Be honest about difficult thoughts. Clinicians hear about intrusive thoughts often and can help.

Treatment options are also described on the page about treatment for postpartum depression and anxiety.

Common Questions About Perinatal and Hormonal Mental Health Support

What does perinatal mental health support involve?

Perinatal mental health support involves therapy for mood, anxiety, and intrusive thoughts during pregnancy and after birth, with practical strategies and coordination with medical care.

When should I seek help for postpartum mood changes?

Seek help if low mood, anxiety, or intrusive thoughts last more than about two weeks after birth, feel intense, or affect daily life. Urgent symptoms need immediate care.

Can I get therapy during pregnancy for anxiety?

Yes. Therapy during pregnancy can help with anxiety, low mood, past birth trauma, and adjusting to the changes ahead.

Are frightening thoughts about a newborn common for new parents?

Yes. Unwanted intrusive thoughts about a baby are common in the postpartum period and are not a sign you will act on them. A therapist can help.

Which talk therapies have evidence for perinatal mood concerns?

Cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy have good evidence for postpartum depression and anxiety, sometimes alongside medication.

What kind of support helps with perimenopause mood changes?

Therapy for anxiety, low mood, irritability, and sleep problems, combined with a medical assessment of hormonal symptoms, often helps most during perimenopause.

Why track mood across two menstrual cycles before an assessment?

Daily tracking over at least two menstrual cycles can show whether symptoms follow a pattern, which helps with assessing conditions like PMDD.

Can a therapist prescribe medication during pregnancy or breastfeeding?

No. Therapists do not prescribe. A doctor, obstetrician, midwife, or psychiatrist can discuss medication options during pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Are virtual sessions a good option for new parents?

Many new parents find virtual sessions easier to fit around feeding and naps. You generally need to be in BC for sessions with a BC clinician.

Is there a role for a partner in postpartum support sessions?

Partners can sometimes join a perinatal therapy session, and couples support may help both of you adjust to life with a new baby.
