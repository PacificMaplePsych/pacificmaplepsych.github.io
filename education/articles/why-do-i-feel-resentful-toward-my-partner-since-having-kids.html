Why Do I Feel Resentful Toward My Partner Since Having Kids

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You love your kids, and you love your partner. But lately you catch yourself keeping score. Who got up last night, who remembered the dentist appointment, who got to go to the gym, who gets to sit down after dinner while the other one clears up. Small things that never bothered you before now spark a flash of irritation, and sometimes the resentment feels bigger than the love.

Resentment after having children is one of the most common relationship challenges parents face, and one of the least talked about. It does not mean you chose the wrong partner. It usually means the relationship is under real strain and something about how you share the load, or connect, needs attention.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Sleep deprivation. Chronic lack of sleep makes everyone more irritable, less patient, and more likely to interpret a partner's actions negatively.

An uneven load. One partner often ends up carrying more of the childcare, housework, or mental planning, even in couples who expected to share equally. That imbalance is a powerful source of resentment.

The invisible work. Remembering appointments, tracking what the kids need, planning meals, and noticing when supplies run low is real work, often called the mental load. When it goes unrecognized, resentment grows.

Lost time and identity. Less time for yourself, your career, friends, and hobbies can leave you feeling you have given up more than your partner has.

Postpartum mood changes. Depression, anxiety, and anger after having a baby are common and can intensify resentment. If anger feels intense or new, Why Do I Feel Angry More Than Usual Lately may be helpful.

Different parenting styles. Disagreements about discipline, screen time, or routines can leave one partner feeling undermined or unsupported.

When It Looks More Like Parenting Overload

When both partners are depleted, each tends to focus on what they are doing and what the other is not. Resentment builds quietly when needs are not spoken, then leaks out in sarcasm, criticism, or withdrawal. Over time, this can turn into feeling distant from your partner.

Signs that parenting overload is fuelling resentment include:

  • Feeling like the default parent who notices and handles everything.
  • Rarely having time alone or time as a couple.
  • Irritability that spills onto the kids, such as snapping at them more than you would like.
  • A sense that you have nothing left to give at the end of the day.
  • Arguments about chores that are really about feeling unseen.

Resentment is often a signal of unmet needs, such as rest, recognition, fairness, or connection. Naming those needs clearly is usually more productive than arguing over individual tasks.

What Can Help

  • Make the invisible visible. Write down everything each partner does, including the mental load. Many couples are surprised by what the list reveals.
  • Divide by ownership. Rather than helping with tasks, each partner fully owns certain areas, including the planning and remembering.
  • Talk about needs, not scores. Try "I need an hour to myself on weekends" rather than "you never let me have a break."
  • Protect couple time. Even twenty minutes of talking without discussing logistics can rebuild warmth.
  • Show appreciation. Noticing and thanking each other for specific things helps both partners feel seen.

Common Questions About Resentment After Having Children

Is it normal to resent my partner after having a baby?

Yes. Research consistently shows that relationship satisfaction often dips after children arrive, largely due to sleep loss, workload, and less time together. Resentment is common and usually improves when couples address the underlying causes.

What is the mental load in parenting?

The mental load refers to the invisible work of remembering, planning, and organizing family life, such as tracking appointments, school forms, and what needs buying. It is real work that often falls unevenly and is a common source of resentment.

Why do we argue about chores so much since having kids?

Chore arguments are often about deeper issues such as fairness, recognition, exhaustion, and feeling unsupported. Addressing those needs directly usually reduces the number of arguments about specific tasks.

Can postpartum depression or anxiety cause resentment toward a partner?

Yes. Postpartum mood changes can increase irritability, anger, and feelings of being alone, which can show up as resentment. Support for postpartum mental health often helps the relationship too.

How can we divide childcare more fairly?

Listing every task, including planning and remembering, and assigning full ownership of certain areas to each partner tends to work better than one partner helping the other. Revisiting the plan regularly keeps it fair.

How do I talk to my partner about resentment without starting a fight?

Choose a calm moment, speak about your feelings and needs rather than their faults, and suggest specific changes. Starting with appreciation for what they do can make the conversation feel less like an attack.

Can resentment damage a relationship long term?

Unaddressed resentment can gradually erode closeness and lead to contempt, which is linked to relationship breakdown. Addressing it early, while both partners are still invested, gives the relationship the best chance.

Why do I feel like the default parent?

The default parent is the one who is automatically expected to notice and handle children's needs. This role often develops gradually, especially if one partner took more leave or works fewer hours, and it can be rebalanced with deliberate conversations.

Does couples therapy help new parents?

Yes. Couples therapy can help new parents communicate needs, share responsibilities more fairly, manage conflict, and rebuild connection during a demanding life stage.

How can we reconnect as a couple with young children?

Short, regular moments of undistracted time, such as a daily check-in after bedtime, expressing appreciation, and occasional time away from the kids can help partners reconnect even during busy years.
