What Is Avoidant Personality Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Avoidant personality disorder (AvPD) is a personality disorder marked by a pervasive, long-standing pattern of social inhibition, feelings of inadequacy, and intense sensitivity to criticism or rejection. People with AvPD usually want close relationships very much but avoid them out of fear of being judged, humiliated, or rejected. The pattern typically begins by early adulthood and affects many areas of life, including work, friendships, and romance. AvPD is often misunderstood as shyness or introversion, but it causes significant distress. It is treatable, and many people build fuller social lives with support.

What Avoidant Personality Disorder Looks Like

  • Avoiding jobs, promotions, or activities that involve significant contact with people, out of fear of criticism.
  • Only getting involved with people when certain of being liked.
  • Holding back in close relationships for fear of being shamed or ridiculed.
  • Being preoccupied with the possibility of rejection in social situations.
  • Feeling socially inept, unappealing, or inferior to others.
  • Reluctance to try new things or take personal risks because they might be embarrassing.

At work, someone with AvPD might turn down opportunities that involve presentations or teamwork. In relationships, they may keep partners at a distance or stay quiet about their needs. Loneliness is common, and many people with AvPD describe watching life from the sidelines.

Why It Happens

AvPD likely develops from an interaction of temperament and experience. Some children are naturally more inhibited and sensitive to new situations. Experiences such as repeated criticism, rejection, bullying, emotional neglect, or feeling ashamed in childhood can shape beliefs that one is inadequate and that others will reject them. Avoidance then protects against feared rejection but prevents positive experiences that could challenge these beliefs, so the pattern becomes entrenched over time.

Many people with AvPD have a rich inner life and care deeply about others. The problem is not a lack of interest in people but a powerful fear that closeness will lead to pain. Recognizing this can reduce shame and make seeking help feel more possible.

What It Is Not

  • Not shyness. Shyness is common and mild. AvPD is pervasive and significantly limits life.
  • Not introversion. Introverts may prefer solitude but do not typically fear rejection or feel inadequate.
  • Closely related to social anxiety disorder. The two overlap heavily, and some clinicians see AvPD as a more pervasive form. The page on social anxiety disorder explains the overlap.
  • Not schizoid personality. People with schizoid traits generally do not desire close relationships, while people with AvPD do.
  • Not avoidant attachment alone. Avoidant attachment involves discomfort with closeness in relationships, while AvPD involves broader social fear and inadequacy.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

AvPD is diagnosed by a psychologist or psychiatrist through a detailed assessment of long-term patterns across relationships and settings, often alongside screening for social anxiety, depression, and other conditions. Treatment typically involves therapy. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps people challenge beliefs about inadequacy and gradually face social situations. Schema therapy addresses deep-rooted patterns formed early in life. Group therapy can offer a safe place to practise connection. Progress is often gradual, but many people become more comfortable with others and more confident in themselves.

Common Questions About Avoidant Personality Disorder

What are the key features of avoidant personality disorder?

Key features include avoiding social situations out of fear of criticism, feeling inadequate, being very sensitive to rejection, and holding back in relationships despite wanting closeness.

Do people with avoidant personality disorder want relationships?

Yes. Unlike some other patterns, people with AvPD usually want close relationships but avoid them out of fear of being judged or rejected.

When does avoidant personality disorder usually develop?

It typically becomes apparent by early adulthood, often with roots in childhood temperament and experiences of criticism or rejection.

Is avoidant personality disorder the same as social anxiety disorder?

They overlap heavily. AvPD tends to be more pervasive, affecting self-image and most relationships, while social anxiety may be more situation-specific.

How is AvPD different from schizoid personality disorder?

People with schizoid traits generally do not desire close relationships, while people with AvPD strongly want connection but fear rejection.

Can childhood bullying contribute to avoidant personality disorder?

Yes. Repeated rejection, bullying, or criticism in childhood can shape beliefs of inadequacy that contribute to avoidant patterns.

How does avoidant personality disorder affect work?

It can lead people to avoid promotions, teamwork, or roles involving public contact, limiting career growth despite ability.

Which therapies help with avoidant personality disorder?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, schema therapy, and group therapy can help reduce fear of rejection, challenge beliefs of inadequacy, and build social confidence.

Can someone with avoidant personality disorder improve?

Yes. Many people make meaningful progress with therapy, building relationships and confidence, though change is often gradual.

What helps a friend or partner who fears rejection this deeply?

Be patient, consistent, and non-judgmental, invite rather than pressure, and encourage professional support. Small, reliable positive experiences help build trust.
