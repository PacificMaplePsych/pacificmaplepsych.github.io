Building Secure Attachment as an Adult

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Building secure attachment as an adult means gradually learning to feel safe with closeness and independence, to trust others while trusting yourself, and to handle conflict and distance without panic or shutdown. Attachment styles, the patterns people develop in close relationships, often begin in childhood, but they are not fixed. Research describes something called earned security, where adults who had insecure early experiences develop more secure patterns through reflection, supportive relationships, and sometimes therapy. Change usually happens slowly, through repeated experiences of safety and new ways of responding.

Why Secure Attachment Can Be Built

Attachment styles are explained on the page about attachment styles. People with anxious attachment may fear abandonment and seek reassurance. People with avoidant attachment may value independence and pull away from closeness. Disorganized attachment can involve wanting closeness while also fearing it.

These patterns developed for good reasons, often to cope with caregivers who were inconsistent, distant, or frightening. The brain remains able to learn throughout life. When adults repeatedly experience relationships where needs are met, feelings are understood, and conflicts are repaired, their expectations begin to change. Understanding your own history, and making sense of it, is also linked with earned security.

Strategies to Try

1. Learn Your Patterns

Notice how you react when you feel close to someone, when they pull away, or when there is conflict. Do you chase, withdraw, or swing between the two?

2. Make Sense of Your Story

Reflect on your early relationships and how they shaped you. Writing or talking about your history, with compassion, helps you understand your reactions rather than being driven by them.

3. Choose Relationships That Feel Safe

Spend time with people who are consistent, responsive, and respectful. Secure friends and partners can model new ways of relating.

4. Practise Communicating Needs

State your needs clearly and calmly, such as "I feel anxious when I do not hear from you all day. Could you send a quick message?" rather than protesting or hiding them.

5. Tolerate Discomfort

If you tend toward anxiety, practise pausing before seeking reassurance. If you tend toward avoidance, practise staying present a little longer in emotional conversations.

6. Repair After Conflict

Returning after arguments to understand each other and reconnect builds trust, as described on the page about repairing after an argument.

7. Build Self-Soothing Skills

Grounding, slow breathing, and self-compassion help you manage strong emotions without relying entirely on others or shutting down.

8. Strengthen Your Relationship With Yourself

Security includes trusting yourself, keeping your commitments to yourself, and treating yourself with kindness.

Common Obstacles

Old patterns can be powerful, especially during stress or conflict. Slipping back does not mean failure. Noticing and choosing a different response next time is progress. Some people are drawn to partners whose patterns feel familiar, such as a pursuer drawn to someone who withdraws. Recognizing this can help you make different choices. Closeness can feel uncomfortable at first for people used to distance, and space can feel threatening for those used to anxious pursuit. These feelings usually ease with experience.

Past trauma can make security feel especially hard to reach, and it may need trauma-informed support.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If attachment patterns cause repeated relationship distress, intense fear of abandonment or closeness, or difficulty trusting anyone, therapy can help. The therapy relationship itself can offer a secure base, and approaches such as emotionally focused, attachment-based, and schema therapy can help. The page about individual therapy for relationship patterns explains more. Couples counselling can help partners build security together.

Common Questions About Building Secure Attachment

Can an adult change their attachment style?

Yes. Adults can develop more secure attachment through reflection, supportive relationships, and sometimes therapy, known as earned security.

What is earned secure attachment?

Earned secure attachment describes adults who had insecure early experiences but developed more secure patterns later in life.

How long does it take to build more secure attachment?

Building secure attachment usually happens gradually, over months or years of repeated safe experiences and new responses.

If I panic when my partner goes quiet, what steps move me toward secure attachment?

People with anxious attachment can practise pausing before seeking reassurance, communicating needs calmly, and building self-soothing skills.

I pull away when relationships get close. How do I build more secure attachment?

People with avoidant attachment can practise staying present in emotional conversations and gradually allowing more closeness.

Can a secure partner help me become more securely attached?

A consistent, responsive partner can model secure attachment and help you experience relationships as safer over time.

Why do I keep choosing partners who trigger my insecure attachment?

Familiar attachment patterns can feel comfortable even when painful, which can draw people toward partners who activate their insecurity.

Does understanding my childhood help with attachment?

Making sense of early experiences with compassion is linked with earned secure attachment and helps you respond rather than react.

Can therapy help me build secure attachment?

Yes. The therapy relationship can offer a secure base, and attachment-focused approaches help build more secure patterns.

Can couples work on secure attachment together?

Yes. Couples counselling can help partners understand their attachment patterns and build security in the relationship.
