Understanding Nightmare Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Nightmares are vivid, frightening, or upsetting dreams that usually wake a person and are clearly remembered. Almost everyone has occasional nightmares, especially during stress or illness. Nightmare disorder is diagnosed when nightmares happen often, cause significant distress, and affect sleep, mood, or daytime functioning. People may dread going to sleep, wake exhausted, or feel anxious during the day because of their dreams. Nightmare disorder is common after trauma but can also occur on its own. It is treatable, and specific therapies can reduce how often nightmares happen and how distressing they feel.

What Nightmare Disorder Looks Like

  • Frequent, vivid dreams involving threats to safety, survival, or self-worth.
  • Waking suddenly and quickly becoming alert and oriented.
  • Clear memory of the dream content.
  • Racing heart, sweating, or fear on waking.
  • Difficulty falling back to sleep afterward.
  • Dreading going to bed or delaying sleep.
  • Daytime tiredness, low mood, anxiety, or trouble concentrating.
  • Recurring themes, or nightmares about the same event.

Nightmares usually occur during rapid eye movement sleep, often in the second half of the night. This distinguishes them from night terrors, which happen earlier in the night, involve confusion, and are often not remembered.

Why It Happens

Dreams are thought to help the brain process emotions and memories. When a person is under stress, anxious, or has experienced trauma, dreams can become more intense and frightening. After trauma, nightmares often replay the event or its themes and are a core symptom of PTSD. Other contributors include anxiety, depression, sleep deprivation, irregular sleep schedules, certain medications, alcohol and substance use or withdrawal, and some medical conditions.

Nightmares can also become a learned pattern. Once they happen often, fear of having them can make sleep lighter and more disrupted, which can increase dream recall and intensity. The brain may rehearse the same frightening dream script night after night. Avoiding sleep, or using alcohol to fall asleep, can make nightmares worse over time. Poor sleep then affects mood and coping, as described on the page about sleep and mental health.

Children often have nightmares, which usually decrease with age. When nightmares persist into adulthood, or begin after a difficult experience, they are worth addressing.

A few habits can also make nightmares more likely: watching distressing content late at night, heavy meals close to bedtime, and going to bed at very different times from one night to the next. Changing these alone rarely resolves nightmare disorder, but they can support treatment.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as night terrors. Night terrors involve confusion and little memory, while nightmares are vivid and remembered.
  • Not a prediction or a sign that something bad will happen. Dreams reflect emotions and memory processing.
  • Not something you have to live with. Effective treatments exist.
  • Not the same as insomnia, though nightmares can lead to insomnia and the two often occur together.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about how often nightmares occur, their content, sleep patterns, trauma history, mood, substance use, and medications. A physician may review medical factors and screen for other sleep disorders. Imagery rehearsal therapy is a well-supported treatment. It involves writing down a recurring nightmare, changing the story in any way that feels better, and rehearsing the new version while awake for a few minutes each day. Over time, the new script can replace the old one. Cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia can improve overall sleep. When nightmares are linked with trauma, trauma-focused therapy often reduces them. Relaxation skills, consistent sleep routines, and limiting alcohol also help. Some medications may be considered by a physician.

Common Questions About Nightmare Disorder

What is nightmare disorder?

It involves frequent, vivid, distressing dreams that wake you and cause significant distress or daytime difficulties.

How often do nightmares need to happen to be a disorder?

There is no single number. The key is frequency combined with significant distress or effects on daily life.

What is the difference between nightmares and night terrors?

Nightmares are vivid and remembered, occurring later in the night. Night terrors involve confusion and little memory.

Why do I have nightmares after a traumatic event?

Trauma can intensify dreams as the brain tries to process the experience, and nightmares are a core PTSD symptom.

How does rewriting the ending of a bad dream help it stop coming back?

It involves rewriting a recurring nightmare and rehearsing the new version while awake, which can reduce nightmares.

Can alcohol cause nightmares?

Yes. Alcohol disrupts sleep, and withdrawal from alcohol or some substances can increase vivid dreams.

Why am I afraid to go to sleep?

Frequent nightmares can make bedtime feel threatening, leading to avoidance and lighter, more disrupted sleep.

Could a prescription I started recently be giving me vivid bad dreams?

Some medications can increase vivid dreams. A doctor or pharmacist can review your medications.

Do nightmares in adults mean something is wrong?

Occasional nightmares are normal. Frequent, distressing nightmares are worth discussing with a professional.

Can therapy stop recurring nightmares?

Therapies such as imagery rehearsal therapy, CBT for insomnia, and trauma-focused therapy often reduce nightmares significantly.
