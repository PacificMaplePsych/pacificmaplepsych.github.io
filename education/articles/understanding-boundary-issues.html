Understanding Boundary Issues

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Boundaries are the limits and expectations that help define where you end and another person begins. They cover your time, energy, emotions, body, belongings, and values. Healthy boundaries allow people to be close while still respecting each other's needs. Boundary issues arise when boundaries are too loose, making it hard to say no or separate your feelings from others', or too rigid, making closeness and flexibility difficult. They can also occur when others repeatedly ignore or push past your limits. Boundary issues are very common, and boundaries are skills that can be learned at any stage of life.

What Boundary Issues Look Like

Loose or porous boundaries may include:

  • Struggling to say no, even when overloaded.
  • Feeling responsible for other people's emotions.
  • Oversharing personal information early in relationships.
  • Going along with plans or requests to avoid conflict.
  • Feeling resentful, drained, or taken for granted.

Rigid boundaries may include:

  • Keeping others at a distance and avoiding vulnerability.
  • Rarely asking for help or sharing feelings.
  • Strict rules that leave little room for compromise.

Many people have a mix, with loose boundaries in some relationships, such as with family, and rigid ones in others, such as at work.

Why It Happens

Boundaries are shaped early in life. In families where a child's needs, privacy, or feelings were not respected, or where the child had to manage a parent's emotions, it can be hard to learn that one's own limits matter. Some people grew up in homes where saying no led to anger, withdrawal of love, or punishment, so they learned to comply. Others grew up in chaotic or intrusive environments and developed rigid boundaries to protect themselves. Cultural and family expectations about obligation, respect, and closeness also shape what feels normal.

Later experiences, such as controlling relationships, workplace pressures, or trauma, can further affect boundaries. People-pleasing, fear of abandonment, and low self-worth often go hand in hand with loose boundaries, as explored on the page about people-pleasing. When boundaries are consistently loose, relationships can become unbalanced, as described on the page about one-sided relationships.

Digital life has added new boundary challenges. Constant messaging, work email on phones, and social media can make it hard to protect time and attention. Many people find it helpful to set clear expectations about response times and availability, both at work and with friends and family.

Boundaries often need extra care during life changes. Moving in with a partner, becoming a parent, caring for an aging relative, or starting a new job can all shift expectations. Talking about needs early, before resentment builds, tends to make these transitions smoother for everyone involved.

What It Is Not

  • Not selfish. Healthy boundaries protect relationships by preventing resentment and burnout.
  • Not controlling others. A boundary is about what you will do, not about forcing someone else to change.
  • Not the same as walls. Healthy boundaries are flexible and allow closeness.
  • Not something you must justify endlessly. A clear, kind no is enough.
  • Not permanent. Boundaries can be adjusted as relationships and needs change.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Building boundaries often starts with noticing your own feelings, such as resentment, exhaustion, or discomfort, as signals that a limit may be needed. Therapy can help you understand where your patterns come from, identify your needs and values, and practise communicating limits clearly and calmly. Assertiveness skills, such as using simple I statements and following through with consequences, are often taught. Cognitive behavioural and schema therapies can address beliefs such as having to please others to be loved. Couples or family therapy can help when boundary issues affect close relationships.

Common Questions About Boundary Issues

What are personal boundaries?

Boundaries are limits and expectations that protect your time, energy, emotions, body, and values in relationships.

What are signs of poor boundaries?

Signs include difficulty saying no, feeling responsible for others' emotions, oversharing, and frequent resentment or exhaustion.

Can boundaries be too strict?

Yes. Rigid boundaries keep others at a distance, making it hard to share feelings, ask for help, or be flexible.

Why do I feel guilty setting boundaries?

If saying no once led to anger or rejection, setting limits can trigger guilt even when the boundary is reasonable.

How do I set a boundary without seeming rude?

Be clear, calm, and kind. State what you need or will do, without long justifications or apologies.

What should I do if someone keeps ignoring my boundaries?

Restate the boundary, follow through with the consequence you described, and consider limiting contact if it continues.

Are boundaries different in different cultures?

Yes. Cultural and family norms shape expectations about closeness and obligation. Healthy boundaries can respect those values while protecting wellbeing.

How do childhood experiences shape boundaries?

Growing up with intrusive, controlling, or emotionally dependent caregivers can make it harder to recognize and assert personal limits.

Can setting boundaries improve relationships?

Yes. Clear boundaries reduce resentment, build trust, and help people feel safer and more respected.

Where do I start if I have never been good at saying no?

Start small. Notice resentment as a signal, practise short, kind refusals in low-stakes situations, and consider therapy to understand your patterns.
