How to Talk to Your Family About Getting Assessed

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

To talk to your family about getting assessed for autism or ADHD, choose a calm time, explain in simple terms why you are seeking an assessment and what you hope to learn, and be clear about what, if anything, you need from them, such as answering questions about your childhood. Some family members respond with support or recognition, while others may feel surprised, defensive, or worried that they missed something. You do not need anyone's permission to be assessed, and it is your choice whether and when to share. If family input would help the assessment but the relationship is difficult, other sources of childhood information can be used instead.

How These Conversations Usually Go

Supportive responses. Some relatives say they always wondered, recognize traits in themselves, or are glad to help. Autism and ADHD often run in families, so a conversation may prompt others to reflect too.

Surprise or doubt. Comments like "you seem fine" or "everyone is a little like that" are common, especially if you have masked or coped well, as described on the pages about autism masking and ADHD masking.

Guilt or defensiveness. Parents may worry that they missed signs or did something wrong. It can help to explain that many people were not identified as children, especially girls and those who masked, and that understanding was very different years ago.

Stigma or misinformation. Some family members may have outdated ideas about autism or ADHD. Sharing reliable information can help over time.

Practical Details

Why family input can help. Both autism and ADHD are developmental, so clinicians look for signs present in childhood. A parent, sibling, or long-time family friend may be asked to complete questionnaires or speak with the clinician, with your consent.

If family cannot help. If parents are not available, not supportive, or the relationship is unsafe, clinicians can use other sources, such as siblings, old friends, school records, photos, or your own memories. The page about adult autism assessment discusses this.

Privacy. Your assessment results are confidential. You decide what to share with family afterward.

Partners. A partner may be able to describe current traits and can be an important support during the process, as noted on the page about preparing for an assessment.

Children. If you are a parent, you can choose how and when to talk with your own children about an assessment, in age-appropriate ways.

How to Prepare

  • Clarify your reasons. For example, "I want to understand why some things have always been harder for me."
  • Decide what you need. Do you want emotional support, help with childhood questions, or simply to inform them?
  • Pick the right person first. Start with the family member most likely to respond well.
  • Share simple information. A short article or explanation can help, such as the page about signs of autism in adults.
  • Set boundaries. You can pause the conversation if it becomes unhelpful.
  • Plan support for yourself. Talk with a friend or therapist if reactions are hurtful.

The process of deciding whether to be assessed is covered on the page about whether to get assessed for ADHD as an adult.

Common Questions About Talking to Family About an Assessment

How do I tell my family I want an autism or ADHD assessment?

Choose a calm time, explain why you are seeking an assessment, and be clear about what you need from your family, such as help with childhood questions.

Do I need my family's permission to get assessed as an adult?

No. Adults do not need family permission for an assessment, and you choose whether and when to tell your family.

Why might a parent need to be involved in my assessment?

Autism and ADHD are developmental, so a parent may be asked about your childhood, with your consent, to help the clinician understand early signs.

What if my parents are not supportive of my assessment?

If parents are not supportive, clinicians can use siblings, old friends, school records, or your memories instead of family input.

Why do some family members react with doubt?

Family members may doubt an assessment if you masked or coped well, or if they hold outdated ideas about autism or ADHD.

How can I reassure a parent who feels guilty?

Explain to your parent that many people were not identified in childhood, especially those who masked, and that understanding was very different then.

Can talking about my assessment lead relatives to recognize traits in themselves?

Yes. Autism and ADHD often run in families, so some relatives recognize traits in themselves after you talk about your assessment.

Will my family see my assessment results?

No. Assessment results are confidential, and you decide what, if anything, to share with your family afterward.

Which family member should I talk to first about getting assessed?

Start with the family member most likely to respond supportively, then decide whether to share with others.

What if a family conversation about my assessment becomes hurtful?

You can pause the family conversation and set boundaries, and talking with a friend or therapist can help if reactions are hurtful.
