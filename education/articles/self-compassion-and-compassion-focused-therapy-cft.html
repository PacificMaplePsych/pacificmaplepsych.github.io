Self-Compassion and Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Compassion-focused therapy (CFT) is an evidence-informed approach that helps people who struggle with shame and harsh self-criticism develop self-compassion, a warm, understanding, and supportive way of relating to themselves. Self-compassion means treating yourself with the same kindness you would offer a friend who is struggling. It is not self-pity, self-indulgence, or lowering standards. Research suggests self-compassion is linked with lower anxiety and depression and greater resilience. CFT was developed for people whose self-criticism is so strong that standard approaches, such as challenging thoughts, do not feel convincing. It combines ideas from evolutionary psychology, attachment research, and cognitive behavioural therapy.

How Compassion-Focused Therapy Works

CFT describes three emotion systems that help people survive and thrive:

  • The threat system, which detects danger and produces feelings such as anxiety, anger, and shame.
  • The drive system, which motivates people to pursue goals and rewards.
  • The soothing system, which brings feelings of safety, calm, and connection.

In many people, especially those who experienced criticism, neglect, bullying, or trauma, the threat system is highly active and the soothing system is underdeveloped. The inner critic can function like an internal threat, attacking the self whenever something goes wrong. This is explored on the page about chronic self-criticism. People may try to cope through striving, perfectionism, or avoidance, which keep the threat system going.

CFT aims to balance these systems by strengthening the soothing system. It helps people understand that their brains and patterns developed in ways they did not choose, which reduces blame. It then builds compassion, both the courage to engage with suffering and the motivation to relieve it, directed toward oneself and others. People learn to notice the inner critic, understand what it is trying to protect, and develop a compassionate inner voice.

What Sessions Are Like

CFT often begins with learning about the three systems and how the brain responds to threat. This can bring relief, as people realize their struggles are understandable. Therapists explore where self-criticism comes from and what function it serves, such as trying to prevent mistakes or rejection.

Sessions include practical exercises, such as:

  • Soothing rhythm breathing, slowing the breath to calm the body.
  • Compassionate imagery, imagining a place of safety or a compassionate figure.
  • Developing a compassionate self, imagining the qualities of wisdom, strength, and warmth and practising them.
  • Compassionate letter writing, writing to yourself about a difficulty from a caring perspective.
  • Exploring different parts of the self, such as the angry, anxious, and sad parts, with compassion.

Between sessions, people practise these exercises regularly, since building compassion takes repetition. CFT can be delivered individually or in groups, and its length varies from a few months to longer. It is often integrated with other therapies.

Self-compassion can feel unfamiliar or even threatening at first. Some people fear it will make them lazy or weak, or find kindness toward themselves brings up grief. This is common and is addressed gently in therapy. Therapists help people go at a pace that feels manageable.

Who It Can Help

CFT can help people who experience high levels of shame and self-criticism, including those with depression, anxiety, trauma, perfectionism, eating disorders, and low self-esteem. Research on CFT is growing, with studies suggesting it can reduce self-criticism and shame and improve mood, though more research is needed. Self-compassion practices can also benefit anyone going through a difficult time.

People who prefer a structured, skills-based approach may benefit from combining CFT with cognitive behavioural therapy. When trauma is central, trauma-focused therapy may also be needed. For people in crisis, stabilization comes first.

Common Questions About Compassion-Focused Therapy

What would change in my daily life if CFT worked for me?

CFT aims to reduce shame and self-criticism by strengthening the brain's soothing system and building self-compassion.

Does self-compassion mean letting myself off the hook?

No. Self-compassion involves kindness and understanding while still taking responsibility and pursuing goals.

What are the three emotion systems in CFT?

They are the threat system, the drive system, and the soothing system.

Why does being kind to myself feel so uncomfortable?

If you are used to self-criticism, kindness can feel unfamiliar or bring up grief. Therapy helps you go at your own pace.

What exercises are used in compassion-focused therapy?

Common exercises include soothing rhythm breathing, compassionate imagery, compassionate letter writing, and developing a compassionate self.

Will self-compassion make me less motivated?

Research suggests self-compassion supports motivation and resilience, since people are less afraid of failure.

Who can benefit most from compassion-focused therapy?

People with strong shame and self-criticism, including those with depression, anxiety, trauma, or perfectionism.

I tried challenging my thoughts in CBT but never believed the new ones. Would CFT suit me better?

CFT builds on CBT but focuses on developing a compassionate inner voice, especially when challenging thoughts does not feel convincing.

Why does my inner voice sound like a parent or coach who used to criticize me?

It often develops from experiences of criticism, neglect, bullying, or trauma, as a way to try to prevent further harm.

Can I practise self-compassion on my own?

Yes. Exercises such as compassionate breathing and letter writing can help, and therapy offers more guided support.
