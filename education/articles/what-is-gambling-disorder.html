What Is Gambling Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Gambling disorder is associated with a higher risk of suicide, especially when debts are large. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 if you are in immediate danger.

Gambling disorder is a recognized mental health condition, classified as a behavioural addiction, involving a persistent pattern of gambling that causes significant problems and distress. It is the only non-substance addiction formally included in the main diagnostic manual. People with gambling disorder feel strong urges to gamble, find it hard to stop, and continue despite financial, relationship, and emotional harm. It can range from mild to severe and often co-occurs with depression, anxiety, and substance use. Gambling disorder is treatable, and recovery is possible.

What Gambling Disorder Looks Like

  • Needing to gamble with increasing amounts to feel the same excitement.
  • Restlessness or irritability when trying to cut down.
  • Repeated unsuccessful attempts to control or stop gambling.
  • Frequent preoccupation with gambling, reliving past bets, or planning the next.
  • Gambling when distressed, anxious, or low.
  • Chasing losses by returning to win money back.
  • Lying to hide the extent of gambling.
  • Jeopardizing relationships, jobs, or opportunities.
  • Relying on others for money to cover gambling debts.

Gambling disorder can involve casinos, lottery products, sports betting, online gambling, and other forms. Online platforms have made gambling available at all hours, which can speed up the development of problems.

Why It Happens

Gambling affects the brain's reward system. Unpredictable wins and near misses produce strong reward signals, and over time the brain adapts, much as it does with substances. Risk factors include genetics, early exposure to gambling, impulsivity, ADHD, depression, anxiety, and substance use. Stressful life events and financial pressure can increase risk, as gambling may seem like a quick solution or an escape. Cognitive distortions, such as believing in lucky streaks or that a win is "due," help maintain the pattern.

Families often bear significant effects, including financial hardship, broken trust, and emotional strain. Support for family members is an important part of recovery.

Gambling disorder often progresses in stages. It may begin with occasional wins that feel exciting, followed by more frequent play, larger bets, and losses. As losses grow, people may chase them, borrow money, or hide their gambling. Eventually, many reach a point of desperation, when debts and secrecy become overwhelming. Recognizing where someone is in this progression can guide the kind of support they need, and it is never too early or too late to seek help.

What It Is Not

  • Not recreational gambling. Many people gamble occasionally within limits without harm.
  • Not a moral failing. Gambling disorder involves changes in brain reward systems and is influenced by many factors.
  • Not a manic episode. Gambling during mania comes with other episodic symptoms such as decreased need for sleep.
  • Not solved by one big win. Wins often reinforce the pattern rather than ending it.
  • Related to, but different from, compulsive buying. Both involve hard-to-control urges and emotional cycles.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A psychologist, physician, or addiction specialist assesses gambling disorder by exploring gambling patterns, consequences, urges, and co-occurring conditions. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most studied treatment, helping people identify triggers, challenge gambling-related beliefs, and manage urges. Motivational interviewing supports commitment to change. Self-exclusion programs, financial safeguards, peer support groups, and family involvement are also helpful. British Columbia offers free, confidential gambling support services. Treating depression, anxiety, or ADHD can reduce relapse risk.

Common Questions About Gambling Disorder

Is gambling disorder an official diagnosis?

Yes. Gambling disorder is recognized in the main diagnostic manual as a behavioural addiction, the only non-substance addiction formally included.

How does gambling affect the brain?

Unpredictable wins and near misses activate the brain's reward system strongly. With repetition, the brain adapts, increasing urges and tolerance, similar to substance addictions.

What are the risk factors for gambling disorder?

Risk factors include genetics, early exposure, impulsivity, ADHD, depression, anxiety, substance use, stressful life events, and easy access to gambling.

Is online sports betting more likely to lead to addiction?

Online and sports betting can be riskier because they are always available, fast, and heavily marketed, allowing rapid repeated bets.

How does gambling disorder affect families?

Families often face financial hardship, broken trust, conflict, and emotional distress. Family members can benefit from their own support.

Why do heavy gambling debts raise the risk of a crisis?

Mounting debts, shame, and hopelessness can increase suicide risk. Anyone struggling with these feelings should reach out to 9-8-8 or other support.

What are cognitive distortions in gambling?

They are mistaken beliefs, such as thinking a win is due, believing in lucky streaks, or overestimating control, that keep people gambling.

How do casino and online self-bans work in BC?

Self-exclusion is a voluntary program in which a person asks to be banned from gambling venues or sites for a period, offered through provincial gambling providers.

How is gambling disorder treated?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, self-exclusion, financial safeguards, peer support, and family involvement are commonly used.

Is recovery from gambling disorder possible after a relapse?

Yes. Recovery from gambling disorder is possible, and relapse is common along the way. Treatment and support help people regain control over time.
