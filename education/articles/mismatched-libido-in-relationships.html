Mismatched Libido in Relationships

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Mismatched libido, also called desire discrepancy, happens when partners want sex at different frequencies or in different ways. It is one of the most common concerns couples bring to therapy, and nearly every long-term couple experiences it at some point. Desire is influenced by stress, health, hormones, medications, relationship quality, life stage, and individual differences. Some people experience spontaneous desire that appears out of the blue, while many others experience responsive desire that emerges after intimacy begins. Neither partner is wrong or broken. Couples tend to do best when they talk openly, reduce pressure and blame, and broaden how they think about intimacy.

Why Desire Differs

Stress and Mental Health

Anxiety, depression, burnout, and exhaustion can lower desire, as described on the page about how stress and anxiety affect sexual desire.

Health and Medications

Hormonal changes, chronic illness, pain, and medications such as some antidepressants can affect libido.

Life Stages

Pregnancy, parenting young children, perimenopause, and aging can all shift desire, as described on the page about perimenopause and mental health.

Relationship Factors

Unresolved conflict, resentment, or feeling unappreciated can dampen desire, as described on the page about rebuilding intimacy after conflict.

Types of Desire

Spontaneous desire arises without an obvious trigger. Responsive desire appears in response to touch, closeness, or arousal. Many people, especially in long-term relationships, experience mostly responsive desire, which is normal.

The Pursue-Withdraw Cycle in the Bedroom

The higher-desire partner may feel rejected and push for sex more, while the lower-desire partner feels pressured and pulls away further. Both end up hurt. This mirrors the broader pattern described on the page about the pursue-withdraw pattern.

What Helps

Talk Without Blame

Discuss desire outside the bedroom, with curiosity rather than accusation. Share what makes you feel close and what gets in the way.

Reduce Pressure

Pressure tends to lower desire further. Agreeing on affectionate touch that does not have to lead to sex can help both partners relax.

Understand Responsive Desire

The lower-desire partner may find that desire builds once intimacy begins, if they feel safe and unpressured.

Broaden Intimacy

Physical affection, emotional closeness, play, and different forms of sexual connection all count.

Address Underlying Factors

Treating anxiety, depression, pain, or medication side effects, and resolving relationship conflict, often improves desire.

Prioritize Connection

Scheduling time together can seem unromantic, but it often helps busy couples.

Questions Couples Can Explore Together

Some couples find it helpful to talk through questions such as: When do we each feel most connected? What kinds of touch feel good without leading anywhere? What gets in the way of desire, such as tiredness, stress, or unresolved conflict? What does each of us need to feel wanted and safe? Talking about these questions outside the bedroom, and with curiosity rather than criticism, can shift the conversation from "Why do you not want me?" to "How can we find each other again?"

It can also help to recognize that the higher-desire partner may be seeking reassurance and closeness, not only sex, while the lower-desire partner may need to feel less pressure before desire can emerge. When both partners feel understood, the gap often narrows. If one partner has experienced trauma, pain, or significant anxiety around sex, individual support alongside couples work can be especially helpful.

When to Get Support

Consider support if desire differences cause frequent conflict, distance, or distress, or if sex has become painful, avoided, or associated with anxiety. A physician can check for medical causes. Couples therapy or sex-focused therapy can help partners communicate and reconnect, as described on the page about how couples therapy works. Pain during sex is described on the page about painful sex and vaginismus.

Common Questions About Mismatched Libido

What is mismatched libido?

Mismatched libido, or desire discrepancy, is when partners want sex at different frequencies or in different ways.

Is mismatched libido normal?

Yes. Mismatched libido is one of the most common concerns in long-term relationships.

What causes low libido in relationships?

Low libido can be caused by stress, mental health, hormones, medications, health problems, life stage, or relationship conflict.

Is responsive desire normal in long-term relationships?

Yes. Many people in long-term relationships mostly experience responsive desire, which emerges after intimacy begins rather than spontaneously.

How do couples deal with different sex drives?

Couples deal with different sex drives by talking openly, reducing pressure, understanding responsive desire, and broadening intimacy.

Can antidepressants lower libido?

Yes. Some antidepressants can lower libido, and a prescriber may be able to adjust medication.

Does a stressful season of life explain mismatched libido?

It can. Stress, exhaustion, and busy life stages often lower one partner's desire, creating mismatched libido that may ease when stress eases.

Does mismatched libido mean the relationship is in trouble?

Not necessarily. Many couples navigate mismatched libido successfully, especially with open communication.

Should couples schedule sex?

Scheduling time for intimacy can help busy couples prioritize connection, as long as it does not create pressure.

How does couples therapy approach differences in sexual desire?

Couples therapy helps partners talk openly about differences in sexual desire, reduce pressure and blame, and find ways to reconnect.
