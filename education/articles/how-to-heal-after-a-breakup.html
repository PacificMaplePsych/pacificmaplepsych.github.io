How to Heal After a Breakup

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Healing after a breakup usually involves allowing yourself to grieve, limiting contact with your ex for a while, taking care of your body and daily routines, leaning on supportive people, and gradually making sense of what happened. Breakups can hurt intensely because they involve the loss of a person, shared routines, future plans, and sometimes part of your identity. Research shows that romantic rejection activates brain systems involved in pain and craving, which helps explain why the urge to check on or contact an ex can feel so strong. Most people feel noticeably better with time. If the pain stays overwhelming, therapy can help.

Why Breakups Hurt So Much

A breakup is a form of grief. You may miss the person, the companionship, physical closeness, and the future you imagined. The loss of attachment can trigger anxiety, sleep problems, rumination, and low mood. For people with anxious attachment, breakups can feel especially destabilizing, as described on the page about anxious attachment.

Ways to Heal

1. Let Yourself Grieve

Sadness, anger, relief, and confusion are all normal. Allowing feelings rather than pushing them away helps them move through, as described on the page about how to process grief without rushing it.

2. Create Distance

Limiting contact, muting or unfollowing on social media, and avoiding checking their profiles reduces the pull to reconnect and gives your brain time to adjust. If you co-parent or work together, keep contact focused and practical.

3. Take Care of Basics

Sleep, eat regularly, move your body, and limit alcohol. These protect your mood when emotions are raw.

4. Lean on Others

Spend time with friends and family, even when you would rather withdraw.

5. Limit Rumination

Replaying the relationship can keep pain fresh. Set aside short periods for reflection and redirect your attention at other times, as described on the page about rumination.

6. Rediscover Yourself

Reconnect with interests, friendships, and goals that may have faded during the relationship.

7. Reflect When You Are Ready

Over time, consider what you learned about your needs, boundaries, and patterns, as described on the page about why some people keep choosing the same type of partner.

Common Obstacles

Contacting an ex for reassurance or closure can restart the pain. Rushing into a new relationship to avoid feelings may delay healing. Self-blame and idealizing the ex are also common. If the relationship was controlling or abusive, the page about recovering from narcissistic abuse may help.

How Long It Takes

There is no set timeline. Many people notice gradual improvement over weeks to months, with harder days around anniversaries or milestones. Longer relationships, unexpected endings, and additional losses can make recovery take longer. The page about struggling after a separation or divorce explores longer partnerships.

When You Were the One Who Ended It

People who end a relationship can also grieve. They may feel guilt, doubt their decision, or miss their former partner, even if the breakup was the right choice. These feelings are normal and do not necessarily mean the decision was wrong. Allowing space for mixed emotions helps healing on both sides.

When to Get Support

Consider therapy if, after several weeks, you feel unable to function, cannot stop thinking about your ex, feel hopeless, or notice signs of depression or anxiety. Therapy can help you process the loss, rebuild confidence, and understand relationship patterns so future relationships feel healthier.

Common Questions About Healing After a Breakup

How do I heal after a breakup?

Heal after a breakup by allowing yourself to grieve, limiting contact, caring for your body, leaning on others, and reflecting on what you learned.

Why do breakups hurt so much?

Breakups hurt because they involve losing a person, routines, plans, and identity, and romantic rejection activates brain systems involved in pain and craving.

How long does it take to get over a breakup?

There is no set timeline, but many people notice gradual improvement over weeks to months after a breakup.

Should I stay friends with my ex after a breakup?

Many people find it easier to heal with distance first, and friendship may or may not be possible later.

Is no contact after a breakup helpful?

Limiting contact after a breakup often helps reduce the pull to reconnect and gives your brain time to adjust.

Why do I keep thinking about my ex?

Thinking about an ex is common after a breakup because of attachment and rumination, and it usually eases over time.

Is it normal to feel relief after a breakup?

Yes. Relief, along with sadness, anger, and confusion, is a normal feeling after a breakup.

Does dating right after a breakup delay healing?

Dating right after a breakup to avoid feelings can delay healing, though there is no single right timeline.

Can a breakup cause depression?

A breakup can trigger depression, especially if sadness, hopelessness, or loss of interest lasts for weeks.

What does therapy focus on after a painful breakup?

Therapy after a painful breakup often focuses on processing grief, rebuilding confidence, reducing rumination, and understanding relationship patterns.
