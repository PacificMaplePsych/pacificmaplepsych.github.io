Therapy Approaches for Eating Disorders

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Eating disorders can cause serious medical complications. If you feel faint, have chest pain, or feel very unwell, seek medical care or call 911. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse.

The main therapy approaches for eating disorders include enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E), family-based treatment for young people, dialectical behaviour therapy skills, specialist supportive clinical management, the Maudsley model of anorexia treatment for adults, and interpersonal therapy for binge eating. These therapies help people restore regular eating, reduce behaviours such as restricting, bingeing, or purging, and address the thoughts, emotions, and life circumstances that keep the eating disorder going. Therapy works best as part of a team that includes medical monitoring and often a dietitian. Recovery is possible, and early treatment tends to lead to better outcomes.

The Main Treatment Options

Enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E). CBT-E is one of the most studied treatments for adults with bulimia, binge eating disorder, and many other eating disorders, and it is also used for anorexia. It focuses on regular eating, understanding what maintains the disorder, and addressing concerns about shape and weight. It often runs around twenty sessions, longer for people who need to restore weight.

Family-based treatment. For children and teens with anorexia or bulimia, family-based treatment is a leading approach. Parents take an active role in restoring healthy eating, guided by a therapist, before gradually handing control back to the young person.

Maudsley model and specialist supportive clinical management. These approaches are used for adults with anorexia and focus on motivation, emotional and relationship factors, and gradual change alongside medical and nutritional care.

Interpersonal therapy. This approach addresses relationship difficulties and life changes linked with eating problems and has good evidence for binge eating disorder.

DBT skills. DBT emotion regulation skills and distress tolerance can help when eating behaviours are used to manage intense feelings.

Compassion-focused therapy. Building self-compassion, as described on the page about compassion-focused therapy, can ease the shame and self-criticism that often accompany eating disorders.

Medical and nutritional care. Regular medical monitoring and support from a registered dietitian are central parts of treatment.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

The plan depends on the type of eating disorder, age, medical stability, how long symptoms have lasted, and co-occurring conditions such as depression, anxiety, OCD, trauma, or substance use. Medical safety comes first. People who are medically unstable may need hospital care or intensive programs before or alongside outpatient therapy. Young people usually benefit from family involvement. Personal preferences, readiness for change, and practical factors such as access and cost also matter. In BC, a family doctor can refer to health authority eating disorder programs. Finding the right clinician is explained on the page about finding an eating-disorder-informed therapist.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Early progress often involves eating more regularly, fewer episodes of bingeing, purging, or restricting, and feeling slightly less preoccupied with food and weight. For people who need to restore weight, physical recovery often comes before thoughts and feelings shift, which can feel uncomfortable. Recovery is rarely a straight line. Setbacks and mixed feelings about change are common and are part of the process. Many people improve significantly within months of consistent treatment, while others need longer.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Following your meal plan, keeping medical appointments, and being honest with your team about behaviours all support recovery. Reducing exposure to diet content online and comparisons can help. Building coping strategies for difficult emotions reduces reliance on eating behaviours. Leaning on supportive people, and letting them know what helps, makes recovery less lonely. Treating yourself with patience, especially after setbacks, keeps you moving forward.

Common Questions About Therapy for Eating Disorders

Is the best eating disorder therapy different for teens and adults?

Often yes. CBT-E is widely studied for adults with eating disorders, and family-based treatment is a leading approach for young people.

How does enhanced CBT tackle the habits that keep an eating disorder going?

Enhanced CBT, or CBT-E, focuses on regular eating and on the thoughts about shape and weight and the behaviours that maintain the eating disorder.

How does family-based treatment work for teens with anorexia?

In family-based treatment, parents take an active role in restoring healthy eating for a teen with anorexia, guided by a therapist, before returning control gradually.

Which therapy helps with binge eating disorder?

CBT-E and interpersonal therapy both have good evidence for binge eating disorder.

Why does eating disorder therapy work alongside a dietitian and medical check-ups?

Eating disorders can affect the heart and other organs, so medical monitoring and nutritional support are central to safe recovery.

How long does therapy for an eating disorder usually take?

Many eating disorder therapies run around twenty sessions or more, and some people need longer, especially when weight restoration is needed.

Can DBT help with eating disorders?

DBT skills can help when eating disorder behaviours are used to manage intense emotions, often alongside other treatments.

Why do my thoughts about food lag behind my physical recovery?

In eating disorder recovery, the body often improves before thoughts and feelings about food and weight shift, which can feel uncomfortable.

How do I get eating disorder treatment through BC health services?

A family doctor can refer you to health authority eating disorder programs, which may have waitlists.

When does an eating disorder need hospital or intensive care?

Medical instability, such as fainting, heart problems, or rapid weight change, may require hospital care or intensive eating disorder programs.
