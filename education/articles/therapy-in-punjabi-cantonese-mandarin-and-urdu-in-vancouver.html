Pacific Maple Psych Centre offers therapy and psychological assessment in Punjabi, Cantonese, Mandarin, and Urdu, in addition to English, and one clinician also speaks conversational Hindi. Many people find it easier to describe emotions, memories, and family experiences in their first language, especially when English feels more formal or distant. Working with a clinician who shares your language can also make it easier to talk about cultural expectations, family roles, and stigma around mental health without needing to explain everything. Sessions are available in person in Vancouver and virtually across BC, so people in Surrey, Richmond, Burnaby, Abbotsford, and other communities can access support in their language.

Why Language Matters in Therapy

Emotions Live in Language

Many people find that feelings and memories from childhood or family life are tied to the language in which they happened. Words for some emotions do not translate neatly, and speaking in your first language can bring them closer to the surface in a helpful way.

Less Translating, More Talking

When therapy happens in a second language, part of your attention goes to finding the right words. In your first language, you can focus on what you want to say.

Culture and Context

A clinician who shares or deeply understands your cultural background may more easily understand family expectations, generational differences, religious or community values, and the stigma that can surround mental health. This is described on the page about feeling caught between two cultures.

Families and Multilingual Households

Some families speak different languages across generations. Therapy in a parent's or grandparent's language can make it easier to involve family members when that is helpful.

Common Concerns People Bring

People come to therapy in their first language for the same reasons as anyone else, including anxiety, depression, relationship and family conflict, stress, grief, and trauma. Some also want support with experiences that are closely tied to culture and migration, such as adjusting to life in a new country, acculturation stress, intergenerational conflict, or worries about what family or community might think about seeking help.

Stigma and Privacy

In many communities, mental health is still a sensitive topic. Some people worry that others will find out they are in therapy. Therapy is confidential, with limited exceptions that your clinician will explain, as described on the page about whether therapy is confidential. Virtual sessions can offer additional privacy for people who prefer not to be seen at a clinic.

Assessments in Other Languages

Psychological assessments can include interviews and feedback in your preferred language. Some standardized tests are only available in English or have been developed mainly with English speakers, so your psychologist will discuss which measures are appropriate and how language and culture are taken into account when interpreting results.
