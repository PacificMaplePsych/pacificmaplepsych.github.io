Why Do I Feel Sad Around the Holidays

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Everyone around you seems excited. The music, the decorations, the parties, the messages about joy and togetherness. But you feel heavy, tearful, or just flat. Maybe you dread family gatherings, miss someone who is no longer here, or feel painfully aware of being alone. You might feel guilty for not feeling festive, which only adds to the weight.

Feeling sad around the holidays is very common. The season brings high expectations, strong memories, and extra pressures, and it often falls during the darkest time of year in Canada.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Grief. Holidays often highlight who is missing. The first holiday after a loss can be especially hard, but grief can return even many years later, as described in Why Do I Still Feel Grief Years Later.

Loneliness. Being apart from family, living alone, or having few close connections can feel sharper when everyone else seems surrounded by people. Loneliness can be especially strong after moving to a new city.

Family stress. Tension, old conflicts, and difficult relatives can make gatherings feel draining rather than joyful.

Financial pressure. Gifts, travel, and hosting add costs that can leave people feeling anxious about money.

Seasonal changes. Shorter days and less sunlight can affect mood, energy, and sleep, and for some people this becomes seasonal depression.

Unmet expectations. The gap between how the holidays are supposed to feel and how they actually feel can bring disappointment.

When It Looks More Like a Difficult Life Transition or Seasonal Depression

The holidays often act as a marker of change: the first year after a divorce, a move, a loss, an empty nest, or a family estrangement. These transitions can make familiar traditions feel painful or out of place, much like feeling lost after a big life change.

For some people, low mood follows a seasonal pattern, starting in the fall or winter and lifting in spring. This is known as seasonal affective disorder, a form of depression. Signs include:

  • Low mood most days during the darker months.
  • Sleeping more and still feeling tired.
  • Craving carbohydrates and gaining weight.
  • Withdrawing from friends and activities.
  • Difficulty concentrating.

If sadness lasts beyond the holiday period, or you notice this pattern every year, it is worth speaking with a doctor or mental health professional.

What Can Help

  • Adjust expectations. You do not have to feel joyful. Allow the season to be what it is this year.
  • Make space for grief. Lighting a candle, sharing memories, or creating a new tradition can honour someone you miss.
  • Set boundaries. It is okay to limit time at gatherings, decline invitations, or set a budget.
  • Plan connection. Reach out to friends, volunteer, or join community events if you expect to be alone.
  • Get light and movement. Daylight walks and regular activity support mood through the darker months.

Common Questions About Holiday Sadness

Why do the holidays make me sad?

Holiday sadness can come from grief, loneliness, family tension, financial pressure, shorter days, and the gap between expectations and reality. It is very common.

Is holiday depression real?

Many people experience low mood during the holidays. For most, it eases after the season. When it lasts longer or follows a yearly pattern, it may be depression or seasonal affective disorder.

How can I cope with grief during the holidays?

Allow yourself to feel it, create small rituals to honour the person, share memories with others, and adjust traditions if needed. Support from others or a therapist can also help.

What if I'm alone for the holidays?

Plan ahead with activities you enjoy, reach out to friends by phone or video, volunteer, or join community events. It is okay to treat the day as an ordinary day if that feels easier.

How do I handle stressful family gatherings?

Set limits on how long you stay, plan breaks, avoid known conflict topics, and have an exit plan. Arranging support, such as a friend to text, can help.

What is seasonal affective disorder?

Seasonal affective disorder is a type of depression that follows a seasonal pattern, usually beginning in fall or winter and easing in spring. It is linked to reduced daylight.

Why do I feel let down after the holidays?

After weeks of build-up, many people feel a dip once the holidays end, especially with the return to routine and dark January days. This usually passes, but persistent low mood deserves attention.

How can I manage holiday spending stress?

Set a budget in advance, suggest gift exchanges or experiences instead of presents, and remember that connection matters more than cost. Talking openly with family can ease pressure.

Can the holidays be harder after a divorce or separation?

Yes. The first holidays after a divorce or separation can bring grief, loneliness, and changes to traditions or time with children. Creating new rituals can help.

When should I get help for sadness around the holidays?

Seek support if low mood lasts more than two weeks, affects daily life, follows a yearly pattern, or comes with hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm.
