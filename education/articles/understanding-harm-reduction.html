Understanding Harm Reduction

If someone is unresponsive, breathing very slowly, or you suspect an overdose, call 911 right away. In British Columbia, free naloxone kits and training are available at many pharmacies and community sites. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Harm reduction is an approach to substance use that focuses on reducing the health, social, and safety risks of using, rather than requiring people to stop. It meets people where they are, treats them with dignity, and supports any positive change, whether that is using more safely, using less, or stopping. Harm reduction includes practical measures, such as naloxone to reverse opioid overdoses, drug checking services, and never using alone, as well as a respectful, non-judgmental attitude in health care and therapy. Research shows harm reduction saves lives, reduces disease transmission, and helps connect people with care. It is part of public health policy in Canada.

How Harm Reduction Works

Harm reduction is based on several principles:

  • People who use substances deserve respect and care. Stigma and shame push people away from help.
  • Any positive change counts. Using less, using more safely, or getting health care are meaningful steps, even without abstinence.
  • People are experts on their own lives. Goals are set by the person, not imposed.
  • Safety comes first. Reducing the risk of overdose, infection, and other harms is a priority.
  • Social factors matter. Housing, poverty, trauma, and discrimination affect substance use and its risks.

In BC, the unregulated drug supply is often contaminated with powerful substances such as fentanyl, which greatly increases overdose risk. Harm reduction responds to this reality with practical tools that help keep people alive while they consider their next steps.

What It Looks Like in Practice

Community and public health measures. These include naloxone kits and training, drug checking services that test substances for dangerous contents, supervised consumption and overdose prevention sites, sterile supply programs, and access to medications for opioid use disorder prescribed by a physician. Some services and phone lines offer support for people who use alone.

Everyday safety steps. Common harm reduction advice includes not using alone, carrying naloxone, avoiding mixing substances, being cautious with a new supply, and knowing the signs of an overdose.

Alcohol and other substances. Harm reduction also applies to alcohol and cannabis, such as setting limits, alternating with water, avoiding driving, and planning safe transportation. The page about cutting back on drinking offers related strategies.

Harm reduction in therapy. A harm reduction-informed therapist does not require abstinence to start treatment. They help people explore their relationship with substances, set their own goals, and address underlying stressors such as trauma, anxiety, or depression. Therapy approaches such as motivational interviewing and CBT, described on the page about therapy approaches for substance use, fit well with a harm reduction stance.

Who It Can Help

Harm reduction can help anyone who uses substances, whether occasionally or daily, and whatever their goals. It is especially important for people who use opioids or other drugs from the unregulated supply. It helps people who are not ready or able to stop, and those who want to cut back gradually. It also supports family members, who can learn to recognize overdose, carry naloxone, and stay connected without judgment.

Harm reduction and abstinence are not opposites. Many people move between goals over time, and some choose abstinence after starting with harm reduction. Planning for high-risk situations and setbacks, described on the page about relapse prevention planning, can support any goal. People with physical dependence on alcohol or certain other substances should talk to a doctor before stopping, since withdrawal can be dangerous.

Common Questions About Harm Reduction

What does harm reduction mean for substance use?

Harm reduction means reducing the health and safety risks of substance use without requiring people to stop, while supporting any positive change.

Does harm reduction encourage people to use drugs?

No. Research shows harm reduction saves lives and connects people with care without increasing drug use.

Where can I get a naloxone kit in BC?

Free naloxone kits and training are available at many BC pharmacies and community harm reduction sites.

What is drug checking in harm reduction?

Drug checking tests substances for dangerous contents such as fentanyl, a harm reduction service available in some BC communities.

Why is using alone risky for people who use drugs?

Using drugs alone means no one can respond with naloxone or call 911 if an overdose happens.

Can harm reduction apply to alcohol?

Yes. Harm reduction for alcohol includes setting limits, alternating with water, avoiding driving, and planning safe transportation.

Do I have to quit substances to start therapy?

No. A harm reduction-informed therapist can support you whatever your substance use goals are.

Are harm reduction and abstinence opposites?

No. Many people move between harm reduction and abstinence goals over time, and both can be supported.

How can families use a harm reduction approach with a loved one?

Families can learn overdose signs, carry naloxone, stay connected without judgment, and support any positive change in substance use.

What are signs of an opioid overdose?

Signs of an opioid overdose include unresponsiveness, very slow or stopped breathing, and blue lips or fingertips. Call 911 right away.
