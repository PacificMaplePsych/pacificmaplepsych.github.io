What Is Complex PTSD (C-PTSD)

Complex post-traumatic stress disorder, often called complex PTSD or C-PTSD, is a condition that can develop after prolonged or repeated trauma, particularly trauma from which escape was difficult or impossible. Examples include ongoing childhood abuse or neglect, long-term domestic violence, human trafficking, and captivity. Complex PTSD is recognized in the World Health Organization's ICD-11 classification. It includes the core symptoms of PTSD, along with additional difficulties in emotional regulation, self-concept, and relationships. Complex PTSD reflects how deeply long-term trauma can affect a person, and it can be treated with patient, trauma-informed care.

What Complex PTSD Looks Like

Core PTSD symptoms:

  • Re-experiencing trauma through intrusive memories, nightmares, or flashbacks.
  • Avoiding reminders of the trauma.
  • A persistent sense of threat, including hypervigilance and startle responses.

Additional features of complex PTSD:

  • Emotional dysregulation. Intense emotions that are hard to manage, or feeling numb and shut down.
  • Negative self-concept. Deep shame, guilt, or a sense of being worthless, damaged, or different.
  • Relationship difficulties. Trouble trusting others, feeling close, or maintaining relationships, sometimes with patterns described on the page about trauma bonds.

People with complex PTSD may also experience dissociation, physical symptoms, and a loss of meaning or hope.

Why It Happens

When trauma is prolonged and inescapable, particularly during childhood or within important relationships, it can shape how a person develops. Children who experience ongoing abuse or neglect may not have the chance to develop a secure sense of self, trust in others, or skills to manage emotions. Emotional neglect can contribute alongside other harms. In adulthood, long-term abuse or captivity can erode a person's sense of identity and safety.

The nervous system adapts to constant threat. Survival responses such as fight, flight, freeze, and appeasing become ingrained. These adaptations helped a person survive, but they can continue long after the danger has ended, affecting daily life and relationships. The page on trauma explores these responses further.

Complex PTSD is sometimes confused with other conditions, such as borderline personality disorder, depression, or anxiety disorders, which can co-occur. The pattern of symptoms and history of prolonged trauma help clinicians understand what is happening. Many people with complex PTSD have received other diagnoses before the trauma is recognized.

Many people with complex PTSD describe emotional flashbacks, sudden waves of fear, shame, or helplessness that feel like the past, without a clear memory attached. Learning to recognize these moments as trauma responses is often an early step in recovery.

What It Is Not

  • Not a personal weakness. It reflects the impact of prolonged harm.
  • Not the same as PTSD from a single event, though it includes PTSD symptoms.
  • Not your fault. People with complex PTSD often carry shame that belongs to those who caused harm.
  • Not untreatable. Recovery is possible, though it often takes time.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A trauma-informed clinician will explore symptoms and history gently, prioritizing safety and choice. Treatment is often phased. The first phase focuses on safety and stabilization: building skills to manage emotions, grounding, and developing a sense of safety in daily life and in the therapeutic relationship. The second phase may involve processing traumatic memories using approaches such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive processing therapy, or other trauma therapies. The third phase focuses on reconnecting with relationships, identity, and meaning. Skills from dialectical behaviour therapy and compassion-focused therapy are often helpful. Recovery is usually gradual, and a consistent, trusting therapeutic relationship is central.

Common Questions About Complex PTSD

Can years of abuse cause something different from ordinary PTSD?

Yes. Complex PTSD develops after prolonged or repeated trauma and includes PTSD symptoms plus difficulties with emotions, self-worth, and relationships.

Which symptoms does complex PTSD add to PTSD?

It adds emotional dysregulation, negative self-concept, and relationship difficulties.

Is complex PTSD an official diagnosis?

Yes. It is recognized in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, though not as a separate diagnosis in the DSM-5.

What kinds of trauma lead to complex PTSD?

Prolonged, repeated trauma such as childhood abuse, neglect, domestic violence, or captivity can lead to complex PTSD.

Why do I feel so much shame after trauma?

Long-term trauma can shape a negative self-concept, leaving people feeling worthless or damaged, even though the harm was not their fault.

Is complex PTSD the same as borderline personality disorder?

No, though they can look similar and sometimes co-occur. Complex PTSD is rooted in prolonged trauma.

What is phased treatment for complex trauma?

It involves stabilization and safety, then processing traumatic memories, then reconnecting with relationships and identity.

Can complex PTSD affect relationships?

Yes. Trust, closeness, and emotional regulation can be difficult, and people may repeat familiar relationship patterns.

Why does healing from long-term trauma often take time?

Treatment often takes longer than for single-event PTSD, because it addresses deep-rooted patterns in emotions, identity, and relationships.

Can people recover from complex PTSD?

Yes. With patient, trauma-informed care, many people build safety, self-worth, and healthier relationships.
