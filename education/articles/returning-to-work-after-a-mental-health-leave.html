Returning to Work After a Mental Health Leave

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Returning to work after a mental health leave usually goes best with a gradual, planned approach rather than an abrupt return to full duties. A graduated return to work might start with shorter days or fewer tasks and build up over several weeks. Preparing ahead, such as practising a daily routine, agreeing on accommodations, and deciding what you will say to colleagues, can ease the transition. It is normal to feel nervous, and some ups and downs are common in the first weeks. Continuing therapy during the return helps you notice early warning signs, handle stress, and keep the gains you made during your leave.

Why a Planned Return Helps

Going straight back to full hours and responsibilities after a leave can quickly reintroduce the pressures that contributed to the problem. A gradual return gives you time to rebuild stamina and confidence while showing you and your employer what supports are needed. It also reduces the chance of a relapse, as described on the page about relapse prevention planning.

Preparing to Return

1. Rebuild Your Routine

A week or two before returning, practise waking at your work time, getting ready, and structuring your day.

2. Talk With Your Health Care Provider

Discuss readiness, recommended limitations, and any documentation your employer or insurer needs.

3. Plan With Your Employer

Many workplaces hold a return-to-work meeting with human resources, a manager, or a disability case manager. A written plan can outline hours, duties, and check-in dates.

4. Identify Accommodations

Examples include gradually increasing hours, flexible start times, a quieter workspace, temporarily reduced workload, regular check-ins, or time off for therapy appointments.

5. Prepare What to Say

You do not have to share your diagnosis with colleagues. A simple statement, such as "I was off for health reasons, and I am glad to be back," is enough.

6. Plan for Tough Days

Write down early warning signs and steps you will take, such as using a break, talking to your manager, or contacting your therapist.

Common Challenges

Anxiety about the first day, worry about what colleagues think, fatigue, and fear of falling behind are common. Some people feel pressure to prove themselves by working extra hard, which can lead back toward burnout, as described on the page about preventing burnout and building sustainable work habits. If the workplace itself contributed to the leave, such as through bullying or chronic overload, those issues need attention too, as described on the page about workplace stress.

When the Return Is Not Going Well

If symptoms return or worsen, it is better to raise it early than to push through. Your plan may need adjusting, such as slowing the pace or changing duties. A setback is not a failure. Sometimes, a return to the same role is not the right fit, and exploring other options is part of recovery, as described on the page about navigating a career change with support.

How Therapy Helps

Therapy during your return can help you manage anxiety, set boundaries, communicate needs, and keep healthy routines. The page about how to talk to your employer about burnout offers communication tips, and the page about taking a mental health leave from work in BC explains the leave process.

Common Questions About Returning to Work After a Leave

How do I return to work after a mental health leave?

Return to work after a mental health leave with a gradual plan, agreed accommodations, a rebuilt routine, and ongoing support from your health care provider.

What is a graduated return to work?

A graduated return to work starts with reduced hours or duties and builds up over several weeks to full duties.

What accommodations help after a mental health leave?

Helpful accommodations include gradually increasing hours, flexible start times, reduced workload, regular check-ins, and time for appointments.

What should I tell coworkers after a mental health leave?

You do not have to share your diagnosis. A simple statement, such as being off for health reasons, is enough.

Is it normal to feel anxious about returning to work?

Yes. Anxiety about returning to work after a mental health leave is very common and usually eases as you settle back in.

What if my symptoms come back after returning to work?

If symptoms return, raise it early with your health care provider and employer so your return-to-work plan can be adjusted.

How long does a graduated return to work take?

A graduated return to work often takes several weeks, though the timeline depends on your health and job.

Should I keep going to therapy after returning to work?

Yes. Continuing therapy after returning to work helps manage stress, notice warning signs, and prevent relapse.

Can my employer refuse a gradual return to work in BC?

Employers in BC have a duty to accommodate mental health conditions to the point of undue hardship, which often includes a gradual return.

What if I cannot return to the same job after a mental health leave?

If returning to the same job is not right, exploring other roles or career options can be part of a healthy recovery.
