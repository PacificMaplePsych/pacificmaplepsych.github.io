Why Do I Need to Repeat Certain Movements or Sounds

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Needing to repeat certain movements or sounds is very common, and it usually serves a purpose for the nervous system, such as calming, focusing, expressing emotion, or relieving tension. In autism, these repetitive behaviours are often called stimming. Repetition can also come from ADHD fidgeting, nervous habits, tics, or the compulsions of OCD. The key differences lie in how the behaviour feels, what drives it, and whether it brings comfort or distress.

People describe rocking, tapping their fingers, humming, repeating a phrase, clicking a pen, flapping their hands when excited, or feeling a pull to repeat a word until it sounds right.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Fidgeting. Many people bounce a leg or tap when concentrating or bored. People with ADHD often find that movement helps them focus, as described on the page about why it is so hard to sit still.

Nervous habits. Anxiety often shows up as repetitive movements, such as picking at skin, biting nails, or tapping, particularly during stress.

Tics. Tics are sudden, repetitive movements or sounds, such as blinking, throat clearing, or sniffing, often preceded by an uncomfortable urge. They are common in childhood and can continue into adulthood, and Tourette syndrome involves both motor and vocal tics.

OCD compulsions. Some people repeat actions to prevent something bad or until it feels right. This is covered on the pages about doing things a certain number of times and needing things to feel just right.

Medical or medication effects. Some medications and neurological conditions can cause repetitive movements. New or unusual movements should be checked by a doctor.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Stimming, short for self-stimulatory behaviour, is a common part of autism. It helps regulate sensory input and emotions, whether calming overwhelm, expressing joy, or helping with focus. Signs that repetition may be autistic stimming include:

  • The movement or sound feels good, calming, or satisfying rather than driven by fear.
  • It increases during strong emotions, both positive and negative, or in overwhelming environments.
  • You have done it since childhood, sometimes learning to hide it.
  • It sits alongside other sensory differences, such as sensitivity to loud noises or textures.
  • Stopping it feels uncomfortable, like holding in a sneeze.
  • You notice other autistic traits, such as strong interests or a preference for routine.

Stimming is not harmful in itself and often helps autistic people function. Many autistic adults suppressed stims as children to avoid attention, which can add to the effort of masking. Neurodiversity-affirming approaches generally support safe stimming rather than trying to eliminate it. The exception is stims that cause injury, which may benefit from finding safer alternatives.

The main contrast with OCD is motivation: stims usually feel soothing or enjoyable, while compulsions are driven by anxiety and a sense that something bad will happen if they are not done.

What Can Help

  • Notice the function. Ask whether the repetition calms, focuses, expresses emotion, or relieves fear. The answer points to the likely cause.
  • Allow helpful stims. If the behaviour is safe and helps you regulate, there is usually no need to stop it.
  • Find discreet options. Fidget tools, textured objects, or small movements can meet the need in settings where you prefer privacy.
  • Reduce overload. If stimming increases in busy environments, sensory supports such as headphones may help.
  • Seek care for distress or harm. Repetition driven by fear, or that causes injury, deserves professional support.

Common Questions About Repeating Movements or Sounds

What is stimming?

Stimming, short for self-stimulatory behaviour, refers to repetitive movements or sounds, such as rocking, hand flapping, or humming. It helps regulate emotions and sensory input and is common in autism.

Do only autistic people stim?

No. Most people stim in small ways, such as tapping or twirling hair. In autism, stimming tends to be more frequent, intense, and important for regulation.

How is stimming different from a tic?

Stimming is usually voluntary and feels calming or enjoyable. Tics are sudden, often preceded by an uncomfortable urge, and feel less under voluntary control.

How can I tell if repeating actions is OCD or stimming?

Stims generally feel soothing or satisfying. OCD compulsions are driven by anxiety and a fear that something bad will happen, and they bring only temporary relief.

Is it okay for adults to stim in public?

Yes. Safe stimming is a natural way to regulate. Some people choose discreet stims in certain settings, but there is nothing wrong with stimming openly.

Why do I stim more when I am excited or stressed?

Strong emotions, both positive and negative, increase the need for regulation. Stimming helps release energy or calm the nervous system during these moments.

Can adults develop tics?

Most tic disorders begin in childhood, but tics can persist or re-emerge in adulthood, especially with stress. New tics in adulthood should be checked by a doctor.

Why did I learn to hide my repetitive movements?

Many autistic people were told to stop stimming as children or noticed others reacting, so they learned to suppress it. This masking can be exhausting over time.

Should I try to stop stimming?

Usually not, if it is safe and helps you. Neurodiversity-affirming approaches support stimming. If a stim causes harm, a therapist can help find safer alternatives.

How do clinicians look at stimming during an autism assessment?

Yes. Assessments explore repetitive movements, sounds, routines, and interests, along with sensory experiences and social communication, across the lifespan.
