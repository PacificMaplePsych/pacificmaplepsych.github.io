What Is Moral Injury

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Moral injury is the psychological, emotional, and spiritual distress that can follow experiences in which a person does, fails to prevent, or witnesses something that deeply violates their moral values, or feels betrayed by people in positions of authority. It was first described in military veterans and is now recognized in health care workers, first responders, police, and others in high-stakes roles. People with moral injury often feel guilt, shame, anger, betrayal, and a loss of trust or meaning. Moral injury is not a formal diagnosis, and it differs from PTSD, which is mainly driven by fear. The two can occur together. With support, people can work through moral injury and rebuild a sense of integrity and purpose.

What Can Cause Moral Injury

  • Acting against your values, such as following orders or policies that cause harm
  • Being unable to prevent harm, such as lacking resources to save a patient
  • Witnessing wrongdoing without being able to intervene
  • Betrayal by leaders or institutions, such as being put in impossible situations
  • Making impossible choices, where every option causes harm

Common Signs

  • Guilt about what you did or did not do
  • Shame and feeling like a bad person
  • Anger at yourself, others, or institutions
  • Betrayal and loss of trust
  • Withdrawal from others or activities
  • Loss of meaning or questioning beliefs
  • Self-punishing behaviours, such as overworking or substance use

How It Differs From PTSD

PTSD is centred on fear and threat, with symptoms such as flashbacks, hypervigilance, and avoidance, as described on the page about what PTSD is. Moral injury is centred on guilt, shame, and betrayal. A person can have one, the other, or both. Treatments for PTSD may not fully address moral injury unless they also explore questions of values, responsibility, and forgiveness.

Moral Distress and Moral Injury

Moral distress describes the discomfort of knowing the right thing to do but being prevented from doing it, for example because of policies, staffing shortages, or lack of resources. It often happens repeatedly in workplaces such as hospitals, care homes, and emergency services. When moral distress is severe, prolonged, or linked to a significant event, it can develop into moral injury, a deeper wound to a person's sense of self and trust. Recognizing moral distress early, talking about it with colleagues, and advocating for change where possible may help prevent it from becoming moral injury. Organizations also play a role. Workplaces that acknowledge ethical challenges, offer debriefing, and involve staff in decisions can reduce the moral burden carried by individuals.

Who Is at Risk

  • Health care workers, especially during periods of overwhelming demand
  • First responders and police, as described on the page about first responder mental health in BC
  • Military members and veterans
  • Social workers, teachers, and others working within systems with limited resources

Moral injury often overlaps with burnout and vicarious trauma, as described on the page about burnout in helping professions.

What It Is Not

Moral injury is not a sign that someone is a bad person. In fact, it arises because people care deeply about doing the right thing. It is also not simply burnout, although the two are related.

What Helps

  • Naming the experience. Understanding moral injury can reduce isolation.
  • Talking with trusted others, including peers who share similar experiences.
  • Exploring responsibility realistically, distinguishing what was within your control from what was not.
  • Self-compassion and forgiveness, where appropriate, as described on the page about self-compassion and compassion-focused therapy.
  • Making amends or acting on values in meaningful ways.
  • Therapy that addresses guilt, shame, and meaning, alongside trauma symptoms if present.

Common Questions About Moral Injury

Was moral injury first studied in military veterans?

Yes. Moral injury was first described in military veterans and is now recognized in health care workers, first responders, and others.

Can someone have moral injury without PTSD?

Yes. Moral injury centres on guilt, shame, and betrayal, while PTSD centres on fear, so a person can have one, the other, or both.

Who experiences moral injury?

Moral injury is common in military members, health care workers, first responders, police, and others in high-stakes roles.

What are signs of moral injury?

Signs of moral injury include guilt, shame, anger, betrayal, withdrawal, loss of meaning, and self-punishing behaviours.

Is moral injury a diagnosis?

Moral injury is not a formal diagnosis, but it is a recognized and well-studied concept.

Can health care workers experience moral injury?

Yes. Health care workers can experience moral injury when they cannot provide the care they believe patients need.

How is moral injury treated?

Moral injury is treated with therapy that explores guilt, shame, responsibility, self-compassion, and values, alongside trauma treatment if needed.

Is moral injury the same as burnout?

No. Moral injury involves violated values and betrayal, while burnout involves exhaustion and cynicism, though they often overlap.

Can moral injury heal?

Yes. With support, people can work through moral injury and rebuild a sense of integrity and purpose.

Does moral injury mean I did something wrong?

Not necessarily. Moral injury often arises from impossible situations and reflects how deeply you care about doing right.
