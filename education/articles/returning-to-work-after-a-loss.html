Returning to Work After a Loss

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Returning to work after a loss usually goes more smoothly when you plan the return with your employer, consider a gradual start, decide ahead of time what you want to share with colleagues, and expect that grief will affect your concentration and energy for a while. Bereavement leave is often short, and many people go back to work long before they feel ready. Work can offer structure and distraction, but it can also bring painful reminders and pressure to act normal. It is common to feel foggy, emotional, or exhausted in the first weeks and months. These are normal parts of grief, not signs of failure.

Why Planning Your Return Helps

Grief affects the body and mind. Many grieving people notice trouble concentrating, forgetfulness, disrupted sleep, fatigue, and sudden waves of emotion. These effects can make ordinary work tasks feel much harder than before. The broader grief experience is explained on the page about grief and its stages.

Workplaces may not know how to respond. Colleagues may say the wrong thing, avoid the subject, or expect you to be back to normal quickly. Planning ahead gives you more control, reduces surprises, and helps your employer support you. It also allows you to protect time for grief, appointments, and practical tasks after a death.

Strategies to Try

1. Talk With Your Employer Before Returning

Contact your manager or human resources to discuss your return date, workload, and any flexibility. Ask about bereavement leave, additional leave options, and employee assistance programs.

2. Consider a Gradual Return

Returning part-time, with lighter duties, or with more flexible hours at first can ease the transition. Some people start mid-week so the first stretch is shorter.

3. Decide What to Share

Choose how much you want colleagues to know and how you would like them to respond. Some people ask a manager to share the news before they return, along with a note such as "I appreciate kind words, but I may not want to talk about it at work."

4. Prepare for the First Day

Plan simple responses to condolences, such as "Thank you, it has been a hard time." Identify a quiet space where you can take a break if emotions rise.

5. Adjust Expectations

Concentration and memory may be affected for weeks or months. Write things down, use checklists, prioritize essential tasks, and ask for help when needed.

6. Plan for Hard Days

Anniversaries, birthdays, and holidays can bring grief back strongly, as described on the page about grief during holidays and anniversaries. Consider booking time off around these dates.

7. Take Breaks

Short walks, breathing breaks, or stepping outside can help when grief surges during the day.

8. Look After Yourself Outside Work

Rest, meals, movement, and time with supportive people help you cope with the demands of work while grieving.

Common Obstacles

Many people feel pressure to return quickly for financial or job security reasons. If this applies, it may help to discuss flexible arrangements rather than extended leave. Some workplaces offer only a few days of bereavement leave, which rarely matches how long grief affects functioning. In BC, employment standards provide some unpaid bereavement leave, and some employers offer more, so it is worth checking your contract or asking human resources.

Colleagues may not acknowledge the loss at all, which can feel hurtful, or may ask intrusive questions. Having a prepared response helps. Losses that others do not fully recognize, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pet, or a pregnancy, can be especially hard at work, as explored on the page about disenfranchised grief. Some people also find that their priorities change after a loss, and questions about career direction may arise. It can help to wait a while before making major decisions.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If grief makes it impossible to concentrate or function at work for a long time, if you feel hopeless, or if grief remains intense many months after the loss, support can help. Grief counselling offers space to process the loss and plan how to cope. Signs of prolonged grief are explained on the page about complicated and prolonged grief. A doctor can assess whether medical leave is needed. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Returning to Work After a Loss

How soon should I go back to work after someone dies?

There is no right time to return to work after a death. Many people go back when leave ends, and a gradual return can make the transition easier.

What should I say to my manager before returning to work after a loss?

Discuss your return date, workload, and flexibility, and ask about bereavement leave and employee assistance programs.

Can I ask for a gradual return to work after a death in my family?

Many employers can arrange part-time hours, lighter duties, or flexible schedules for a gradual return after a loss.

How do I handle condolences from coworkers when I am grieving?

Prepare a simple response, such as thanking them and saying it has been hard, and let your manager share any boundaries you want.

Why is it so hard to concentrate at work while I am grieving?

Grief often affects concentration, memory, and energy for weeks or months, so work tasks may feel much harder than usual.

How much bereavement leave do workers in BC get?

BC employment standards provide some unpaid bereavement leave, and some employers offer more, so check your contract or ask human resources.

Should I tell my whole team about my loss?

It is your choice. Some grieving people ask a manager to share the news and explain how they would like colleagues to respond.

What if grief hits me suddenly at work?

Identify a quiet space ahead of time, take a short break, breathe slowly, and return when you feel steadier.

Should I make big career decisions soon after a loss?

Grief can change priorities, but it often helps to wait a while after a loss before making major career decisions.

When should I get support for grief that is affecting my work?

If grief makes it very hard to function at work for a long time, or you feel hopeless, grief counselling or medical support can help.
