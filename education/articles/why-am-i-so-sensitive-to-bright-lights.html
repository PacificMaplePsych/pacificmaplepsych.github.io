Why Am I So Sensitive to Bright Lights

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Fluorescent office lights feel like they are buzzing inside your head. Stepping outside on a sunny day makes you squint and wince. Screens at full brightness, headlights at night, or flickering bulbs in a store can leave you tired, irritable, or with a headache that lasts for hours. Other people do not seem to notice, and you might wonder why it bothers you so much.

Light sensitivity, sometimes called photophobia, is surprisingly common. It can have medical causes worth checking, and it can also be part of how some people's nervous systems process sensory information.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Migraine. Sensitivity to light is one of the hallmark features of migraine, both during attacks and, for some people, between them. If light sensitivity comes with headaches, nausea, or visual disturbances, migraine is a likely factor. Stress-related headaches are also covered in Why Do I Get Headaches or Stomach Aches When I'm Stressed.

Eye conditions. Dry eyes, eye inflammation, cataracts, and some other eye problems can increase sensitivity to light. An optometrist or eye doctor can check for these.

Concussion. Light sensitivity is common after a head injury and can persist for weeks or months in some people.

Medications. Some medications can make eyes more sensitive to light as a side effect.

Anxiety and stress. A nervous system on high alert tends to be more reactive to all kinds of input, including light. When you feel on edge, bright environments can feel harsher.

If light sensitivity is sudden or severe, or comes with eye pain, vision changes, a stiff neck, fever, or a severe headache, please seek medical care promptly.

When It Looks More Like Autism

Sensory differences are a core part of autism, and sensitivity to light is one of the more commonly reported. Autistic people may notice the flicker of fluorescent lights that others do not see, find bright or harsh lighting physically uncomfortable, or feel drained after time in visually busy environments like shopping centres.

For many autistic adults, light sensitivity is part of a broader sensory profile. It often sits alongside sensitivity to sound, touch, and smell, and it contributes to the overall load that can lead to overwhelm, shutdown, or meltdown. Busy, bright, noisy environments combine several sources of input at once, which is why places like crowded stores or events can feel especially hard.

Signs that autistic sensory processing may be involved include:

  • Light sensitivity that has been present since childhood rather than starting after an injury or illness.
  • Strong preferences for dim or natural lighting, sunglasses indoors, or hats with brims.
  • Other sensory sensitivities, such as being overwhelmed by loud noises or sensitivity to certain smells.
  • Feeling exhausted or irritable after time in brightly lit, busy places.
  • Differences in social communication or a strong preference for routine.

What Can Help

  • Get a medical and eye check first, especially if the sensitivity is new or getting worse.
  • Adjust your environment. Warm, dimmable lamps, blinds, and avoiding overhead fluorescent lighting can make a big difference at home.
  • Use screen settings. Lower brightness, dark mode, and night filters reduce strain from devices.
  • Carry tools. Sunglasses, tinted lenses, or a brimmed hat can make outings more comfortable.
  • Ask for workplace adjustments. Desk lamps instead of overhead lights, a seat near a window, or permission to wear tinted glasses are reasonable requests.

Common Questions About Light Sensitivity

What is photophobia?

Photophobia is the medical term for sensitivity or discomfort in response to light. It is a symptom rather than a condition and can be linked to migraine, eye problems, concussion, medications, or sensory processing differences.

Why do many autistic adults find bright lighting uncomfortable?

Autistic brains often filter sensory input less automatically, so harsh light, glare, and flicker can register as intense or even painful. On its own, light sensitivity does not mean someone is autistic, but it is common as part of a broader lifelong sensory profile.

Why do fluorescent lights bother me so much?

Fluorescent lights can flicker and hum in ways some people notice strongly, particularly those with migraine or autistic sensory differences. The harsh, even quality of the light can also feel uncomfortable.

Can migraines cause light sensitivity between headaches?

Yes. Some people with migraine remain more sensitive to light even between attacks, and bright or flickering light can also trigger attacks in some people.

When is light sensitivity a medical emergency?

Seek urgent care if light sensitivity is sudden and severe, or comes with eye pain, vision loss, a stiff neck, fever, confusion, or a sudden severe headache. These can signal conditions that need immediate treatment.

Can anxiety make me more sensitive to light?

Yes. When the nervous system is on high alert, it tends to react more strongly to sensory input of all kinds. Reducing overall stress can make lights feel less harsh for some people.

Is it okay to wear sunglasses indoors for light sensitivity?

For many people, sunglasses or tinted lenses indoors are a practical way to manage discomfort. An eye care professional can suggest lens tints that reduce glare without making indoor spaces too dark.

Can a concussion cause lasting light sensitivity?

Light sensitivity is common after concussion and often improves over weeks to months. If it persists, a doctor or concussion specialist can help with gradual rehabilitation.

What workplace accommodations help with light sensitivity?

Common adjustments include desk lamps in place of overhead lights, seating near natural light, screen filters, permission to wear tinted glasses, and the option to work in a quieter, dimmer space.

How are autistic sensory sensitivities assessed?

Adult autism assessments usually explore sensory experiences across sight, sound, touch, taste, and smell, alongside social communication, routines, and developmental history, to understand the overall pattern.
