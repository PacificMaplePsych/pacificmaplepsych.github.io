How to Know If You Need a Higher Level of Care Than Weekly Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are thinking about suicide, cannot keep yourself safe, or are experiencing a medical emergency, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline), call 911, or go to the nearest emergency department. Youth can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

You may need a higher level of care than weekly therapy if you are at risk of harming yourself or others, are unable to manage daily life such as eating, sleeping, or basic self-care, have symptoms that are worsening despite regular therapy, are experiencing psychosis or severe mania, have a medical risk from an eating disorder or substance use, or need more frequent support than one session a week can provide. Higher levels of care include more frequent therapy, intensive outpatient or day programs, specialized treatment programs, and hospital care. Needing more support is not a failure. It simply means matching the intensity of care to what you need right now.

How It Works

Mental health care is often described as a range from less to more intensive:

  • Weekly or less frequent outpatient therapy. Suitable for most people who are safe and functioning, even if struggling.
  • More frequent outpatient sessions. Twice-weekly therapy or adding a psychiatrist or group program.
  • Intensive outpatient or day programs. Several hours of treatment a few days a week while living at home.
  • Specialized programs. Such as eating disorder treatment, early psychosis intervention, or substance use treatment, including medically supervised withdrawal.
  • Inpatient or residential care. Twenty-four-hour support for people at high risk or who need close medical or psychiatric monitoring.

People can move up or down this range as their needs change, often returning to weekly therapy after a period of more intensive care.

Practical Details

Signs that more support may be needed.

  • Frequent or worsening thoughts of suicide or self-harm.
  • Symptoms getting worse over several weeks despite regular therapy.
  • Not eating, sleeping very little, or being unable to manage basic tasks.
  • Hearing or seeing things others do not, or severe changes in mood and energy, as described on the page about psychosis.
  • Physical warning signs from an eating disorder, such as fainting or rapid weight change.
  • Substance use with withdrawal symptoms or overdose risk, as discussed on the page about support for substance use concerns.

Options in BC. Publicly funded mental health and substance use services are available through health authorities, including specialized programs, often with a referral from a family doctor. Hospital emergency departments provide urgent assessment. Some private intensive programs also exist. Availability and wait times vary by region.

Your therapist's role. An outpatient therapist can help you recognize when more care is needed, support referrals, and coordinate with your doctor. They may continue seeing you alongside other services or resume therapy afterward.

How to Take the Next Step

  • Talk openly with your therapist. Share honestly how you are doing between sessions.
  • See your family doctor. They can assess physical health and refer to psychiatry or specialized programs, as described on the page about talking to your doctor about a referral.
  • Use crisis services when needed. Do not wait for a referral in an emergency.
  • Involve trusted people. Family or friends can help with appointments and safety.
  • Make a safety plan. List warning signs, coping steps, and people and services to contact.

If you are unsure whether your current therapy is helping, the page about how to know if therapy is working may also help.

Common Questions About Needing More Than Weekly Therapy

How do I know if I need more than weekly therapy?

You may need more than weekly therapy if you are at risk of harm, cannot manage daily life, have worsening symptoms, or need more frequent support.

What is a higher level of care in mental health?

A higher level of care means more intensive support than weekly therapy, such as frequent sessions, day programs, specialized treatment, or hospital care.

What is an intensive outpatient or day program?

An intensive outpatient or day program provides several hours of mental health treatment a few days a week while you continue living at home.

When should I go to the hospital for mental health?

Go to the hospital if you cannot keep yourself safe, are thinking about suicide, have psychosis or severe mania, or face a medical emergency.

Is needing a higher level of care a sign that therapy failed?

No. Needing a higher level of care simply means matching treatment intensity to your current needs, and many people later return to weekly therapy.

Can my therapist help me access intensive care?

Yes. An outpatient therapist can help you recognize when more intensive care is needed, support referrals, and coordinate with your doctor.

Are intensive mental health programs publicly funded in BC?

BC health authorities offer publicly funded mental health and substance use programs, often with a referral. Availability and waits vary by region.

What warning signs suggest an eating disorder needs more intensive care?

Fainting, rapid weight change, or other physical warning signs suggest an eating disorder may need more intensive care than weekly therapy.

Can I keep seeing my therapist during a day program?

Sometimes. Your outpatient therapist may continue alongside a day program or resume sessions afterward, depending on the program.

What is a safety plan when weekly therapy is not enough?

A safety plan lists warning signs, coping steps, and people and crisis services to contact when weekly therapy is not enough to stay safe.
