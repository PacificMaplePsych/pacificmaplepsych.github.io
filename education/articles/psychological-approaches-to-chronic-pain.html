Psychological Approaches to Chronic Pain

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Psychological approaches to chronic pain, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and mindfulness-based approaches, help people reduce the impact of pain on their lives, manage distress, and improve daily functioning. These approaches do not suggest that pain is imagined. Chronic pain is real, and it involves complex interactions between the body, brain, and nervous system. Emotions, stress, sleep, and attention all influence how pain is experienced. Psychological therapies are recommended in many chronic pain guidelines and work best as part of a broader plan that includes medical care, physical therapy, and other supports.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy for chronic pain. CBT helps people understand how thoughts, emotions, and behaviours interact with pain. It includes skills such as pacing, relaxation, problem-solving, and addressing thoughts like "this will never get better." It has good research support for reducing distress and improving functioning.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people make room for pain rather than struggling against it, and focus on living in line with their values. It is well studied for chronic pain.

Mindfulness-based approaches. Mindfulness programs teach people to observe sensations with curiosity rather than alarm, which can reduce distress related to pain.

Pain reprocessing and pain education. Learning how the nervous system can become more sensitive over time, as explained on the page about the mind-body connection in chronic pain, can reduce fear. Some newer approaches focus on helping the brain interpret signals as less threatening, and research on these is growing.

Relaxation and biofeedback. Techniques such as relaxation training and biofeedback help reduce muscle tension and calm the stress response.

Trauma-focused therapy. For people whose pain is connected with trauma, or who have trauma histories that intensify distress, trauma-focused therapy may help.

Interdisciplinary pain programs. These combine psychological therapy with physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and medical care, and can be especially helpful for complex pain.

Medication and medical treatments are managed by physicians and are an important part of many plans.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan depends on the type and duration of pain, how it affects daily life, and co-occurring concerns such as depression, anxiety, sleep problems, or trauma. Chronic pain and mood often affect each other, as described on the page about depression and chronic illness. Personal goals matter. Some people want to return to work, others want to sleep better or feel less anxious. Past experiences with treatment and preferences about approach are also considered. Ideally, the therapist coordinates with the person's physician and other providers.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress is often measured in improved functioning and quality of life, not only in lower pain levels. People may notice they can do more activities, feel less fear about pain, sleep better, or feel less overwhelmed during flare-ups. Pain intensity may also decrease for some people, though not for everyone. Change is usually gradual, often over weeks to months. Flare-ups are a normal part of chronic pain and do not mean progress has been lost. Learning how to respond to flare-ups is part of the work.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Pacing activities, balancing activity and rest rather than overdoing it on good days and crashing afterward, can reduce flare-ups. Gentle, regular movement, as recommended by your care team, supports physical and emotional wellbeing. Protecting sleep helps, since poor sleep can intensify pain, as described on the page about insomnia. Staying connected with others and engaging in meaningful activities, even in adapted ways, reduces isolation. Practising relaxation and mindfulness regularly builds skills for difficult days. Treating yourself with patience matters, since living with pain is demanding.

If pain is sudden, severe, or different from your usual pain, seek medical care or call 911. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Therapy for Chronic Pain

Does being referred to therapy for pain mean my pain is imagined?

No. Chronic pain is real. Therapy helps with how pain affects your life, mood, and nervous system.

What would I actually practise in CBT sessions focused on my back pain?

CBT teaches skills such as pacing, relaxation, and managing unhelpful thoughts, which can reduce distress and improve functioning.

What is the goal of ACT for chronic pain?

ACT helps people make room for pain and focus on living in line with their values, rather than struggling against pain.

Can therapy reduce how much pain I feel?

Some people notice lower pain intensity, though the main benefits are often improved functioning and less distress.

I overdo it on good days and pay for it after. How do I break that pattern?

Activity pacing helps. It means balancing activity and rest, planning steady amounts each day rather than pushing hard on good days and crashing afterward.

Why do my pain flare-ups often follow a tense week at work?

Stress activates the nervous system and muscle tension, which can heighten pain sensitivity.

What is an interdisciplinary pain program?

It combines psychological therapy with physiotherapy, occupational therapy, and medical care.

How long does therapy for chronic pain take?

Change is usually gradual, often over weeks to months, depending on goals and needs.

Why does my pain seem worse when I sleep poorly?

Poor sleep can increase pain sensitivity, and pain can disrupt sleep, creating a cycle.

Should my therapist work with my doctor on chronic pain?

Ideally yes. Coordination between providers helps create a more effective plan.
