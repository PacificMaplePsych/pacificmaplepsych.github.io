Grounding Techniques for Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Grounding techniques are simple strategies that bring your attention back to the present moment when anxiety, panic, overwhelming emotions, or flashbacks pull you into worry or the past. They work through the senses, the body, and simple thinking tasks, such as noticing five things you can see, pressing your feet into the floor, or naming colours around you. Grounding does not make anxiety disappear, but it can reduce its intensity and help you feel more in control. These skills are easy to learn, can be used almost anywhere, and work best when practised regularly, not only during a crisis.

Why Grounding Helps

When anxiety rises, the brain's threat system takes over. Attention narrows toward danger, thoughts race, and the body prepares to fight or flee. People may feel detached, overwhelmed, or stuck in worst-case thinking. During panic attacks, physical sensations can feel frightening, which increases fear further. After trauma, emotional flashbacks can make the past feel present.

Grounding interrupts this cycle by shifting attention to neutral, concrete information in the present, such as sounds, textures, or your breath. This signals to the brain that you are safe right now. Grounding can help bring people back into their window of tolerance, the zone in which they can think clearly and manage emotions.

Strategies to Try

Sensory Grounding

  • 5-4-3-2-1. Name five things you can see, four you can touch, three you can hear, two you can smell, and one you can taste.
  • Temperature. Hold a warm mug of tea or wash your hands with warm water, noticing the sensation.
  • Texture. Carry a smooth stone, a soft fabric, or another comforting object to hold and explore with your fingers.
  • Scent. Smell a familiar, calming scent, such as a hand cream or essential oil.
  • Sound. Listen closely to the sounds around you, or play calming music.

Body Grounding

  • Feet on the floor. Press your feet into the ground and notice the support beneath you.
  • Slow breathing. Breathe in gently for four counts and out for six. Longer exhales can calm the body.
  • Movement. Walk slowly, stretch, or shake out your hands.
  • Muscle tension and release. Tense and relax muscles, as in progressive muscle relaxation.

Mental Grounding

  • Describe your surroundings. Describe the room in detail, silently or aloud.
  • Categories. Name as many animals, cities, or foods as you can, or count backward from one hundred by sevens.
  • Orientation. Remind yourself of the date, where you are, and that you are safe now.
  • Grounding statement. Repeat a calming phrase, such as "This feeling will pass. I am safe right now."

Common Obstacles

Grounding can feel awkward or unhelpful at first, especially during intense anxiety. Practising when calm makes it easier to use during difficult moments. Some techniques may suit you better than others, so experimenting helps. Some people find that focusing on the breath or the body increases anxiety, especially after trauma. Keeping your eyes open, focusing on external senses, or choosing movement can help.

Grounding can become a way of avoiding feelings if it is used every time anxiety appears. It works best as a tool to bring intensity down so you can think clearly, not as a way to push feelings away entirely. For broader strategies, the page about stress management techniques may help.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If anxiety, panic, or flashbacks are frequent or intense, interfere with daily life, or lead you to avoid situations, therapy can help. Cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and trauma-focused therapies can address the causes of anxiety, not just the symptoms. If you have chest pain, trouble breathing, or symptoms that feel new or severe, seek medical care or call 911. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Grounding Techniques

What are grounding techniques?

They are simple strategies that bring attention back to the present using the senses, the body, or simple mental tasks.

How does the 5-4-3-2-1 technique work?

Name five things you see, four you can touch, three you hear, two you smell, and one you taste.

Can grounding help during a panic attack?

Yes. Grounding can shift attention away from frightening sensations and help the body calm down.

Why do grounding techniques work for anxiety?

They shift attention to neutral, present-moment information, signalling to the brain that you are safe right now.

What grounding techniques can I use in public?

Pressing your feet into the floor, holding a small object, slow breathing, or silently naming things you see are discreet options.

Why does focusing on my breathing make me more anxious?

Some people find internal focus increases anxiety. External senses, movement, or open-eyed techniques may help more.

Can grounding help with flashbacks?

Yes. Grounding can help remind you of the present and reduce the intensity of flashbacks.

Should I practise grounding when I am not anxious?

Yes. Practising when calm makes it easier to use grounding during difficult moments.

Can grounding become a form of avoidance?

It can if used to push away all feelings. It works best to reduce intensity so you can think clearly.

What should I carry for grounding on the go?

A smooth stone, soft fabric, calming scent, or mint can be useful sensory grounding tools.
