What Is Vicarious Trauma

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Vicarious trauma is a gradual change in a person's inner experience that results from repeated, empathetic engagement with other people's traumatic experiences. It is most often described among therapists, counsellors, social workers, health care workers, first responders, lawyers, journalists, and others who regularly hear about or witness suffering. Over time, this exposure can shift how a person sees themselves, others, and the world, affecting beliefs about safety, trust, control, and meaning. Vicarious trauma is not a personal failing. It is an occupational hazard of caring work, and it can be recognized, prevented, and addressed.

What Vicarious Trauma Looks Like

  • A growing sense that the world is dangerous and people cannot be trusted.
  • Intrusive images or thoughts related to clients' or patients' stories.
  • Feeling hypervigilant or overprotective of loved ones.
  • Emotional numbness or difficulty feeling joy.
  • Cynicism, hopelessness, or loss of meaning in work.
  • Sleep problems or nightmares.
  • Withdrawing from friends, family, or activities.
  • Reacting strongly to reminders, similar to trauma triggers.

Why It Happens

People in helping roles often listen closely to detailed accounts of violence, abuse, loss, and disaster. Empathy allows them to connect and help, but it also means they absorb some of the emotional weight of these experiences. When exposure is frequent and recovery time is limited, the cumulative effect can change a person's worldview. Researchers describe vicarious trauma as involving changes to core beliefs, not just temporary stress.

Risk increases with heavy trauma-related caseloads, limited supervision, working in isolation, organizational stress, and a personal history of trauma. Strong identification with clients and difficulty setting boundaries also add to risk. Vicarious trauma is closely related to secondary traumatic stress, which involves PTSD-like symptoms such as intrusive images, and to compassion fatigue, which centres on emotional exhaustion and reduced empathy. These experiences often overlap.

Vicarious trauma can affect home life as well. Helpers may find it difficult to talk about their work because of confidentiality or not wanting to burden loved ones, which can leave them carrying heavy material alone. Partners and friends may notice irritability, withdrawal, or increased worry about safety without understanding why.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as burnout. Burnout comes from general work stress and overload. Vicarious trauma specifically involves changes in beliefs after exposure to others' trauma. The two can occur together, as discussed on the page about burnout in helping professions.
  • Not a sign you are unsuited to your work. It reflects the demands of trauma exposure, often in people who care deeply.
  • Not permanent. Beliefs and wellbeing can be restored with support and changes to work practices.
  • Not only an individual problem. Organizations share responsibility for prevention.

How It Is Assessed and Addressed

Recognizing vicarious trauma often begins with noticing changes in beliefs, mood, sleep, or relationships. Regular clinical supervision and peer consultation provide space to process difficult material. Balancing caseloads, taking breaks between intense sessions, and protecting time off help reduce cumulative exposure. Personal therapy can help helpers process what they carry, address any personal trauma that is activated, and reconnect with meaning. Activities that restore a sense of safety, joy, and connection, such as time in nature, creative outlets, and relationships outside work, are important. Organizations can help through trauma-informed policies, reasonable workloads, and a culture where talking about vicarious trauma is normal.

Many helpers also describe vicarious resilience, the positive growth that can come from witnessing others' courage and recovery. Noticing these moments alongside the hard ones can help sustain meaning in the work.

Common Questions About Vicarious Trauma

Can listening to other people's trauma change how I see the world?

Yes. Vicarious trauma is a gradual change in beliefs about safety, trust, and meaning that results from repeated exposure to others' trauma.

Who is at risk of vicarious trauma?

Therapists, counsellors, social workers, health care workers, first responders, lawyers, and journalists are among those at higher risk.

Does vicarious trauma overlap with feeling emotionally drained by caring?

Often. Vicarious trauma centres on changes to worldview and beliefs, while compassion fatigue, which frequently occurs alongside it, centres on emotional exhaustion and reduced empathy.

Can I develop PTSD-like symptoms from hearing about trauma secondhand?

Yes. Called secondary traumatic stress, this involves PTSD-like symptoms, such as intrusive images, from indirect exposure to trauma through others.

Can vicarious trauma cause nightmares?

Yes. Nightmares, intrusive images, and sleep problems can occur after repeated exposure to others' traumatic stories.

Why do I feel the world is more dangerous since starting my job?

Repeated exposure to trauma can shift beliefs about safety and trust, which is a core feature of vicarious trauma.

How can helping professionals prevent vicarious trauma?

Regular supervision, balanced caseloads, breaks between intense work, time off, and restorative activities all help.

What is vicarious resilience?

Vicarious resilience is the positive growth helpers can experience from witnessing clients' strength and recovery.

Should I talk to my supervisor about vicarious trauma?

Yes. Supervision is one of the most important supports, and discussing its effects is a normal part of trauma-informed practice.

Can personal therapy help with vicarious trauma?

Yes. Therapy offers space to process difficult material, address activated personal history, and restore a sense of meaning.
