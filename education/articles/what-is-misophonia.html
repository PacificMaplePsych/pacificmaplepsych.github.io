What Is Misophonia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Misophonia is a condition in which specific everyday sounds, often made by other people, trigger intense emotional and physical reactions. Common triggers include chewing, slurping, breathing, sniffing, throat clearing, pen clicking, and typing. Reactions can include sudden irritation, anger, disgust, anxiety, or an urge to escape, along with muscle tension or a racing heart. People with misophonia usually know their reaction is out of proportion, which can lead to guilt and avoidance. Misophonia often begins in childhood or adolescence. It is not yet a formal diagnosis, but researchers have agreed on a consensus definition, and therapy can help people manage reactions and reduce their impact.

Signs of Misophonia

  • Strong reactions to specific sounds, especially mouth and breathing sounds
  • Anger or disgust that feels sudden and hard to control
  • Physical responses, such as tension, sweating, or a racing heart
  • Avoiding situations, such as family meals, offices, or public transit
  • Using headphones or noise to mask triggers
  • Guilt or shame about reactions toward loved ones
  • Visual triggers, such as seeing someone chew, for some people

Why It Happens

Research suggests misophonia involves differences in how the brain links certain sounds with emotional and threat responses. Brain imaging studies have found heightened activity in areas involved in emotion and attention when people with misophonia hear trigger sounds. It often runs in families and frequently occurs alongside anxiety, OCD, ADHD, and autism, as described on the page about sensory overload.

How Misophonia Affects Life

Misophonia can strain relationships, especially when triggers come from partners, children, or parents. It can make work, school, and social events difficult. Some people withdraw to avoid triggers, which can lead to loneliness. Others feel ashamed of their reactions or worry that they are "bad" people.

Misophonia in Children and Teens

Misophonia often begins in late childhood or early adolescence, and the first trigger sounds are frequently made by family members. Children may become upset at the dinner table, leave the room suddenly, or seem irritable without being able to explain why. Parents may interpret these reactions as rudeness or defiance. Understanding that the reactions are automatic can reduce conflict at home. Practical steps, such as background music during meals, flexible seating, and agreed signals for needing a break, can help. A psychologist can help children learn coping strategies and support families in making reasonable accommodations without reinforcing avoidance too much.

What It Is Not

Misophonia is not simply being annoyed by noise, and it is not a choice or a character flaw. It is also different from hyperacusis, in which sounds are physically uncomfortable or painful because of their volume, and from general sound sensitivity in autism, although these can overlap.

What Helps

Practical Strategies

  • Use sound masking, such as white noise or music, in triggering situations.
  • Plan ahead. Choose seating or environments that reduce exposure.
  • Communicate with loved ones. Explain misophonia and agree on reasonable accommodations, such as background music during meals.
  • Take breaks when you feel your reaction building.

Therapy

  • Cognitive behavioural therapy can help change thoughts that fuel reactions, reduce avoidance, and build coping skills.
  • Emotional regulation skills help manage the surge of anger or disgust, as described on the page about DBT skills for emotional regulation.
  • Relaxation and counterconditioning may help reduce the strength of triggers.

Treating Related Conditions

If anxiety, OCD, or ADHD is also present, treating it can reduce overall stress and reactivity.

When to Get Support

Consider support if misophonia causes significant distress, strains relationships, or leads you to avoid important activities. Being understood is often the first relief. The page about why emotions can feel too big to handle may also help.

Common Questions About Misophonia

Is misophonia just being annoyed by noise?

No. Misophonia involves intense, automatic anger, disgust, or anxiety in response to specific sounds, far beyond ordinary annoyance.

What are common misophonia triggers?

Common misophonia triggers include chewing, slurping, breathing, sniffing, throat clearing, pen clicking, and typing.

Is misophonia a real condition?

Yes. Misophonia is a recognized condition with a research consensus definition, though it is not yet a formal diagnosis.

Why does the brain react so strongly to trigger sounds in misophonia?

In misophonia, the brain appears to link certain sounds with emotional and threat responses, so trigger sounds set off a strong, automatic reaction.

Is misophonia related to autism or ADHD?

Misophonia often occurs alongside autism, ADHD, anxiety, and OCD, though it can occur on its own.

What is the difference between misophonia and hyperacusis?

Misophonia is an emotional reaction to specific sounds, while hyperacusis is physical discomfort or pain from sound volume.

Is there a treatment that makes misophonia go away?

There is no cure for misophonia, but therapy and practical strategies can reduce how strongly you react and how much it affects daily life.

Does CBT help misophonia?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy can help people with misophonia manage reactions, reduce avoidance, and build coping skills.

How can families make meals easier for someone with misophonia?

Families can help someone with misophonia by playing background music during meals, agreeing on seating, and understanding that reactions are automatic rather than personal.

When does misophonia start?

Misophonia often begins in childhood or adolescence, though it can start at any age.
