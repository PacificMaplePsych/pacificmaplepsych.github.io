Workplace Bullying and Toxic Workplaces

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Workplace bullying is repeated, unreasonable behaviour directed at a worker that is intended, or should reasonably be known, to humiliate or intimidate them. It can include belittling comments, public criticism, exclusion, spreading rumours, sabotage, unreasonable workloads, or threats. A toxic workplace more broadly is one where disrespect, fear, favouritism, or chronic overload are part of the culture. These environments are linked to anxiety, depression, sleep problems, burnout, and physical health symptoms. In BC, employers have legal obligations to prevent and address bullying and harassment, and workers can report concerns internally and to WorkSafeBC. Getting support early can protect your health.

Signs of Workplace Bullying

  • Belittling or humiliating comments, in private or in front of others
  • Excessive monitoring or unfair criticism
  • Exclusion from meetings, information, or social events
  • Undermining your work or taking credit for it
  • Unreasonable demands or deadlines designed to set you up to fail
  • Spreading rumours or gossip
  • Threats, intimidation, or yelling

Reasonable management actions, such as performance feedback or work assignments given respectfully, are not bullying.

Signs of a Toxic Workplace

  • Fear of speaking up or making mistakes
  • High turnover and burnout
  • Favouritism or unfair treatment
  • Poor communication and constant crises
  • Lack of boundaries around working hours

How It Affects Mental Health

People experiencing bullying or toxic environments often report dread before work, as described on the page about dreading going to work every day, along with anxiety, low mood, self-doubt, sleep problems, and physical symptoms such as headaches or stomach aches. Over time, this can lead to burnout or depression, as described on the page about workplace stress.

Why It Can Be Hard to Recognize

Workplace bullying often starts subtly, with a pattern of small slights, exclusions, or criticisms that are easy to explain away. People may doubt themselves, wondering whether they are too sensitive or whether the criticism is deserved. Bullies may also be well liked by others or hold power, which makes it harder to speak up. Keeping a written record can help clarify the pattern over time. Talking with a trusted colleague, union representative, or therapist can also provide an outside perspective. If you notice that you dread going to work, feel constantly on edge, or are losing confidence in skills you once felt sure of, it is worth taking seriously.

Your Options in BC

Document

Keep a record of incidents, including dates, times, what happened, witnesses, and any messages.

Use Internal Processes

BC employers are required to have policies and procedures for preventing and responding to bullying and harassment. Review your workplace policy and report to a supervisor, human resources, or another designated person.

Contact WorkSafeBC

WorkSafeBC oversees employers' bullying and harassment obligations. Workers may also be able to make a claim for a mental health condition caused by significant work-related stressors, including bullying and harassment.

Union and Legal Support

If you are a union member, your union can help. An employment lawyer can advise on your rights. If the treatment is related to a protected characteristic, such as race, gender, or disability, the BC Human Rights Code may also apply.

Protecting Your Wellbeing

  • Seek support from trusted colleagues, friends, and family.
  • Set boundaries around work hours where possible.
  • Look after sleep, exercise, and time away from work.
  • Consider whether time off or a change of role is needed, as described on the page about taking a mental health leave from work in BC.

What It Is Not

Being bullied at work is not a sign of weakness or incompetence. Bullying often targets capable, conscientious people. This page offers general information, not legal advice.

When to Get Support

Therapy can help you cope with anxiety and self-doubt, plan how to respond, and recover after leaving a toxic environment. The page about returning to work after a mental health leave may also help.

Common Questions About Workplace Bullying

What is workplace bullying?

Workplace bullying is repeated, unreasonable behaviour intended to humiliate or intimidate a worker, such as belittling, exclusion, or sabotage.

What are signs of a toxic workplace?

Signs of a toxic workplace include fear of speaking up, high turnover, favouritism, constant crises, and lack of boundaries around work hours.

Is performance feedback considered bullying?

No. Reasonable management actions, such as respectful performance feedback, are not bullying.

Can being bullied at work lead to burnout or depression?

Yes. Ongoing workplace bullying can lead to anxiety, depression, sleep problems, and burnout over time.

How do I report workplace bullying in BC?

Report workplace bullying through your employer's bullying and harassment policy, and contact WorkSafeBC if concerns are not addressed.

Can I file a WorkSafeBC claim for stress caused by workplace bullying?

WorkSafeBC may accept a mental health claim caused by significant work-related stressors, including bullying and harassment.

Should I document workplace bullying?

Yes. Keep records of dates, times, what happened, witnesses, and messages.

Can I take a leave because of workplace bullying?

A health professional can assess whether time off or accommodations are needed if workplace bullying is affecting your health.

Why do bullies target certain people at work?

Workplace bullies often target capable, conscientious people, and being targeted is not a sign of weakness.

Can therapy help with workplace bullying?

Yes. Therapy can help you cope with anxiety and self-doubt, plan next steps, and recover after a toxic workplace.
