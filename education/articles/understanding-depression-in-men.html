Understanding Depression in Men

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you or someone you know is having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Depression can affect anyone, but in many men it shows up in ways that are easy to miss. Instead of talking about sadness, men with depression may become irritable, angry, withdrawn, or focused on work. They may drink more, take more risks, or complain mainly of physical symptoms. Social expectations to be strong, self-reliant, and in control can make it harder for men to recognize depression or seek help. As a result, depression in men is often underdiagnosed and undertreated. Suicide rates are higher among men in Canada, which makes recognizing depression especially important. Depression is treatable, and reaching out is a sign of strength.

What Depression in Men Can Look Like

  • Feeling irritable instead of sad, or quick to anger.
  • Withdrawing from family, friends, or activities.
  • Working excessively or becoming consumed by other activities to avoid feelings.
  • Drinking more than planned or using other substances.
  • Risk-taking, such as reckless driving or gambling.
  • Fatigue, sleep problems, headaches, or digestive issues.
  • Loss of interest in hobbies, sex, or time with others.
  • Feeling numb, empty, or hopeless.
  • Difficulty concentrating or making decisions.

Why It Is Often Missed

Many men grow up with messages that emotions, especially sadness and fear, should be hidden or controlled. These norms can make it hard to name feelings, admit struggling, or ask for help. Depression may be expressed through behaviours that seem unrelated, such as anger or overwork, so friends, family, and even health professionals may not recognize it. Some men worry that seeking help will be seen as weakness or affect their job.

Life events such as job loss, financial pressure, relationship breakdown, separation from children, retirement, illness, and bereavement can trigger depression. Grief in men is also often expressed in less visible ways, as described on the page about grief in men. Isolation is a significant risk factor, as many men have fewer close confidants as they age. Substance use can mask depression and increase suicide risk.

Physical health and depression are closely connected. Men may visit a doctor for back pain, fatigue, or sleep problems without mentioning mood. Health professionals who ask directly about stress, drinking, and mood can help uncover depression that might otherwise be missed.

Partners and family members often notice changes first. They may see more arguments, less patience with children, long hours at work, or a loss of interest in things once enjoyed. Sharing these observations with care, rather than criticism, can open a conversation that leads to support.

What It Is Not

  • Not weakness. Depression is a health condition that can affect anyone.
  • Not just stress or a bad temper. Ongoing irritability and withdrawal can be signs of depression.
  • Not something to handle alone. Support makes recovery more likely.
  • Not a reason to feel ashamed. Many men experience depression and recover.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor can screen for depression and check for medical causes. Treatment options are the same as for other forms of depression and are effective. Cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural activation, and interpersonal therapy help with mood, thinking patterns, and relationships. Some men prefer practical, goal-focused therapy approaches, which are widely available. Medication can help, particularly for moderate to severe depression. Reducing alcohol, staying active, and reconnecting with friends support recovery. Peer programs and men's groups can reduce isolation and stigma.

Common Questions About Depression in Men

How does depression show up differently in men?

Men may show depression through irritability, anger, withdrawal, overwork, drinking, or physical complaints rather than sadness.

Why do men often not seek help for depression?

Social expectations to be strong and self-reliant, and fear of seeming weak, can make it harder to ask for help.

Is anger a sign of depression in men?

It can be. Ongoing irritability or anger is a common way depression shows up in men.

Why are suicide rates higher among men?

Contributing factors include underdiagnosed depression, isolation, substance use, and reluctance to seek help.

Can drinking be a sign of hidden depression?

Yes. Some men drink more to cope with low mood, which can mask depression and increase risk.

How can I talk to a man in my life about depression?

Choose a private moment, share specific changes you have noticed, listen without judgment, and encourage support.

What therapy approaches do men find helpful for depression?

Many men find practical, goal-focused approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy and behavioural activation helpful.

Can life changes trigger depression in men?

Yes. Job loss, separation, retirement, illness, and bereavement are common triggers.

Are there support groups for men with depression?

Yes. Peer programs and men's groups can reduce isolation and make it easier to talk.

Is depression in men treatable?

Yes. Therapy, medication, and lifestyle changes are effective for depression in men.
