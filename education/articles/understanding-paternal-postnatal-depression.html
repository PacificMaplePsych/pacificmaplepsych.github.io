Understanding Paternal Postnatal Depression

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 if anyone is in immediate danger. In British Columbia, you can also call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse.

Paternal postnatal depression is depression that develops in fathers during pregnancy or in the year after a baby is born. Non-birthing parents in other family structures can experience the same pattern. It is more common than many people realize, and it often goes unrecognized, because attention understandably focuses on the birthing parent and because symptoms may look different from the classic picture of depression. Paternal postnatal depression affects the parent, the couple, and the child's early environment. It is treatable, and recognizing it early helps the whole family.

What Paternal Postnatal Depression Looks Like

  • Irritability, anger, or a short temper, as in feeling angry since having a baby.
  • Withdrawing from the partner, baby, family, or friends.
  • Working longer hours or staying busy to avoid home.
  • Low mood, hopelessness, or feeling numb.
  • Feeling disconnected from the baby, similar to not feeling bonded.
  • Increased drinking, gaming, or other ways of escaping.
  • Fatigue, sleep problems, and difficulty concentrating.
  • Anxiety about providing for the family or being a good parent.
  • Physical complaints, such as headaches or stomach problems.

Many fathers do not recognize these changes as depression, and may describe themselves as stressed, tired, or frustrated. Partners may notice first.

Why It Happens

Becoming a parent brings enormous change. Sleep deprivation, financial pressure, shifts in the couple relationship, and loss of personal time and freedom can all contribute. Many fathers feel pressure to be the strong one and put their own needs aside, which can make it harder to ask for help. Changes in the couple relationship, including less time together and resentment since having kids, are common. Research also suggests hormonal changes occur in fathers around the birth of a child.

One of the strongest risk factors is depression in the birthing partner. When one parent is struggling, the other is more likely to struggle too. Other risk factors include a personal history of depression or anxiety, limited support, relationship difficulties, a difficult birth, and a baby with health problems or who is hard to settle. Paternal depression can also affect children's development, which is one more reason early support matters.

Many new fathers also report feeling like outsiders during pregnancy and the early weeks, as health care and support often focus on the birthing parent and baby. Feeling unsure of their role, or excluded from decisions and appointments, can add to isolation. Being included and asked directly how they are coping can make a real difference.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign of weakness or not loving your child. It is a common health condition.
  • Not just being tired. Ongoing low mood, irritability, or withdrawal for more than two weeks deserves attention.
  • Not less important than maternal postpartum depression. Both parents' mental health matters for the family.
  • Not something to hide. Talking about it opens the door to support.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor can screen for depression and check physical health. A mental health professional will ask about mood, stress, sleep, relationships, and safety. Treatment options mirror those for other forms of depression. Cognitive behavioural therapy and interpersonal therapy are effective, and couples therapy can help partners support each other through the transition. Practical steps, such as sharing night duties, protecting some rest, staying connected with friends, and limiting alcohol, support recovery. Medication may be recommended for some. Peer groups for new fathers can reduce isolation.

Common Questions About Paternal Postnatal Depression

Can fathers get postnatal depression?

Yes. Fathers and other non-birthing parents can develop depression during pregnancy or in the year after a baby is born.

How common is paternal postnatal depression?

It is more common than many people realize, and rates are higher when the birthing partner is also depressed.

What are the signs of depression in new dads?

Signs include irritability, anger, withdrawal, overworking, low mood, increased drinking, and feeling disconnected from the baby.

Why is paternal postnatal depression often missed?

Attention usually focuses on the birthing parent, and fathers may show depression as irritability or withdrawal rather than sadness.

Does my partner's postpartum depression affect my own mental health?

It can. Depression in one parent is a strong risk factor for depression in the other.

Can hormones change in new fathers?

Research suggests some hormonal changes occur in fathers around the birth of a child, which may play a role in mood.

How does a father's depression affect the baby?

Parental depression can affect interaction and bonding, which is why early treatment benefits the whole family.

What helps a new dad who is struggling?

Talking openly, sharing night duties, protecting rest, staying connected with friends, and seeking professional support all help.

Who can a struggling new dad talk to first?

Family doctors, 8-1-1, community parenting programs, peer groups for fathers, and therapists offer support.

Can couples therapy help after having a baby?

Yes. Couples therapy can help partners communicate, share the load, and support each other's mental health.
