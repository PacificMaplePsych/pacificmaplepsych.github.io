What Is ARFID (Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder)

Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder, known as ARFID, is an eating disorder in which a person limits the amount or variety of food they eat to the point that it affects their health, growth, or daily life. Unlike anorexia nervosa, ARFID is not driven by concerns about weight or body shape. Instead, restriction is linked to sensory sensitivity, fear of negative consequences such as choking or vomiting, or a general lack of interest in food. ARFID can affect children, teens, and adults. It is more than picky eating, and it responds to specialized treatment.

What ARFID Looks Like

ARFID is often described through three main patterns, which can overlap:

  • Sensory sensitivity. Strong reactions to taste, texture, smell, or appearance of food, leading to a very limited list of safe foods.
  • Fear of aversive consequences. Avoiding food after choking, vomiting, or pain, or fearing these will happen. This can overlap with emetophobia.
  • Low interest in eating. Rarely feeling hungry, forgetting to eat, or finding eating a chore.

Other signs include:

  • Eating only a small number of foods, often of a certain brand, colour, or texture.
  • Weight loss, low weight, or not growing as expected in children.
  • Relying on supplements or specific drinks for nutrition.
  • Anxiety or distress at meals, especially with unfamiliar foods.
  • Avoiding social events involving food.

Why It Happens

ARFID develops from a combination of factors. Many people with ARFID have heightened sensory sensitivity, and ARFID is more common among autistic people and people with ADHD. A frightening experience, such as choking or severe vomiting, can create a strong fear that leads to avoidance. Anxiety disorders often co-occur. Some people seem to have naturally low appetite or interest in food. Medical conditions affecting the gut can also contribute, and eating may become linked with discomfort.

Avoidance tends to keep ARFID going. When a feared or disliked food is avoided, anxiety drops, which reinforces avoidance, and the list of safe foods can shrink over time. Family mealtimes may become tense as parents try to encourage eating.

For adults, ARFID can go unrecognized for many years. People may have been labelled picky eaters as children and learned to manage by eating the same foods or avoiding social meals. Many adults feel embarrassed and hide their eating, which can lead to isolation. Learning that ARFID is a recognized condition often brings relief and makes it easier to seek help.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary picky eating. Many children are selective, but ARFID affects health, growth, or daily life.
  • Not anorexia nervosa. ARFID is not driven by weight or body image concerns, unlike anorexia.
  • Not a parenting failure. ARFID has biological and psychological roots.
  • Not something to force. Pressuring someone to eat usually increases anxiety and avoidance.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment usually involves a physician to check weight, growth, and nutrition, and a mental health professional to explore eating patterns, fears, and sensory factors. A dietitian experienced in eating disorders is often part of the team. Cognitive behavioural therapy for ARFID gradually expands food variety and amount through structured, paced exposure, while addressing fears and building tolerance. Family-based approaches help parents support children without pressure. Occupational therapy may help with sensory aspects or feeding skills. For people with low interest in eating, regular meal schedules and reminders are useful. Treatment is adapted for autistic people and those with sensory differences.

Common Questions About ARFID

Can someone restrict food without caring about weight or shape?

Yes. In ARFID, food intake is limited by sensory sensitivity, fear of consequences, or low interest in eating rather than body image.

How is ARFID different from picky eating?

Picky eating is common and usually harmless. ARFID affects health, growth, nutrition, or daily life and often involves significant anxiety.

Can adults have ARFID?

Yes. ARFID affects children, teens, and adults, and many adults go unrecognized for years.

Is ARFID linked with autism?

ARFID is more common among autistic people, often because of sensory sensitivity, though it also occurs in non-autistic people.

Can a choking experience lead to ARFID?

Yes. A frightening choking or vomiting episode can lead to fear-based avoidance of foods or eating.

How is ARFID different from anorexia nervosa?

ARFID is not driven by weight or body shape concerns, while anorexia involves fear of weight gain and body image disturbance.

What does recovery from ARFID usually look like?

Recovery is gradual and often involves cognitive behavioural therapy for ARFID, gradual food exposure, family support, dietitian input, and medical monitoring.

Should I pressure my child with ARFID to eat new foods?

Pressure usually increases anxiety. Calm, gradual, structured exposure with professional guidance works better.

Why do I rarely feel hungry?

Some people with ARFID have low appetite or interest in food. A doctor can also check for medical causes.

What professionals help with ARFID?

A team may include a physician, a mental health professional, a dietitian, and sometimes an occupational therapist.
