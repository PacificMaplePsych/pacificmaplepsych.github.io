Signs Your Child May Need Mental Health Support

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If your child talks about wanting to die, harms themselves, or you are worried about their immediate safety, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. You or your child can call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Young people can also reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Every child has difficult days, moods, and phases. Deciding when a change is part of normal development and when it signals a need for support can be hard for parents and caregivers. In general, it is worth paying closer attention when changes are intense, last more than a few weeks, affect daily life at home or school, or seem out of character. Mental health concerns in children and teens are common, and early support tends to lead to better outcomes. Noticing and acting on concerns is an act of care, not overreaction.

Signs Worth Noticing

  • Ongoing sadness, irritability, or tearfulness.
  • Worry or fear that stops a child from going to school, sleeping alone, or seeing friends.
  • Withdrawing from friends, family, or activities they used to enjoy.
  • Big changes in sleep, appetite, or weight.
  • Frequent headaches or stomach aches without a clear medical cause.
  • A drop in school performance or refusing to attend school.
  • Frequent angry outbursts, aggression, or behaviour that is hard to manage.
  • Returning to younger behaviours, such as bedwetting, after a stressful event.
  • Talk of hopelessness, self-harm, or not wanting to be here.
  • Use of alcohol or drugs, or risky behaviour in teens.

Younger children often show distress through behaviour and physical complaints rather than words. Teens may hide struggles or express them as irritability, which can be mistaken for typical adolescent moodiness. The page on parenting a teenager explores some of these challenges.

Why It Happens

Children's mental health is shaped by many factors, including temperament, genetics, family stress, school pressures, friendships, bullying, social media, loss, and trauma. Developmental differences such as ADHD or autism can bring additional challenges if needs are not understood. Big changes, such as a move, separation, new sibling, or illness in the family, can temporarily overwhelm a child's coping. Sometimes there is no single cause. Just as adults can develop anxiety or depression, so can children, and it is no one's fault.

Parents often notice changes first but may doubt themselves or worry about labelling their child. It is natural to feel protective or to hope things will pass. At the same time, many parents also carry their own worry about their child, as described on the page about worrying about your child all the time. A professional can help sort out what is typical and what needs attention.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign of bad parenting. Mental health concerns occur in loving, attentive families.
  • Not something children always grow out of. Some difficulties ease, but others persist without support.
  • Not always a diagnosis. Many children benefit from support during a hard time without needing a label.
  • Not every conflict. Ordinary sibling rivalry and occasional tantrums are part of growing up.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

A good first step is talking with your child in a calm, curious way, and consulting your family doctor or paediatrician. School counsellors can share what they notice at school. A psychologist can assess emotional, behavioural, and developmental concerns and recommend next steps. Treatment for children often involves parents, since changes at home can make a big difference. Approaches may include cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for children, play-based therapy for younger children, and parent coaching. In British Columbia, publicly funded Child and Youth Mental Health services are also available.

Common Questions About Children's Mental Health

How can I tell whether my child's behaviour is a phase or a mental health concern?

Consider how intense the change is, how long it lasts, and whether it affects school, friendships, or family life. Changes lasting more than a few weeks are worth checking.

What are early signs of anxiety in children?

Excessive worry, clinginess, avoiding school or activities, trouble sleeping, and frequent stomach aches or headaches can all be early signs.

How does depression look different in kids and teens?

Children and teens may seem irritable, bored, or withdrawn rather than openly sad, and may complain of physical symptoms.

Should I be worried if my child refuses to go to school?

Persistent school refusal often signals anxiety, bullying, learning difficulties, or other concerns and is worth discussing with the school and a professional.

How do I talk to my child about their feelings?

Choose a relaxed moment, ask open questions, listen without judging or fixing, and let them know you are there whenever they are ready.

Who should I contact first if I am worried about my child?

Your family doctor, paediatrician, or school counsellor is a good starting point. They can refer you to further assessment or services.

Can young children really have mental health problems?

Yes. Children of any age can experience anxiety, depression, trauma reactions, and other concerns, often shown through behaviour.

What does child therapy usually involve?

It often includes sessions with the child, parent involvement, and approaches such as play-based work or cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for age.

What should I do if my teen talks about self-harm?

Stay calm, listen, and take it seriously. Seek professional help promptly, and call 911 or 9-8-8 if you are worried about immediate safety.

Are there free children's mental health services in British Columbia?

Yes. Child and Youth Mental Health teams across the province offer publicly funded services, and school counsellors can help connect families.
