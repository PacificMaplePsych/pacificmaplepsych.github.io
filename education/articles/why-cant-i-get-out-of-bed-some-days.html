Why Can't I Get Out of Bed Some Days

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

The alarm goes off and you are awake, but your body feels like it is made of concrete. You know what you need to do today, and you may even want to do it, yet getting up feels impossible. Minutes turn into an hour. When you finally get up, you may already feel behind, guilty, or frustrated with yourself. On some days, you do not get up at all.

This experience is far more common than people admit, and it is not laziness or a lack of willpower. It is a sign that something, physical, emotional, or both, is draining your energy and motivation.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or feel unable to keep yourself safe, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Sleep problems. Poor quality sleep, irregular schedules, or a sleep disorder such as sleep apnea can leave you exhausted no matter how long you are in bed. Loud snoring, waking up gasping, or heavy daytime sleepiness are worth mentioning to a doctor.

Physical health. Thyroid problems, anemia, vitamin deficiencies, lingering effects of viral illness, chronic pain, and some medications can all cause heavy fatigue. A medical check is an important step if this is new.

Burnout. Prolonged work stress can drain energy so thoroughly that mornings become a daily battle, especially on workdays. This often comes with dreading work and getting sick more often.

Alcohol and substances. Drinking in the evening, even moderately, disrupts sleep quality and can make mornings much harder.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD find getting started with any task, including getting up, difficult, particularly when the day ahead feels unstructured or uninteresting.

Mood cycles. In bipolar disorder, periods of depression can bring heavy exhaustion and oversleeping, alternating with periods of much higher energy, which we discuss in Why Do My Moods Swing So Dramatically.

When It Looks More Like Depression

Depression affects the body as much as the mind. Many people with depression describe a physical heaviness, slowed movement and thinking, and a deep fatigue that sleep does not fix. Getting out of bed requires energy and a reason, and depression tends to drain both.

Signs that depression may be involved include:

  • Low mood, emptiness, or numbness that has lasted two weeks or more.
  • Having lost interest in things you used to enjoy, so there is little to look forward to.
  • Feeling worst in the morning and a bit better as the day goes on.
  • Changes in appetite, sleeping much more or much less than usual, and difficulty concentrating.
  • Harsh self-criticism, guilt, or feelings of worthlessness about not getting up.
  • Irritability or a short fuse, which for some people is more noticeable than sadness, as described in Why Do I Feel Irritable Instead of Sad.

Staying in bed can bring short-term relief from facing the day, but it often deepens depression over time by reducing activity, daylight, and connection, all of which help mood recover.

What Can Help

  • Shrink the first step. Instead of "get up and start the day," aim for "sit up and put my feet on the floor." Small steps are easier to start and tend to build momentum.
  • Plan one small morning anchor, such as a warm drink by a window, a short walk, or a text to a friend.
  • Get light early. Opening curtains or stepping outside soon after waking helps regulate your body clock and energy.
  • Replace self-criticism with curiosity. Asking "what is making this hard today?" is more useful than blaming yourself.
  • Tell someone. Letting a trusted person know you are struggling makes it easier to get support.

Common Questions About Not Being Able to Get Out of Bed

Is not being able to get out of bed a sign of depression?

It can be. Depression commonly causes physical heaviness, fatigue, and low motivation that make getting up very difficult. It is more likely to be depression when it comes with low mood, loss of interest, and other symptoms lasting two weeks or more.

Why do I feel so heavy and slow in the mornings?

Morning heaviness can come from poor sleep, alcohol the night before, physical illness, or depression, which often feels worst early in the day. If the heaviness is frequent and does not improve with better sleep, it is worth talking to a professional.

Can a medical problem make it hard to get out of bed?

Yes. Thyroid problems, anemia, vitamin deficiencies, sleep apnea, chronic pain, and some medications can all cause significant fatigue. A doctor's visit is an important step, especially if the fatigue is new or unexplained.

Is staying in bed all day making my depression worse?

Often it does. Spending long periods in bed reduces daylight exposure, activity, and social contact, all of which help mood recover. Gentle, gradual increases in activity tend to improve energy over time, even when motivation is low.

What is behavioural activation for depression?

Behavioural activation is an evidence-based therapy approach that helps people with depression gradually re-engage in meaningful and enjoyable activities. Instead of waiting to feel motivated, you take small planned steps, and motivation often follows.

Can burnout make it impossible to get up for work?

Burnout can drain energy so deeply that workday mornings feel overwhelming. It is often tied specifically to work and may ease somewhat on days off. If the struggle extends to all days and all areas of life, depression may also be involved.

Why does getting out of bed feel easier on some days than others?

Energy and mood naturally fluctuate with sleep, stress, hormones, and what the day holds. Noticing which days are easier can reveal useful patterns, such as better mornings after earlier nights or when something enjoyable is planned.

Can oversleeping be a symptom of depression?

Yes. While some people with depression struggle to sleep, others sleep much more than usual and still feel exhausted. Oversleeping is especially common in some forms of depression and during depressive periods in bipolar disorder.

How can I get out of bed when I have no motivation at all?

Try making the first step as small as possible, such as sitting up, opening the curtains, or putting on a sweater. Planning one small, pleasant thing to do after getting up and reducing self-criticism can also help build momentum.

Where can I get help right away if I'm struggling to keep myself safe?

In Canada, you can call or text 9-8-8 at any time to reach the Suicide Crisis Helpline, or call 911 in an emergency. You can also go to your nearest emergency department. Reaching out is a sign of strength, not weakness.
