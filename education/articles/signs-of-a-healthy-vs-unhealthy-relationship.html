Signs of a Healthy vs. Unhealthy Relationship

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

A healthy relationship is one where both people feel safe, respected, and free to be themselves, and where conflict is handled with care and repair. An unhealthy relationship involves patterns such as control, disrespect, fear, or ongoing imbalance that leave one or both people feeling smaller over time. Every relationship has difficult moments, so the question is less about whether problems happen and more about how they are handled and how you feel overall. The patterns described here apply to romantic partners, and many apply to friendships and family relationships too.

If you are afraid of your partner, or they are hurting, threatening, or controlling you, your safety comes first. Call 911 in an emergency, and see our abuse resources and safety planning information. Couples therapy is generally not recommended when there is abuse.

What a Healthy Relationship Looks Like

  • Respect. Each person's opinions, feelings, and boundaries are taken seriously, even in disagreement.
  • Trust. You can rely on each other and do not need to monitor or check up on one another.
  • Safety. You can express yourself without fear of punishment, threats, or harm.
  • Independence. Both people keep friendships, interests, and a sense of self outside the relationship.
  • Fair conflict. Disagreements focus on the issue, not on insults, and both people can repair afterward.
  • Mutual support. Both people give and receive care, and neither is always the one compromising.

In daily life, this can look like apologizing after an argument, making decisions together, and feeling glad, rather than anxious, when you see each other.

Why Unhealthy Patterns Develop

Unhealthy patterns can develop for many reasons. Some people learned unhealthy ways of relating in their families. Stress, untreated mental health concerns, or substance use can strain communication. Attachment patterns can create cycles of pursuing and withdrawing. In some relationships, one person uses power and control deliberately, which is abuse. People sometimes stay in unhealthy relationships because of love, hope for change, fear, finances, children, or cultural and family pressure, and these reasons deserve understanding, not judgment.

What It Is Not

Unhealthy is not the same as imperfect. Healthy couples argue, have rough patches, and sometimes feel distant. The difference is in patterns over time. Warning signs of an unhealthy or abusive relationship include:

  • Controlling who you see, what you wear, or how you spend money.
  • Monitoring your phone, location, or messages without consent.
  • Frequent put-downs, humiliation, or name-calling.
  • Making you question your memory or perception.
  • Threats, intimidation, or any physical harm.
  • Pressuring you sexually or ignoring your consent.
  • Isolating you from friends or family.
  • Feeling like you have to earn affection or walk on eggshells.

Some people feel drawn to the same kind of difficult dynamic again and again, which is explored on the page about choosing the same type of partner.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

There is no diagnosis for an unhealthy relationship, but a therapist can help you look at patterns, safety, and your own needs. When both partners are committed and there is no abuse, couples therapy can help improve communication and repair. When there is abuse or fear, individual support and safety planning come first, and specialized domestic violence services can help. Individual therapy can also help people understand their relationship history and build healthier connections.

Common Questions About Healthy and Unhealthy Relationships

What are the signs of a healthy relationship?

Healthy relationships involve respect, trust, safety, independence, fair conflict, and mutual support. Both people feel free to be themselves and can repair after disagreements.

Which red flags tend to appear early in a controlling relationship?

Early signs can include jealousy framed as love, pressure to spend all your time together, criticism, checking your phone, and discouraging you from seeing friends or family.

Is it normal for couples to argue?

Yes. Disagreement is part of any relationship. What matters is whether arguments stay respectful, focus on the issue, and are followed by repair.

What counts as controlling behaviour in a relationship?

Controlling behaviour includes monitoring messages, restricting who you see, controlling money, dictating what you wear, or punishing you for independent decisions.

What is coercive control?

Coercive control is a pattern of behaviours, such as isolation, monitoring, threats, and humiliation, used to dominate a partner. It can be abusive even without physical violence.

Why do people stay in unhealthy relationships?

People stay for many reasons, including love, hope for change, fear, financial dependence, children, and family or cultural pressure. These reasons deserve understanding and support.

Can an unhealthy relationship become healthy again?

Sometimes, when both people recognize the problems, take responsibility, and commit to change, often with professional help. When there is abuse, safety must come first.

Should we go to couples therapy if there is abuse?

Couples therapy is generally not recommended when there is abuse, because it can increase risk. Individual support and safety planning are usually the safer first steps.

How do I know if I am the one being unhealthy in my relationship?

Reflecting on whether you control, criticize, or frighten your partner, and noticing their reactions, is a useful start. Seeking help to change these patterns shows real responsibility.

Where can I get help if I think my relationship is abusive?

Call 911 in an emergency. Domestic violence services, transition houses, and a therapist can help with safety planning and support. Our abuse resources pages list further options.
