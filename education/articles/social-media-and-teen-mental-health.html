Social Media and Teen Mental Health

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If a teen talks about wanting to die or harming themselves, or you are worried about their immediate safety, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Young people can reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Social media has both benefits and risks for teen mental health. It can help teens stay connected with friends, find communities, and express themselves, especially teens who feel isolated offline. At the same time, research links heavy or problematic use with poorer sleep, more anxiety and depression, body image concerns, and exposure to cyberbullying or harmful content for some teens. The effects depend on how much time teens spend online, what they see and do, what it replaces, such as sleep or in-person time, and individual vulnerability. Rather than banning social media outright, most experts recommend clear family agreements, protecting sleep, and ongoing, open conversations.

How Social Media Can Affect Teens

Potential Benefits

  • Staying connected with friends and family
  • Finding supportive communities, including for 2SLGBTQ+ youth or teens with shared interests
  • Creativity and self-expression
  • Access to information and support

Potential Risks

  • Sleep loss. Late-night scrolling delays sleep, as described on the page about teen sleep.
  • Comparison and body image. Curated images can fuel insecurity, as described on the page about comparing yourself to others.
  • Cyberbullying, as described on the page about bullying and cyberbullying.
  • Harmful content, such as content promoting self-harm or disordered eating.
  • Compulsive use driven by notifications and endless feeds.
  • Fear of missing out and pressure to be constantly available.

Warning Signs

  • Mood changes after using social media
  • Difficulty stopping or becoming upset when devices are limited
  • Sleeping poorly or staying up late online
  • Withdrawing from in-person activities
  • Increased worry about appearance or likes
  • Secretive behaviour or signs of being harassed online

These can overlap with signs described on the page about signs of depression in teens.

What Parents Can Do

  1. Talk regularly. Ask what they enjoy online and what feels stressful, with curiosity rather than judgment.
  2. Create a family media plan. Agree on device-free times, such as meals and bedtime.
  3. Charge devices outside bedrooms at night.
  4. Use privacy settings and discuss what to share online.
  5. Teach critical thinking about filtered images, algorithms, and advertising.
  6. Model healthy habits with your own device use.
  7. Encourage offline activities such as sports, hobbies, and time with friends.
  8. Know when to step in if you see signs of cyberbullying, harmful content, or declining mood.

Questions to Start a Conversation

Open-ended questions can help teens talk about their online lives without feeling judged. You might ask: What do you enjoy most about the apps you use? Is there anything online that ever makes you feel bad about yourself? Have you ever seen something online that worried you? What would you do if someone was being mean to you or a friend online? Listening more than lecturing, and sharing your own struggles with phone use, can make these conversations more natural.

What It Is Not

Social media is not the single cause of teen mental health problems, and not every teen who uses it heavily will struggle. Taking away devices suddenly can backfire, especially if social media is a teen's main source of connection. A collaborative approach usually works better.

When to Get Support

If your teen seems depressed, anxious, or unable to cut back despite wanting to, or if online experiences have been harmful, therapy can help. The pages about problematic technology and social media use and changing your relationship with your phone offer more strategies.

Common Questions About Social Media and Teens

Does social media affect teen mental health?

Yes. Social media can support connection, but heavy or problematic use is linked to poorer sleep, anxiety, depression, and body image concerns for some teens.

How much social media is too much for teens?

There is no single limit, but social media is a concern when it disrupts sleep, school, in-person relationships, or mood.

What are the benefits of social media for teens?

Social media can help teens stay connected, find supportive communities, express creativity, and access information.

Why does late-night scrolling cut into teen sleep?

Late-night scrolling cuts into teen sleep through stimulation, screen light, and the difficulty of stopping, which delays when teens fall asleep.

Do filtered photos on social media affect how teens see their bodies?

Yes. Curated and filtered images on social media can contribute to body image concerns and comparison for some teens.

Should I ban social media for my teen?

Sudden bans can backfire. Clear family agreements, device-free times, and open conversations usually work better.

What is a family media plan?

A family media plan is a set of agreed rules about device use, such as device-free meals and charging phones outside bedrooms.

What are warning signs of problematic social media use in teens?

Warning signs include mood changes after use, difficulty stopping, poor sleep, withdrawing from activities, and worry about likes or appearance.

How can I protect my teen from cyberbullying?

Talk openly, use privacy settings, teach blocking and reporting, and step in if you see signs of harassment.

Can therapy help teens with social media problems?

Yes. Therapy can help teens build healthier habits, manage anxiety or low mood, and cope with harmful online experiences.
