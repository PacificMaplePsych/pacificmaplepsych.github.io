Understanding Hypomania

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you or someone you know has gone several nights with little sleep, is taking serious risks, seems confused, or is seeing or hearing things others do not, seek urgent medical help and call 911 if anyone is in danger. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8.

Hypomania is a period of unusually elevated, expansive, or irritable mood, along with increased energy or activity, lasting at least four days and present most of the day. It is similar to mania but less severe. People with hypomania can usually keep working and functioning, and there are no psychotic symptoms. Hypomania is a key feature of bipolar II disorder and can also occur in bipolar I disorder and cyclothymia. Because it can feel good, productive, or simply like being yourself on a great week, hypomania is often not recognized. Understanding it is important for accurate diagnosis and treatment.

What Hypomania Looks Like

  • Feeling unusually confident, upbeat, or energized.
  • Needing much less sleep without feeling tired, as in periods of barely sleeping and feeling unstoppable.
  • Talking more or faster than usual.
  • Racing thoughts or jumping between ideas.
  • Starting many projects or being very goal-driven.
  • Being easily distracted.
  • Irritability or impatience, especially when slowed down.
  • Spending, sexual behaviour, or decisions that are riskier than usual.

Others often notice the change before the person does. Friends or family may comment that you seem wired, different, or not yourself. The shift is noticeable compared with your usual baseline.

Why It Happens

Hypomania is part of bipolar spectrum conditions, which have strong genetic links and involve differences in how the brain regulates mood, energy, and sleep. Episodes can be triggered by sleep disruption, stress, major life changes, seasonal shifts, substance use, or certain medications, including some antidepressants when taken without a mood stabilizer. In bipolar II disorder, people experience hypomanic episodes and major depressive episodes, and depression is often the part that brings people to seek help.

Because many people seek help during depression and do not mention past hypomania, bipolar II disorder can be misdiagnosed as depression for years. This matters because treatment differs. The page on bipolar disorder and depression explains the key differences.

Hypomania can also occur alongside depressive symptoms at the same time, a pattern described on the page about mixed features. Mixed states can feel agitated, restless, and very distressing, and they may carry a higher risk of impulsive behaviour. Clinicians pay close attention to these presentations.

What It Is Not

  • Not just a good mood. Hypomania is a clear change from your baseline that others can observe.
  • Not full mania. Mania is more severe, may involve psychosis, and usually causes serious impairment or hospitalization, as described on the page about bipolar I disorder.
  • Not always harmless. Decisions made during hypomania can have lasting consequences, and it is often followed by depression.
  • Not the same as ADHD, though both can involve distractibility and restlessness. Hypomania comes in distinct episodes.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a detailed history of mood episodes, sleep, energy, and behaviour, often with input from family or close friends. Mood charting can help track patterns. A psychiatrist or physician may prescribe mood-stabilizing medication. Psychotherapy is an important part of treatment. Psychoeducation helps people recognize early warning signs. Interpersonal and social rhythm therapy focuses on stabilizing daily routines and sleep. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps manage depression and risky decisions. Protecting sleep, limiting alcohol and drugs, and having a plan for early signs can reduce relapse.

Common Questions About Hypomania

How much milder is hypomania compared with a full manic episode?

Hypomania is milder, lasts at least four days, and does not cause severe impairment or psychosis. Mania is more intense and often requires urgent care.

How long does a hypomanic episode last?

By definition it lasts at least four consecutive days, most of the day, though episodes can continue longer.

Can hypomania feel good?

Yes. Many people feel confident, productive, and energized, which is one reason hypomania often goes unrecognized.

Is hypomania a sign of bipolar II disorder?

Hypomanic episodes combined with major depressive episodes define bipolar II disorder. Hypomania can also occur in bipolar I and cyclothymia.

Why is bipolar II often misdiagnosed as depression?

People usually seek help when depressed and may not mention past hypomania, so the full pattern is missed.

Can antidepressants trigger hypomania?

In some people with bipolar spectrum conditions, antidepressants taken without a mood stabilizer can trigger hypomania. Prescribers monitor for this.

How does sleep loss affect hypomania?

Sleep disruption is a common trigger. Reduced need for sleep can also be an early warning sign of an episode.

What are early warning signs of a hypomanic episode?

Common early signs include sleeping less, talking faster, feeling unusually confident, starting many projects, and increased irritability.

What is interpersonal and social rhythm therapy?

It is a therapy for bipolar disorder that focuses on stabilizing daily routines, sleep, and relationships to reduce mood episodes.

What can loved ones do when they notice someone becoming wired and sleepless?

Learning the warning signs, gently sharing observations, supporting routines, and helping with a relapse plan can all help.
