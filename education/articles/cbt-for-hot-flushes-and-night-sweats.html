CBT for Hot Flushes and Night Sweats

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for hot flushes and night sweats is a short, structured psychological treatment that helps people cope with menopausal vasomotor symptoms. It does not stop hot flushes from happening, but research shows it reduces how much they bother people and interfere with daily life, and it can improve sleep and mood. CBT teaches practical skills, such as paced breathing and calm responses when a flush starts, and helps change worrying thoughts, such as fear of embarrassment at work. Professional organizations, including The Menopause Society, recommend CBT and clinical hypnosis as evidence-based non-hormonal options, which is helpful for people who cannot or prefer not to use hormone therapy.

How CBT for Hot Flushes Works

Understanding the Symptoms

Sessions begin with information about why hot flushes happen and what triggers them, such as stress, heat, alcohol, or spicy food. Understanding the body's response often reduces fear.

Changing How You Respond

When a flush starts, many people tense up, feel panicky, or worry that others notice, which can make the experience feel more intense. CBT teaches calm, accepting responses, such as letting the flush pass, slowing your breathing, and continuing what you were doing.

Working With Thoughts

Thoughts such as "Everyone can see I am sweating" or "I cannot cope with this" add distress. CBT helps you notice these thoughts and consider more balanced alternatives.

Paced Breathing and Relaxation

Slow breathing, practised daily and used at the start of a flush, can reduce the intensity of the experience, as described on the page about breathing exercises that help with anxiety.

Night Sweats and Sleep

CBT addresses night waking with strategies for settling back to sleep, and often draws on CBT for insomnia, as described on the page about insomnia during menopause.

What Sessions Are Like

CBT for menopausal symptoms is usually brief, often four to six sessions, individually or in a group. Between sessions, you may track hot flushes, practise breathing, and try new responses in daily life. Sessions also look at stress, sleep, and the broader emotional side of the menopause transition.

An Example of a CBT Approach

Imagine someone who has a hot flush during a work meeting and immediately thinks, "Everyone can see I am sweating, and they will think I am unprofessional." The thought increases embarrassment and tension, which can make the flush feel more intense. In CBT, the person might practise noticing the thought, considering what evidence supports it, and trying a more balanced view, such as "Most people are focused on the meeting, and hot flushes are common." Combined with slow breathing, this can make the experience feel more manageable.

Who It Can Help

CBT for hot flushes can help people who:

  • Cannot use hormone therapy, such as some people with a history of breast cancer.
  • Prefer a non-medication approach, or want to combine therapy with medical treatment.
  • Find hot flushes embarrassing or anxiety-provoking, especially at work.
  • Have sleep problems or low mood linked to menopausal symptoms.

How It Compares With Other Options

Hormone therapy is the most effective treatment for reducing how often hot flushes happen, and non-hormonal medications are also available through a physician. Clinical hypnosis has evidence for reducing hot flush frequency and severity, as described on the page about hypnotherapy. CBT focuses on reducing the impact of symptoms and can be used alongside these options. Many people find a combination works best.

The emotional side of the transition, including anxiety and mood changes, is described on the page about perimenopause and mental health, and the general principles of CBT are explained on the page about cognitive behavioural therapy.

Common Questions About CBT for Hot Flushes

Can CBT help with hot flushes?

Yes. CBT can reduce how much hot flushes bother people and interfere with daily life, and it can improve sleep and mood.

Does CBT stop hot flushes from happening?

CBT does not usually stop hot flushes, but it reduces their impact. Clinical hypnosis and hormone therapy can reduce frequency.

Is CBT recommended for menopause symptoms?

Yes. The Menopause Society recommends CBT as an evidence-based non-hormonal option for hot flushes and night sweats.

Is CBT for hot flushes a short-term treatment?

Yes. CBT for hot flushes and night sweats is usually brief, often around four to six sessions, individually or in a group.

What is paced breathing for hot flushes?

Paced breathing for hot flushes means slow, gentle breathing, practised daily and used at the start of a flush to reduce its intensity.

Can CBT help night sweats?

Yes. CBT can help with night sweats by reducing distress and teaching strategies to settle back to sleep.

Is CBT for hot flushes an option after breast cancer?

Yes. CBT for hot flushes is a useful option for people who cannot use hormone therapy, including some breast cancer survivors.

Does hypnosis help hot flushes?

Yes. Clinical hypnosis has evidence for reducing hot flush frequency and severity and is recommended as a non-hormonal option.

Can I use CBT and hormone therapy together?

Yes. CBT for hot flushes can be combined with hormone therapy or other medical treatments.

Can CBT help with embarrassment about hot flushes at work?

Yes. CBT can help with embarrassment about hot flushes at work by changing worrying thoughts and building calm responses.
