Why Do I Feel Restless All the Time

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Even on vacation, even when nothing in particular is wrong, there is a low hum of unease that will not quite settle. You feel like you are always a little bit "on," even in moments that are supposed to be restful. If constant restlessness has become your baseline rather than an occasional state, it is worth understanding what could be driving it, since a few quite different things can produce a similar feeling.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Generalized anxiety. A persistent, free floating sense of unease, without necessarily being anchored to one specific worry, is a hallmark of generalized anxiety. The body can feel keyed up and unable to settle even when there is nothing obviously threatening in the moment.

Chronic stress and burnout. Extended periods of overload keep the body's stress response activated for far longer than it is meant to be, which can produce a persistent wired, restless feeling that does not resolve just because a particular deadline or stressor has passed.

Excess stimulant use. High or frequent caffeine intake, energy drinks, or other stimulants can produce an ongoing restless, jittery baseline that has little to do with anything psychological.

Hyperthyroidism. An overactive thyroid is a medical condition that commonly produces restlessness, a racing feeling, and difficulty settling, alongside other physical symptoms. This is worth ruling out with a doctor, particularly if the restlessness came on somewhat suddenly.

Agitated depression. Not all depression looks like low energy and withdrawal. Some people experience what is sometimes called agitated depression, where low mood is accompanied by restlessness, irritability, and an inability to settle, rather than the more commonly expected fatigue and flatness.

Substance withdrawal. Restlessness is a common withdrawal symptom from various substances, including caffeine itself, and is worth considering if it appeared around a change in use.

When It Looks More Like ADHD

Restlessness is part of the hyperactive-impulsive presentation of ADHD, but in this context it tends to look different from the anxiety described above in an important way. ADHD-related restlessness often functions more like an ongoing internal hum that is present fairly consistently, across relaxed situations and stressful ones alike, rather than being closely tied to specific worrying thoughts or stressful triggers.

Many adults with ADHD describe a persistent difficulty truly relaxing, even during downtime that is specifically meant for rest, along with a sense of needing to be doing something, or at least feeling like they should be, most of the time. This is not the same as anxious worry about a specific outcome. It is closer to an underlying restlessness that exists somewhat independently of what is actually happening in the person's life at that moment.

It is also worth being clear that ADHD and anxiety very commonly occur together, and each can make the other harder to spot and more intense to live with. A thorough assessment does not try to force a single explanation onto this kind of restlessness, but instead looks carefully at the whole pattern, including when it started, what makes it better or worse, and whether other ADHD or anxiety features are present, to figure out what is actually going on, which is sometimes more than one thing at once.

What Can Help

Regardless of the exact cause, a few things tend to help broadly: reducing unnecessary stimulant intake, building in genuinely restful downtime without guilt attached to it, and structuring some outlet for physical or mental energy rather than trying to suppress restlessness entirely. Beyond that, the most effective path forward depends heavily on identifying the specific underlying driver, since the strategies that help anxiety-driven restlessness are not necessarily the same ones that help ADHD-related restlessness, and a medical cause needs medical treatment rather than a psychological approach alone.

Common Questions About Constant Restlessness

Is constant restlessness usually anxiety, ADHD, or something else entirely?

It can be any of these, or a combination. Anxiety-driven restlessness tends to track with specific worries, ADHD-related restlessness tends to be a more consistent internal hum regardless of circumstances, and a range of medical and lifestyle factors can also produce a similar feeling.

Could a medical condition like a thyroid problem explain this?

Yes, this is worth ruling out with a doctor, particularly hyperthyroidism, which commonly produces restlessness and a racing feeling. It is especially worth checking if the restlessness appeared somewhat suddenly or came with other new physical symptoms.

Why can't I relax even when I'm supposed to be resting?

This can happen with both anxiety and ADHD, though for somewhat different reasons. In anxiety, the mind may still be scanning for concerns even during rest. In ADHD, an underlying restlessness or need to be doing something can persist even when there is nothing pressing at all.

Is it possible to have both anxiety and ADHD at the same time?

Yes, this is very common, and each condition can intensify and mask the other. A thorough assessment considers both possibilities together rather than assuming one automatically explains all of the symptoms.

Does caffeine or other stimulants make restlessness worse?

Yes, for most people. High or frequent stimulant intake can produce or worsen a restless, jittery baseline on its own, independent of any underlying psychological or medical cause, and is often one of the easier factors to adjust and test.

Can burnout cause the same restless, wired feeling as ADHD?

Yes. Extended periods of stress keep the body's stress response system activated well beyond the original stressor, which can produce a persistent restless, wired feeling that has a lot in common, at least on the surface, with ADHD-related restlessness.

Is restlessness during depression different from restlessness during anxiety?

It can be. In some forms of depression, restlessness appears alongside irritability and an inability to settle, rather than the more commonly expected low energy and withdrawal. This differs somewhat from anxiety, where restlessness is more often tied to worried, racing thoughts.

What does “agitated depression” mean?

It refers to a presentation of depression where restlessness, irritability, and difficulty settling are prominent, rather than the fatigue and flatness more commonly associated with depression. It is a recognized pattern, not a separate diagnosis.

How does an assessment distinguish between these overlapping causes?

An assessment looks at when the restlessness started, what makes it better or worse, whether it is tied to specific worries or present more consistently, and whether other features of anxiety, depression, or ADHD are also present, alongside ruling out relevant medical causes.

What kind of support helps with chronic restlessness regardless of the cause?

Reducing unnecessary stimulant intake, building in genuine rest without guilt, and finding a healthy outlet for physical or mental energy tend to help broadly. Beyond that, treatment works best when it targets the specific underlying cause identified through a proper assessment.
