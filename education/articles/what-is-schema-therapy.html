What Is Schema Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Schema therapy is an integrative approach that helps people identify and change deep, long-standing patterns, called schemas, that often develop when core emotional needs are not met in childhood. Schemas are broad beliefs and feelings about yourself and others, such as "I am defective," "people will abandon me," or "my needs do not matter," that shape how you react, whom you are drawn to, and how you cope. Schema therapy was developed by psychologist Jeffrey Young, combining elements of cognitive behavioural therapy, attachment theory, psychodynamic ideas, and experiential techniques. It is often used for chronic difficulties that have not improved with shorter therapies, including personality-related patterns.

How Schema Therapy Works

Schema therapy is based on the idea that children have core needs, such as safety, connection, autonomy, self-expression, and realistic limits. When these needs are repeatedly unmet, through neglect, criticism, instability, overprotection, or trauma, children form schemas to make sense of their experiences. These patterns can continue long after childhood, as explored on the page about childhood emotional neglect.

Schemas are kept alive through coping styles, such as surrendering to the schema, avoiding situations that trigger it, or overcompensating by acting in the opposite direction. For example, someone with a defectiveness schema might accept harsh treatment, avoid close relationships, or become perfectionistic and critical of others. Schema therapy also describes modes, the emotional states people shift between, such as a vulnerable child mode, an angry child mode, a punitive critic mode, or a healthy adult mode.

Therapy helps people recognize their schemas and modes, understand where they came from, meet unmet needs in healthier ways, and strengthen the healthy adult who can make new choices. The work often addresses patterns described on the page about chronic self-criticism.

What Sessions Are Like

Early sessions involve assessment, including questionnaires about schemas and exploration of your history and current difficulties. Together, you and the therapist develop an understanding of how your schemas formed and how they show up today.

Later sessions combine several techniques:

  • Cognitive techniques. Examining evidence for and against schemas and developing more balanced views.
  • Experiential techniques. Imagery rescripting, where you revisit painful memories and imagine needs being met, and chair work, where different modes speak to each other.
  • Behavioural pattern-breaking. Trying new ways of acting in relationships and daily life.
  • Limited reparenting. Within professional boundaries, the therapist offers some of the warmth, validation, and limits that were missing, which helps heal schemas.

Schema therapy is often longer-term than CBT, commonly lasting one to three years for complex patterns, though shorter formats and group schema therapy also exist.

Progress in schema therapy often shows up as noticing a schema being triggered and choosing a different response, such as speaking up instead of withdrawing, or treating yourself kindly after a mistake. Over time, the healthy adult mode grows stronger, and old patterns lose some of their pull.

Who It Can Help

Schema therapy has research support for borderline personality disorder and is used for other personality-related difficulties, chronic depression, long-standing anxiety, relationship patterns, and eating disorders. It suits people whose difficulties feel deeply rooted and repeat across relationships and life stages, including patterns described on the page about individual therapy for relationship patterns.

Because it can be intensive and long-term, schema therapy may not be the first choice for single, recent problems, which often respond well to shorter approaches such as CBT. People in crisis may need stabilization first. For some, compassion-focused or psychodynamic approaches may be a better fit.

Common Questions About Schema Therapy

What is a schema in schema therapy?

In schema therapy, a schema is a deep, long-standing belief and feeling about yourself or others, often formed when childhood needs were unmet.

Who developed schema therapy?

Schema therapy was developed by psychologist Jeffrey Young, combining CBT, attachment theory, psychodynamic ideas, and experiential techniques.

Why do I seem to flip between being a scared child and a harsh critic, and how does schema therapy explain it?

Schema therapy calls these shifting states modes, such as vulnerable child, punitive critic, or healthy adult, and helps strengthen the healthy adult.

What is imagery rescripting in schema therapy?

Imagery rescripting in schema therapy involves revisiting painful memories and imagining needs being met, which helps change schemas.

What does limited reparenting mean in schema therapy?

Limited reparenting means the schema therapist offers some of the warmth, validation, and limits that were missing, within professional boundaries.

Why is schema therapy often a longer commitment than other therapies?

Schemas are deep patterns formed over many years, so schema therapy for complex difficulties often lasts one to three years, though shorter and group formats exist.

What does research show about schema therapy for BPD?

Yes. Schema therapy has research support for borderline personality disorder and is used for other personality-related difficulties.

I did CBT but my deep feelings of being defective never shifted. Could schema therapy go further?

It may. Schema therapy builds on CBT but works more deeply with childhood origins, emotions, and the therapy relationship, which can help long-standing schemas shift.

What are schema coping styles?

Schema coping styles are surrendering to a schema, avoiding its triggers, or overcompensating by acting in the opposite direction.

Who is schema therapy best suited for?

Schema therapy suits people with deep, long-standing patterns that repeat across relationships and have not improved with shorter therapies.
