How to Know If Couples Therapy Is Working

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Couples therapy is usually working when arguments become less intense or frequent, you recover from conflict more quickly, you each feel more heard and understood, you can talk about hard topics without things spiralling, and you notice more moments of warmth, humour, or closeness. Progress is rarely a straight line, and it is common for things to feel harder before they improve as painful topics are opened up. Signs of progress may show up first in small ways, such as one calmer conversation or an apology that lands better. A good therapist will check in on progress regularly and adjust the approach if things feel stuck.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

  • Conflict changes. Arguments are shorter, less heated, and less likely to involve criticism, contempt, defensiveness, or shutting down.
  • Repair improves. One of you reaches out after a disagreement, and the other is more able to accept it.
  • You understand the pattern. You can name the cycle you get stuck in and catch it earlier, as explored on the page about navigating conflict styles.
  • Emotional safety grows. You feel more comfortable expressing needs and vulnerable feelings.
  • Connection returns. More affection, shared time, or interest in each other's day.
  • Goals move forward. Whether that is rebuilding trust, making a decision, or co-parenting more smoothly.

Sometimes therapy helps a couple realize they want to separate. When that happens with less conflict and more clarity, it can still be a meaningful outcome.

Practical Details

Timing. Some couples notice early changes within a few sessions, while bigger shifts often take a couple of months. Recovery from betrayal usually takes longer, as discussed on the page about rebuilding trust after a betrayal. The overall timeline is described on the page about how long couples therapy usually takes.

Measuring progress. Some therapists use brief questionnaires to track relationship satisfaction over time. You can also rate how things are going every few sessions.

Frequency. Infrequent sessions early on can slow progress, as explained on the page about how often couples attend therapy.

Effort between sessions. Much of the change happens at home, when you practise new ways of talking and responding.

Individual factors. Untreated depression, anxiety, trauma, ADHD, or substance use in one partner can slow couples work. Individual therapy alongside couples sessions may help.

What to Do If It Feels Stuck

  • Raise it in session. Tell your therapist honestly that progress feels slow. This often leads to useful adjustments.
  • Revisit goals. Make sure you are both working toward the same outcomes.
  • Look at what happens between sessions. Are you practising, or falling back into old habits?
  • Consider fit. If one partner feels the therapist takes sides or does not understand them, discuss it. A different therapist or approach, such as the Gottman Method or emotionally focused therapy, may suit you better.
  • Address safety. If there is abuse or fear, joint sessions may not be appropriate. See the abuse resources page.

Common Questions About Progress in Couples Therapy

How do I know if couples therapy is working?

Couples therapy is likely working if conflict is calmer, you recover from arguments faster, you feel more understood, and closeness is returning.

How soon should couples see progress in therapy?

Some couples notice small changes within a few sessions, while larger shifts often take a couple of months or longer.

Is it normal for a relationship to feel worse during couples therapy?

Yes. A relationship can feel harder for a time during couples therapy as painful topics are opened up, before improvement follows.

What are early signs of progress in couples therapy?

Early signs of progress in couples therapy include one calmer conversation, a better-received apology, or catching a conflict cycle sooner.

Can couples therapy be successful if we separate?

Yes. Couples therapy can still be meaningful if it helps partners separate with less conflict and more clarity.

What should we do if couples therapy feels stuck?

Tell your couples therapist, revisit goals, look at what happens between sessions, and consider whether the approach or fit needs to change.

Should we switch couples therapists if one partner feels unheard?

Discuss it first with your couples therapist. If one partner still feels unheard or that the therapist takes sides, a different therapist may help.

Can one partner's mental health slow couples therapy progress?

Yes. Untreated depression, anxiety, trauma, ADHD, or substance use in one partner can slow couples therapy, and individual therapy may help.

Do couples therapists measure progress?

Some couples therapists use brief questionnaires to track relationship satisfaction over time and check in on progress regularly.

How much of couples therapy progress happens at home?

A lot. Much of the change in couples therapy happens when partners practise new ways of talking and responding at home.
