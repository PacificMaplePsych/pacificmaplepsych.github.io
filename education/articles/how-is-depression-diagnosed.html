How Is Depression Diagnosed

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Depression is diagnosed by a health professional, such as a family doctor, psychiatrist, psychologist, or nurse practitioner, based on a detailed conversation about your mood, interests, sleep, energy, thinking, and daily life. There is no blood test or scan that confirms depression. Clinicians look at how long symptoms have lasted, how severe they are, and how much they affect functioning. They also check for medical causes and other conditions that can look similar. Questionnaires are often used to support the conversation. A diagnosis can help you understand what is happening and access effective treatment.

What Clinicians Look For

  • Core symptoms. Persistent low mood, sadness, or emptiness, and loss of interest or pleasure in activities, for most of the day, most days, over at least two weeks.
  • Other symptoms. Changes in sleep or appetite, fatigue, slowed thinking or restlessness, trouble concentrating, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, and thoughts of death or suicide.
  • Impact on daily life. How symptoms affect work, studies, relationships, and self-care.
  • Duration and pattern. Whether symptoms are a single episode, recurring, long-lasting at a lower intensity, or linked with seasons, hormonal changes, or life events.
  • Physical symptoms. Depression often includes aches, digestive changes, or low energy, as described on the page about physical symptoms of depression.
  • Safety. Clinicians ask directly about thoughts of suicide or self-harm. This is a routine and caring part of assessment, not a judgment.
  • History of high or elevated mood. Periods of unusually high energy, reduced need for sleep, or impulsivity may point to bipolar disorder, which changes treatment. The page about bipolar II disorder explains this.
  • Other explanations. Grief, burnout, anxiety, substance use, medications, and medical conditions such as thyroid problems or anemia.

What the Process Involves

  1. A conversation about symptoms. The clinician asks what you have been experiencing, when it started, and how it affects your life. Bringing notes can help if it is hard to remember details.
  2. Questionnaires. Standardized screening tools can measure the severity of symptoms and track progress later.
  3. History. Questions about past episodes, family history of mood conditions, life events, relationships, substance use, and medications.
  4. Medical checks. A physician may order blood tests to rule out conditions such as thyroid problems, vitamin deficiencies, or anemia.
  5. Safety planning where needed. If thoughts of suicide are present, the clinician may work with you on a plan to stay safe.
  6. Discussion of findings and next steps. Including whether depression is present, its type and severity, and treatment options.

Many people start with a family doctor, who can assess, rule out medical causes, and refer to other services. Psychologists and psychiatrists can offer more detailed assessments when the picture is complex. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse if you are unsure where to begin.

It can be hard to talk about depression, especially if you feel ashamed or worry about being judged. Clinicians hear about depression often, and honest answers help them understand what will help. It is fine to say you find it difficult to talk about.

What a Diagnosis Can and Cannot Tell You

A diagnosis can name what you have been experiencing and explain why everyday tasks have felt so hard. It opens access to treatment, including therapy, medication prescribed by a physician, and practical supports. It can also help with workplace or school accommodations. Many people find relief in learning that depression is a common, treatable condition and not a personal failing. More about the condition itself is on the page about major depressive disorder.

A diagnosis cannot capture everything about your experience or explain every cause. Depression looks different from person to person, and the label is a starting point for understanding rather than a full story. Sometimes the picture changes over time, for example if other symptoms emerge. It can also be hard to separate depression from burnout or grief. Some people seek support without a formal diagnosis, and therapy can still help.

Common Questions About Depression Diagnosis

Who can tell me whether I have depression?

Family doctors, psychiatrists, psychologists, and nurse practitioners can assess and diagnose depression.

Is there a blood test that shows depression?

No. Blood tests can rule out other causes, but depression is diagnosed through a clinical conversation.

How long do I need to feel low before it counts as depression?

Clinicians usually look for symptoms most of the day, most days, for at least two weeks, along with other signs.

Why does my doctor ask me about thoughts of suicide?

It is a routine, caring part of assessment that helps keep you safe and guides treatment.

What questionnaires are used when checking for depression?

Standardized screening forms ask about mood, interest, sleep, and energy, and help measure severity.

Why would my doctor order blood work for low mood?

Blood tests can check for thyroid problems, anemia, vitamin deficiencies, or other medical causes of fatigue and low mood.

Why am I asked about times I felt unusually energetic?

Past periods of elevated mood may suggest bipolar disorder, which is treated differently.

What should I bring to an appointment about my mood?

Notes about your symptoms, when they started, how they affect you, medications, and any family history can help.

Can I get help for low mood without a formal diagnosis?

Yes. Therapy and support can help whether or not you have a formal diagnosis.

What happens after I am diagnosed with depression?

You and your clinician discuss treatment options, which may include therapy, medication, and practical supports.
