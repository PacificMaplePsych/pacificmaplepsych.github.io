What Are Mental Compulsions

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Mental compulsions are repetitive acts performed in the mind to reduce anxiety or distress caused by obsessions, the unwanted thoughts, images, or urges that are central to obsessive-compulsive disorder. Unlike visible compulsions, such as handwashing or checking locks, mental compulsions happen silently. Examples include mentally reviewing past events, repeating special words or prayers, counting, or arguing with a thought to prove it is not true. Because they are invisible, mental compulsions are often missed, even by the person doing them. Recognizing them is an important step in effective OCD treatment.

What Mental Compulsions Look Like

  • Reviewing. Going over past events or conversations to make sure you did nothing wrong, similar to replaying conversations over and over.
  • Neutralizing. Replacing a bad thought with a good one, or saying a phrase to cancel it out.
  • Mental checking. Checking your feelings or reactions to see whether an intrusive thought means something.
  • Self-reassurance. Repeatedly telling yourself you would never do something, or seeking certainty.
  • Counting or repeating. Silently counting, praying, or repeating words a set number of times.
  • Analyzing. Trying to figure out why a thought occurred or what it says about you.
  • Mental list-making or planning to prevent feared outcomes.

Mental compulsions often accompany intrusive thoughts that feel frightening. They can take up hours each day and leave people exhausted, distracted, and anxious.

Why It Happens

OCD works through a cycle. An intrusive thought triggers anxiety, doubt, or disgust. A compulsion, whether visible or mental, brings brief relief. That relief teaches the brain that the compulsion was necessary, making the obsession feel more important and the urge to repeat the compulsion stronger. Over time, the cycle strengthens. Mental compulsions follow the same pattern as physical ones, such as checking things over and over, but happen inside the mind.

Seeking reassurance from others is closely related. People may ask loved ones the same questions repeatedly, as described on the page about needing constant reassurance, or search online for answers. When reassurance is not available, the mind may try to provide it internally. Mental compulsions are especially common in forms of OCD focused on harm, relationships, morality, or identity.

Because mental compulsions can look like ordinary thinking, people often do not realize they are part of OCD. They may describe themselves as overthinkers or worriers. A key clue is the purpose: a mental compulsion is done to reduce anxiety or prevent a feared outcome, and it tends to feel urgent and repetitive rather than helpful.

What They Are Not

  • Not ordinary reflection. Healthy reflection leads somewhere. Compulsive reviewing loops without resolution.
  • Not a sign that obsessions are true. The urge to check reflects anxiety, not meaning.
  • Not the same as worry, though they can look similar. Mental compulsions are driven by the need to neutralize or gain certainty.
  • Not something willpower alone can stop. Structured treatment helps.

How They Are Assessed and Treated

A clinician experienced in OCD will ask about obsessions, visible and mental compulsions, avoidance, and how much time they take. Because mental rituals are hidden, it can take time to identify them all. Exposure and response prevention is the most effective treatment. It involves gradually facing triggers and intrusive thoughts while resisting both visible and mental compulsions. Resisting mental rituals can involve practising acceptance of uncertainty, gently redirecting attention, and allowing thoughts to be present without engaging. Cognitive therapy and acceptance and commitment therapy can support this work. Medication may also help some people.

Common Questions About Mental Compulsions

What kinds of rituals can happen only inside the mind?

Examples include mentally reviewing events, neutralizing bad thoughts, silent counting or praying, self-reassurance, and analyzing why a thought occurred.

How are mental compulsions different from obsessions?

Obsessions are unwanted thoughts that cause distress. Mental compulsions are acts done in the mind to reduce that distress.

Can you have OCD with only mental compulsions?

Yes. Some people have mainly or only mental compulsions, which is sometimes called Pure O, although compulsions are still present.

Why do mental compulsions make OCD worse?

They bring brief relief, which teaches the brain that the obsession was dangerous and the ritual necessary, strengthening the cycle.

Is mentally reviewing past events a compulsion?

It can be. Reviewing becomes a compulsion when it is repetitive, driven by anxiety, and aimed at gaining certainty.

How can I tell if I am overthinking or doing a mental ritual?

Mental rituals feel urgent, repetitive, and aimed at reducing anxiety or preventing harm, rather than solving a real problem.

How does ERP work for mental compulsions?

ERP involves facing triggers while resisting mental rituals, allowing anxiety to rise and fall without neutralizing or reviewing.

Is asking for reassurance a compulsion?

Repeatedly seeking reassurance to reduce OCD anxiety functions as a compulsion and keeps the cycle going.

Why are mental compulsions hard to stop?

They are invisible, fast, and often automatic, so it takes practice to notice and resist them.

Can medication help with mental compulsions?

For some people, medications prescribed for OCD can reduce the intensity of obsessions and compulsions, alongside therapy.
