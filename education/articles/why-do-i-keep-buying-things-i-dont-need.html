Why Do I Keep Buying Things I Don't Need

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Buying things you do not need usually happens because shopping briefly changes how you feel, offering excitement, comfort, or relief from boredom or stress. Online stores, one-click checkout, and targeted ads make that relief easier to reach than ever. For many people, this is an occasional habit. For some, it becomes compulsive buying, a pattern of repeated urges to shop that feel hard to resist and lead to financial strain, clutter, guilt, and conflict. Understanding what the shopping does for you is the key to changing it.

People describe browsing late at night and buying without thinking, feeling a rush when a package arrives followed by guilt, hiding purchases from family, or owning many items with the tags still on.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Marketing and easy access. Sales, limited-time offers, saved payment details, and personalized ads are designed to encourage quick purchases.

Emotional regulation. Shopping can soothe stress, sadness, loneliness, or anger for a short time. This is sometimes called retail therapy.

ADHD and impulsivity. Adults with ADHD often find it harder to pause before buying and may lose track of spending. People who make impulsive decisions they later regret may recognize this.

Mood episodes. A sudden spending spree alongside little need for sleep and high energy can be a sign of hypomania, which is worth discussing with a doctor.

Low self-esteem. Buying clothes, products, or status items can be an attempt to feel better about yourself or fit in.

Money anxiety. Some people avoid looking at their finances, which lets spending continue unnoticed. The page on feeling anxious about money explores this.

When It Looks More Like Compulsive Buying

Compulsive buying, sometimes called shopping addiction, involves repeated, hard-to-control urges to buy, often followed by regret. It is often discussed alongside OCD and related conditions because it shares features with compulsive behaviours, and clinicians also compare it to behavioural addictions such as gambling. Signs include:

  • Frequent preoccupation with shopping or planning purchases.
  • Buying more than you can afford, or items you do not use.
  • A build-up of tension before buying and relief or excitement afterward.
  • Guilt, shame, or hiding purchases from others.
  • Financial problems, debt, or conflict with family about spending.
  • Repeated failed attempts to cut back.

Compulsive buying can also lead to clutter. When someone struggles not only to stop buying but also to let go of possessions, the page on struggling to get rid of things may be relevant. The pattern often coexists with depression, anxiety, or ADHD, and treating those can make the urges more manageable.

What Can Help

  • Add a waiting period. Wait 24 to 48 hours before buying non-essential items. Many urges fade.
  • Remove easy access. Delete saved payment details, unsubscribe from marketing emails, and remove shopping apps.
  • Track spending. Seeing where money goes, without judgment, builds awareness.
  • Notice the feeling. Before buying, ask what you are feeling and what you hope the purchase will change.
  • Find other ways to cope. A walk, a call, or a hobby can meet the same emotional need.
  • Get practical help with debt. Non-profit credit counselling can help if spending has created financial strain.

Common Questions About Buying Things You Do Not Need

Why do I buy things I don't need when I'm stressed?

Shopping can bring a brief sense of excitement or comfort that eases stress. Because the relief is short-lived, the urge often returns, creating a cycle of buying and regret.

What is compulsive buying disorder?

Compulsive buying involves repeated, hard-to-control urges to shop, leading to distress, financial problems, or conflict. It is sometimes called shopping addiction and is treatable.

Is retail therapy harmful?

Occasional shopping to lift your mood is common and usually harmless. It becomes a problem when it is the main way you cope with feelings or when spending causes financial or relationship harm.

Why do adults with ADHD often struggle with impulse purchases?

Yes. ADHD can affect impulse control, planning, and keeping track of money, which makes impulsive purchases and missed bills more likely.

Can a sudden shopping spree be a sign of bipolar disorder?

It can be. Spending sprees combined with high energy, little need for sleep, and racing thoughts may indicate hypomania or mania, which should be assessed by a doctor.

Why do I feel guilty after I buy something?

Guilt often follows impulsive buying when the purchase conflicts with your budget or values. The guilt can then trigger more shopping to feel better, keeping the cycle going.

How can I stop online impulse shopping?

Remove saved payment information, unsubscribe from marketing emails, use a waiting period before buying, and delete shopping apps from your phone to add friction.

Is compulsive buying related to hoarding?

They can overlap. Some people who buy compulsively also struggle to discard items, which can lead to clutter. Not everyone who overspends has difficulty letting things go.

Who can help if my shopping has led to debt?

Non-profit credit counselling organizations can help create a budget and repayment plan. Your bank may also be able to discuss options for managing debt.

What does treatment for compulsive shopping involve?

Therapy, often cognitive behavioural, helps identify triggers, reduce urges, build coping skills, and address related concerns such as anxiety, depression, or ADHD.
