Treatment for Postpartum Depression and Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide or of harming yourself or your baby, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency. Confusion, hallucinations, or unusual beliefs after birth need emergency care. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse any time.

Postpartum depression and anxiety are treated with psychotherapy, such as cognitive behavioural therapy or interpersonal therapy, medication prescribed by a physician when needed, and practical and social support, such as help with night feeds, protected sleep, and peer groups. These conditions are among the most common complications of having a baby, and they respond well to treatment. Most parents recover fully, and earlier support usually leads to faster improvement. Treatment is tailored to the severity of symptoms, whether you are breastfeeding, your history, and your preferences. Seeking help is an act of care for yourself and your family.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps with anxious and depressive thinking, such as constant worry about the baby, harsh self-criticism, or feeling like a bad parent. It also addresses avoidance and builds coping skills. It has good evidence for both postpartum depression and postpartum anxiety.

Interpersonal therapy. This approach focuses on role changes, relationships, and support during the transition to parenthood, and has strong research support for perinatal depression.

ERP for postpartum OCD. When intrusive, unwanted thoughts and compulsions are central, exposure and response prevention is recommended, as described on the page about postpartum OCD.

Medication. For moderate to severe symptoms, a physician may recommend an antidepressant. Many options are considered compatible with breastfeeding, and physicians weigh benefits and risks. General comparisons are covered on the page about medication versus therapy for depression.

Practical support. Help with night feeds, meals, household tasks, and childcare, along with protected stretches of sleep, can make a significant difference.

Peer and group support. New parent groups and perinatal support groups reduce isolation.

Couples and family support. Involving partners helps with communication, sharing the load, and understanding symptoms.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan begins with an assessment of symptoms, severity, safety, sleep, feeding, supports, and past mental health history. Screening questionnaires are often used by family doctors, midwives, and public health nurses. Mild to moderate symptoms often respond well to therapy and practical support. Moderate to severe symptoms may benefit from therapy combined with medication. A history of bipolar disorder or symptoms such as confusion or unusual beliefs need urgent specialist care. Practical factors matter too, such as whether virtual sessions are easier, whether babies are welcome in sessions, and childcare. More about therapy during this time is on the page about therapy support during the perinatal period.

What Recovery Usually Looks Like

Many parents begin to feel better within several weeks of starting treatment. Early signs of progress can include less constant worry, more moments of enjoyment with the baby, better sleep when possible, and feeling more like yourself. Medication usually takes a few weeks to help. Recovery is not always steady, since sleep deprivation, feeding challenges, and illness can bring harder days. Setbacks do not mean treatment has failed. Most parents recover fully, and many find the experience deepens their understanding of their needs.

Supporting Your Own Recovery

Accepting help and asking for specific support, such as a night off from feeds, protects sleep and energy. Eating regularly, getting outside, and gentle movement support mood. Connecting with other parents reduces isolation. Being honest with your care team about how you feel, including intrusive thoughts or low mood, helps them support you. Remember that postpartum depression and anxiety are medical conditions, not signs of being a bad parent.

Common Questions About Postpartum Depression and Anxiety Treatment

What are the main treatments for postpartum depression and anxiety?

Postpartum depression and anxiety are treated with therapy such as CBT or interpersonal therapy, medication when needed, and practical and social support.

Will I have to stop nursing if my doctor recommends medication for postpartum depression?

Often not. Many antidepressants are considered compatible with breastfeeding, and a physician weighs benefits and risks for postpartum depression or anxiety.

How quickly does treatment for postpartum depression work?

Many parents feel better within several weeks of starting postpartum depression treatment, though recovery can be uneven.

Which therapy works best for postpartum anxiety?

CBT has good evidence for postpartum anxiety and helps with constant worry about the baby and avoidance.

How does interpersonal therapy help after having a baby?

Interpersonal therapy focuses on role changes, relationships, and support during the transition to parenthood, easing postpartum depression.

Why does protecting sleep matter for postpartum depression recovery?

Sleep deprivation can worsen postpartum mood and anxiety, so protected sleep is an important part of recovery.

Can my partner be involved in postpartum depression treatment?

Yes. Involving partners in postpartum depression treatment helps with communication, sharing tasks, and understanding symptoms.

What signs after giving birth mean I should go to the emergency department?

Confusion, hallucinations, unusual beliefs, or thoughts of harming yourself or your baby after birth need emergency care. Call 911.

Could a new parent group ease the loneliness of postpartum depression?

Often yes. New parent and postpartum groups reduce isolation and offer understanding from others going through similar experiences.

Will I fully recover from postpartum depression?

Most parents recover fully from postpartum depression and anxiety with treatment and support.
