Am I a Highly Sensitive Person

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You notice the hum of the fridge that no one else hears, feel drained after a busy day of errands, cry at films, and pick up on small changes in other people's moods. You may have been told you are "too sensitive." Psychologist Elaine Aron described this pattern as sensory processing sensitivity and called people with high levels of it highly sensitive people (HSPs). Research suggests roughly 15 to 20 percent of people have this trait. High sensitivity is not a disorder. It is a temperament that involves processing information deeply, and it comes with both strengths, such as empathy and creativity, and challenges, such as overwhelm.

Signs You May Be Highly Sensitive

  • Depth of processing. You reflect deeply, notice nuance, and take time to make decisions.
  • Easily overstimulated. Noise, crowds, bright lights, or busy schedules wear you out, as described on the page about sensory overload.
  • Emotional intensity. You feel joy, sadness, and empathy strongly, and may cry easily over small things.
  • Sensitivity to subtleties. You notice small details, changes in tone, or others' discomfort.
  • Need for downtime. You need quiet time to recharge, especially after socializing, as described on the page about feeling exhausted after socializing.
  • Strong reactions to caffeine, pain, or hunger.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

High sensitivity. A lifelong trait present in many areas of life, without significant impairment when your environment suits you.

Anxiety. Anxiety can also make people more alert to their surroundings and body sensations, but it involves worry and fear rather than simply deep processing.

Autism. Sensory sensitivity is common in autism, but autism also involves differences in social communication and a need for routine or intense interests.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD feel easily overwhelmed and emotionally intense, alongside attention and organization difficulties.

Trauma. Hypervigilance after trauma can look like sensitivity, as described on the page about hypervigilance.

These can overlap, and an assessment can help when you are unsure.

The Strengths of Sensitivity

Highly sensitive people are often empathetic, conscientious, creative, and attuned to beauty and meaning. Research suggests they may benefit more than others from supportive environments, a concept sometimes called differential susceptibility. In the right conditions, sensitivity can be a significant asset in relationships and work.

The Challenges

Highly sensitive people may struggle with overwhelm, burnout, compassion fatigue, perfectionism, and feeling misunderstood. In harsh or chaotic environments, they may be more prone to anxiety and low mood.

Sensitivity in Relationships and Work

In relationships, highly sensitive people often notice their partner's moods quickly and may feel hurt by criticism or conflict more intensely. Clear communication about needs, such as time to recharge after social events, can prevent misunderstandings. At work, open-plan offices, constant interruptions, and tight deadlines can be especially draining. Small adjustments, such as noise-cancelling headphones, scheduled focus time, or breaks between meetings, can make a significant difference. Many highly sensitive people thrive in roles that value empathy, attention to detail, and thoughtful decision-making when their environment allows them to work at their best.

What Helps

  • Plan for recovery time after busy or stimulating activities.
  • Shape your environment. Use noise-cancelling headphones, softer lighting, and calm spaces.
  • Set boundaries around commitments and social time, as described on the page about how to set boundaries without guilt.
  • Name emotions and give them space rather than suppressing them.
  • Choose supportive relationships and work that value your strengths.
  • Practise self-compassion instead of criticizing yourself for being sensitive.

When to Seek Support

If sensitivity leads to frequent overwhelm, anxiety, low mood, or burnout, therapy can help you build coping strategies and self-acceptance. If you wonder whether autism, ADHD, or anxiety might be part of the picture, an assessment can provide clarity.

Common Questions About Highly Sensitive People

What is sensory processing sensitivity?

Sensory processing sensitivity is the research term for the highly sensitive person trait, describing deep processing of information, strong emotional responses, and being easily overstimulated.

Is being highly sensitive a disorder?

No. Being highly sensitive is a normal temperament trait, not a mental health disorder.

How common are highly sensitive people?

Research suggests roughly 15 to 20 percent of people are highly sensitive.

How do I know if I am a highly sensitive person?

You may be a highly sensitive person if you notice subtle details, feel emotions deeply, need downtime after busy days, and are easily overwhelmed by noise or crowds.

Is being highly sensitive the same as autism?

No. Both can involve sensory sensitivity, but autism also includes differences in social communication and routines.

Can highly sensitive people have anxiety?

Yes. Highly sensitive people may be more prone to anxiety in stressful environments, though sensitivity itself is not anxiety.

What are the strengths of highly sensitive people?

Highly sensitive people are often empathetic, conscientious, creative, and attuned to beauty and meaning.

How can a highly sensitive person avoid burnout?

Highly sensitive people can avoid burnout by planning recovery time, setting boundaries, and shaping calmer environments.

Who developed the idea of the highly sensitive person?

Psychologist Elaine Aron developed the concept of the highly sensitive person and sensory processing sensitivity.

Can therapy help highly sensitive people?

Yes. Therapy can help highly sensitive people manage overwhelm, set boundaries, and build self-acceptance.
