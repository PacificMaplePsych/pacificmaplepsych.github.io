Burnout and Perfectionism

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Perfectionism and burnout are closely connected. Perfectionism involves setting extremely high standards and judging yourself harshly when you do not meet them. It often comes with a belief that your worth depends on performance. Burnout is a state of exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness caused by chronic stress that has not been managed. Research consistently finds that certain kinds of perfectionism, especially the self-critical kind, increase the risk of burnout. Perfectionists often push harder when tired, take on too much, and find it hard to rest, which can lead straight to depletion. Understanding this link can help people protect their wellbeing without giving up on doing good work.

What It Looks Like

  • Holding yourself to standards far higher than those you would expect of others.
  • Feeling like you have to be perfect to be acceptable.
  • Working long hours to get details just right.
  • Difficulty delegating or accepting help.
  • Harsh self-criticism after small mistakes.
  • Procrastinating because of fear that work will not be good enough.
  • Feeling guilty when you rest or take time off.
  • Ignoring signs of exhaustion until you crash.
  • Feeling that success is never enough and quickly moving the goalposts.

Why the Two Are Linked

Researchers distinguish between perfectionistic strivings, which involve high standards and a drive to excel, and perfectionistic concerns, which involve fear of mistakes, harsh self-evaluation, and worry about others' judgment. Perfectionistic concerns are most strongly linked with burnout, anxiety, and depression. When self-worth depends on flawless performance, every task feels high stakes, and stress stays constantly elevated.

Perfectionism also shapes behaviour in ways that drain energy. Perfectionists tend to take on extra responsibility, work longer hours, avoid delegating, and skip breaks. They may find it hard to say no or set boundaries. When they start to feel exhausted, they often interpret it as a personal failing and push harder rather than slowing down. Over time, this pattern leads to the exhaustion and detachment of burnout. High achievers are particularly vulnerable, as described on the page about burnout in high achievers.

Perfectionism often develops early. Children who received praise mainly for achievements, who faced harsh criticism, or who felt they had to be good to be loved may learn that mistakes are dangerous. Cultural and workplace pressures can reinforce these beliefs in adulthood.

Burnout can also make perfectionism feel worse. As energy drops, it becomes harder to meet high standards, which increases self-criticism and anxiety. People may try to compensate by working even longer, deepening exhaustion. Recognizing this loop is often a turning point, because it shows that pushing harder is part of the problem rather than the solution.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as having high standards. Healthy striving is flexible and allows for mistakes.
  • Not a guarantee of better work. Perfectionism often slows progress and increases procrastination.
  • Not a personal failing when burnout happens. Burnout reflects unsustainable patterns and conditions.
  • Not something you must give up entirely. The goal is to keep healthy ambition while softening harsh self-judgment.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Recovery from burnout involves reducing demands, restoring rest, and addressing workplace factors. Addressing perfectionism helps prevent relapse. Cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism helps people identify rigid rules, test beliefs such as mistakes are unacceptable, and experiment with good enough. Behavioural experiments, such as leaving a small task imperfect and noticing what happens, can loosen perfectionistic habits. Self-compassion practices reduce harsh self-criticism. Setting clear boundaries around work hours, delegating, and scheduling rest help protect energy. Acceptance and commitment therapy can help people connect with values beyond achievement. Over time, many people find they can work well without driving themselves to exhaustion.

Common Questions About Burnout and Perfectionism

Does perfectionism cause burnout?

Perfectionism, especially the self-critical kind, is a strong personal risk factor for burnout, though work conditions also matter.

What is the difference between perfectionistic strivings and concerns?

Strivings involve high standards and drive. Concerns involve fear of mistakes and harsh self-judgment, which are more linked to burnout.

Why do perfectionists push harder when they are tired?

When worth depends on performance, exhaustion feels like failure, so people often work harder instead of resting.

Why do I put off tasks I actually care about doing well?

Fear that work will not be good enough can make starting tasks feel overwhelming.

How can I lower my standards without feeling like a failure?

Experiment with good enough on small tasks, notice the results, and remind yourself that flexibility supports quality over time.

Is it lazy to stop working before everything is perfect?

No. Perfectionism often links rest with laziness, but breaks are essential for sustained, high-quality work.

Is perfectionism common in high achievers?

Yes. Many high achievers have perfectionistic tendencies, which can increase their risk of burnout.

How does CBT help with perfectionism?

CBT helps identify rigid rules, test beliefs about mistakes, and practise more flexible standards.

Can self-compassion reduce burnout?

Yes. Self-compassion reduces harsh self-criticism and helps people respond to stress with care rather than pressure.

Will I lose my edge if I let go of perfectionism?

Most people find they can keep ambition and quality while reducing anxiety and exhaustion.
