What Is Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Sudden new episodes of confusion, memory loss, or feeling unreal, especially with headache, weakness, or changes in speech or vision, should be checked urgently by a doctor. Call 911 in an emergency.

Depersonalization-derealization disorder is a dissociative condition in which a person has persistent or recurring experiences of feeling detached from themselves, their surroundings, or both. Depersonalization is feeling unreal or disconnected from your own thoughts, body, or emotions. Derealization is feeling that the world around you is unreal, distant, or dreamlike. Throughout these experiences, people know that what they are feeling is not literally true, which separates the condition from psychosis. Brief episodes are common, especially during stress or exhaustion. The disorder is diagnosed when experiences are persistent and cause significant distress. It is treatable.

What It Looks Like

Depersonalization may feel like:

  • Watching yourself from outside your body, as if you are an observer.
  • Feeling robotic, numb, or disconnected from your emotions.
  • Your body or voice not feeling like your own.
  • Memories feeling as if they happened to someone else.

Derealization may feel like:

  • The world looking flat, foggy, or like a film set.
  • Feeling detached from reality or as if in a dream.
  • Familiar places or people seeming strange or unfamiliar.
  • Changes in how distance, size, or time are perceived.

These experiences are often very frightening. People may worry that they are going crazy, losing their mind, or will never feel normal again. That fear can make symptoms worse.

Why It Happens

Depersonalization and derealization are thought to be protective responses. When the nervous system is overwhelmed by stress, fear, or pain, it can dampen emotional and sensory experience to reduce distress. This is part of the broader process of dissociation. In the short term, this detachment can help a person get through a difficult moment. When it persists, it becomes distressing in itself.

Common triggers include severe stress, anxiety and panic attacks, childhood emotional abuse or neglect, trauma, depression, and extreme fatigue. Cannabis and some other drugs can trigger episodes, sometimes leading to lasting symptoms. Many people notice that anxiety about the symptoms themselves keeps the cycle going: feeling unreal causes fear, fear increases arousal, and increased arousal deepens detachment.

Symptoms often fluctuate. Many people notice they feel worse when tired, stressed, or in bright, busy environments, and better when absorbed in an activity or with trusted people. Keeping track of these patterns can help people identify their triggers and what helps.

Living with ongoing unreality can affect work, study, and relationships. Some people withdraw because conversations feel distant or effortful. Others keep functioning well on the outside while feeling detached inside, which can make it hard for others to understand. Explaining the experience to trusted people can reduce isolation.

What It Is Not

  • Not psychosis. People with this condition know their experiences are not literally real.
  • Not a sign of going crazy. It is a recognized, treatable condition.
  • Not brain damage. The experiences are distressing but do not harm the brain.
  • Not rare. Brief depersonalization is common, and the disorder affects around one to two percent of people.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about symptoms, triggers, history, and related conditions such as anxiety, depression, or trauma. A physician may rule out medical or neurological causes and substance effects. Treatment often focuses on reducing fear of the symptoms and addressing underlying stress. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps people understand the cycle and reduce checking and avoidance. Grounding techniques that engage the senses can help during episodes. Trauma-focused therapy may help when symptoms relate to past experiences. Treating co-occurring anxiety or depression often improves symptoms. Many people find that as fear decreases, the feelings of unreality fade.

Common Questions About Depersonalization-Derealization Disorder

Why do I feel like I am on autopilot and not really in my body?

That sense of unreality is called depersonalization. People describe feeling detached from their thoughts, body, or emotions, like watching themselves from outside or acting on autopilot.

Why does the world sometimes look foggy or like a film set?

This is called derealization. The world may seem foggy, flat, dreamlike, or artificial, and familiar places may feel strange.

Is depersonalization a sign of psychosis?

No. People with depersonalization know their experiences are not literally real, which distinguishes it from psychosis.

Can anxiety cause depersonalization and derealization?

Yes. Intense anxiety and panic are common triggers, and fear about the symptoms can keep them going.

How common is depersonalization-derealization disorder?

Brief episodes are very common. The persistent disorder affects around one to two percent of people.

Can cannabis trigger derealization?

Yes. Cannabis and some other drugs can trigger episodes, and for some people symptoms can persist afterward.

Will I ever feel real again?

Many people recover fully, especially as fear of the symptoms decreases and underlying stress or anxiety is treated.

What grounding techniques help during an episode?

Engaging the senses can help, such as holding something textured, naming things you see, or focusing on sounds around you.

Is childhood trauma linked with depersonalization?

Yes. Childhood emotional abuse and neglect are among the strongest risk factors for the persistent disorder.

Which therapies help feelings of unreality fade?

Treatment often includes cognitive behavioural therapy, grounding skills, trauma-focused therapy when relevant, and treating anxiety or depression.
