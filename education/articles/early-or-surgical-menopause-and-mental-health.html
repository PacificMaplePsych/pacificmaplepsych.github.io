Early or Surgical Menopause and Mental Health

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Early menopause means reaching menopause before age 45, and premature menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency means before 40. Surgical menopause happens immediately when both ovaries are removed, and menopause can also be brought on by chemotherapy, radiation, or medications that suppress hormones. Because hormone levels can drop suddenly rather than gradually, symptoms such as hot flushes, sleep problems, and mood changes may be more intense. Early or sudden menopause can also bring grief, especially around fertility, body image, and identity, and it often happens alongside a health crisis. Anxiety and depression are more common in these situations, and support can make a significant difference.

Why It Can Affect Mental Health

A Sudden Hormonal Drop

After surgery or some cancer treatments, estrogen falls abruptly, which can trigger strong physical symptoms and mood changes within days or weeks.

Grief and Loss

Losing fertility unexpectedly, or earlier than planned, can bring deep grief, even for people who were not planning more children. This grief can be invisible to others, as described on the page about disenfranchised grief.

Feeling Out of Step

Going through menopause while peers are having children or not yet thinking about menopause can feel isolating.

Health Worries

Many people with surgical or medically induced menopause are also coping with cancer, a genetic risk such as a BRCA mutation, endometriosis, or another serious condition, as described on the page about coping psychologically with chronic illness.

Body Image and Sexuality

Changes in the body, libido, and sexual comfort can affect confidence and relationships.

Preparing for Surgical Menopause

When surgical menopause is planned, such as for a BRCA mutation or another medical reason, there may be time to prepare. Talking with your physician about symptom management and hormone therapy options, learning what to expect, and arranging emotional support before surgery can make the transition easier. Some people find it helpful to speak with a therapist beforehand about fears, grief, and decisions related to fertility. Planning practical support for the weeks after surgery, when physical recovery and hormonal changes overlap, can also reduce stress.

What You Might Notice

  • Low mood, tearfulness, or irritability
  • Anxiety, including health anxiety
  • Sleep problems and fatigue, as described on the page about insomnia during menopause
  • Brain fog
  • Grief about fertility, plans, or identity
  • Changes in sexual desire or comfort
  • Feeling disconnected from your body or from others

What It Is Not

Struggling emotionally with early or surgical menopause is not an overreaction. These are significant physical and life changes, often arriving without time to prepare. Depression is also not inevitable, and with the right medical and emotional support, many people adjust well.

How It Is Supported

Medical Care

Hormone therapy is often recommended for people with early or premature menopause until around the typical age of menopause, unless there is a reason not to use it, such as some hormone-sensitive cancers. A physician can explain options and support bone and heart health.

Therapy

Therapy can help you grieve, adjust to changes in identity and relationships, manage anxiety and depression, and cope with treatment. Cognitive behavioural approaches can also help with hot flushes and sleep, as described on the page about CBT for hot flushes and night sweats. If fertility loss is part of your experience, the page about infertility and mental health may help.

Connection

Talking with others who have had early or surgical menopause can reduce isolation. Including your partner in conversations about changes in intimacy can also help.

Common Questions About Early and Surgical Menopause

What is early menopause?

Early menopause means reaching menopause before age 45. Menopause before 40 is called premature menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency.

Why does removing the ovaries cause immediate surgical menopause?

Surgical menopause happens immediately when both ovaries are removed because the main source of estrogen and progesterone is gone, causing a sudden hormone drop.

Is surgical menopause harder on mental health than natural menopause?

Surgical menopause can be harder for some people because hormones drop suddenly, often causing more intense symptoms and mood changes.

Does early menopause raise the risk of depression and anxiety?

Yes. Early menopause is linked to a higher risk of depression and anxiety, especially with sudden symptoms, grief, or health worries.

Why does early menopause bring grief about fertility?

Early menopause can end fertility unexpectedly or earlier than planned, so grief about children, plans, and identity is common and understandable.

Can chemotherapy cause menopause?

Yes. Chemotherapy, radiation, and some hormone-suppressing medications can cause menopause, temporarily or permanently.

Is hormone therapy recommended for early menopause?

Hormone therapy is often recommended for early menopause until around the typical age of menopause, unless there is a medical reason not to use it.

What does therapy focus on after surgical menopause?

Therapy after surgical menopause often focuses on grief, mood, anxiety, sleep, body image, and changes in relationships and intimacy.

How can I support my partner through early menopause?

Learn about early menopause, listen without minimizing, be patient with changes in mood and intimacy, and encourage medical and emotional support.

Can early menopause affect sexuality and relationships?

Yes. Early menopause can affect libido, sexual comfort, and confidence, and open communication and support can help.
