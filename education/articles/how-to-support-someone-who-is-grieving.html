How to Support Someone Who Is Grieving

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If the person you are supporting talks about suicide or not wanting to live, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, including for guidance on how to help. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911.

Supporting someone who is grieving usually means showing up, listening more than talking, offering specific practical help, saying the name of the person who died, and staying in touch long after the funeral. You cannot fix grief or take the pain away, and you do not need perfect words. What most grieving people remember is who reached out, who listened, and who kept checking in. Grief has no set timeline, and support often matters most in the weeks and months after the initial rush of attention fades.

Understanding What They Are Going Through

Grief affects emotions, the body, thinking, and daily functioning. A grieving person may feel deep sadness, numbness, anger, guilt, anxiety, or relief, sometimes all in the same day. They may have trouble sleeping, eating, or concentrating, and feel exhausted. Grief often comes in waves, sometimes triggered by reminders and sometimes out of nowhere, as described on the page about grief that hits out of nowhere. It does not move through neat stages, as explained on the page about grief and its stages.

Some losses are harder for others to recognize, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pet, or a pregnancy, and the person may feel their grief is not taken seriously. Sudden, traumatic, or stigmatized deaths, such as suicide or overdose, can bring additional layers of shock, guilt, and isolation.

What Helps and What Tends Not To

What Tends to Help

  • Reach out. A simple message such as "I am so sorry. I am thinking of you" matters.
  • Listen without fixing. Let them talk, cry, or sit in silence. You do not need to fill every pause.
  • Say the person's name. Many grieving people want to remember and talk about the person who died.
  • Offer specific help. Instead of "let me know if you need anything," try "I am bringing dinner on Thursday" or "Can I pick up your kids from school this week?"
  • Keep showing up. Check in weeks and months later, and especially around anniversaries, birthdays, and holidays, as described on the page about grief during holidays and anniversaries.
  • Accept their way of grieving. Some people want to talk, others prefer to stay busy or be alone.

What Tends Not to Help

  • Clichés, such as "everything happens for a reason" or "they are in a better place," unless you know these fit the person's beliefs.
  • Comparing losses or shifting the conversation to your own experience.
  • Rushing them, for example suggesting they should be over it by now.
  • Avoiding them because you do not know what to say. Silence can feel like abandonment.

Looking After Yourself Too

Supporting someone through grief can stir your own feelings, especially if you also knew the person who died or have had losses of your own. It is normal to feel sad, helpless, or drained. Share support with others where possible, set limits on what you can offer, and keep your own routines. If you are grieving the same loss, you may need support of your own as well.

Encouraging Professional Help

Most people move through grief with support from family, friends, and community. Professional help can be useful if grief stays intense and disabling for a long time, if the person cannot function in daily life, uses alcohol or other substances to cope, or feels hopeless. Signs of prolonged grief are described on the page about complicated and prolonged grief. You might gently say, "I have noticed how hard things still are. Would you consider talking to a grief counsellor?" and offer to help find one. If they talk about suicide or not wanting to live, take it seriously and call or text 9-8-8 for guidance.

Common Questions About Supporting Someone Who Is Grieving

How can I comfort a friend whose parent just died?

Simple words such as saying you are sorry and thinking of them are enough. Being present and listening matters more than perfect words when someone is grieving.

Is it okay to mention the name of the person who died?

Yes. Many grieving people want to remember and talk about the person who died, and hearing their name can be comforting.

What practical help can I offer a grieving friend?

Offer specific help, such as bringing meals, running errands, helping with childcare, or handling calls for a grieving friend.

Why can phrases like everything happens for a reason hurt someone in grief?

Clichés can feel dismissive of grief and may not fit the person's beliefs. Comparing losses or suggesting they should be over it can also hurt.

How long should I keep checking in on someone after a death?

Keep checking in for months after a death, especially around anniversaries and holidays, since support often fades just as grief deepens.

What if I do not know what to say to someone grieving?

It is okay to say you do not know what to say. Showing up and listening matters more than words when someone is grieving.

How do I support someone grieving a loss others do not recognize?

Acknowledge the loss directly, whether it is a pet, an ex-partner, or a pregnancy, and treat their grief as real.

How can I support someone grieving when I am grieving too?

Share support with others, set limits, and seek support for your own grief while helping someone else.

When should a grieving person see a counsellor?

If grief stays intense for a long time, makes daily life very hard, or brings hopelessness, a grief counsellor can help.

What should I do if a grieving person talks about not wanting to live?

Take it seriously, ask directly about suicide, and call or text 9-8-8 for guidance, or 911 if they are in immediate danger.
