What Is Somatic Symptom Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

New, severe, or changing physical symptoms, such as chest pain, sudden weakness, trouble breathing, or severe headache, should always be checked by a doctor. Call 911 in an emergency.

Somatic symptom disorder is a condition in which a person experiences one or more physical symptoms, such as pain, fatigue, or digestive problems, that are very distressing or disruptive, along with excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviours related to those symptoms. The symptoms are real and may or may not have a known medical cause. What defines the disorder is not whether a medical explanation exists, but how much worry, time, and energy the symptoms consume and how much they affect daily life. Somatic symptom disorder is treatable, and understanding it can reduce frustration for patients and families.

What Somatic Symptom Disorder Looks Like

  • Persistent physical symptoms, such as pain, fatigue, dizziness, or stomach problems.
  • Frequent, intense worry that symptoms signal a serious illness.
  • Spending a great deal of time researching, monitoring, or discussing symptoms.
  • Frequent medical visits and tests that bring only brief reassurance.
  • Feeling that doctors are missing something, often linked with physical symptoms doctors cannot explain.
  • Avoiding activities for fear of making symptoms worse.
  • Symptoms lasting six months or longer, even if individual symptoms change.

Why It Happens

Somatic symptom disorder develops from an interaction between body, mind, and environment. Stress and emotions strongly affect the body, influencing muscle tension, digestion, heart rate, and pain processing. Some people are naturally more aware of body sensations and more likely to interpret them as signs of danger. Once attention locks onto a symptom, it can feel stronger, and worry can amplify it further. This cycle is sometimes called somatosensory amplification.

Several factors increase risk. These include a history of childhood illness or trauma, anxiety or depression, a family member with serious illness, recent stressful life events, and a tendency to worry. Frequent medical visits and tests can sometimes unintentionally reinforce the focus on symptoms. The connection between emotional stress and physical pain is explored further on the page about the mind-body connection in chronic pain.

Living with somatic symptom disorder can be isolating. People may feel dismissed by health professionals or doubted by family, which adds frustration and distress. Some feel caught between wanting more tests and fearing what the results might show. Recognizing that both the symptoms and the worry are real is often the first step toward a more helpful approach.

What It Is Not

  • Not imagined or faked. The symptoms are real and genuinely distressing.
  • Not the same as illness anxiety disorder. In illness anxiety, physical symptoms are mild or absent and the focus is fear of having an illness, as described on the page about worrying about being seriously ill.
  • Not ruled out by having a medical condition. People with diagnosed illnesses can also have somatic symptom disorder if their response is excessive and disabling.
  • Not a reason to stop medical care. Regular care with one trusted doctor is usually recommended.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves a medical evaluation to check for physical causes and a psychological assessment of thoughts, feelings, and behaviours around symptoms. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most studied treatment. It helps people understand the body-mind cycle, reduce checking and reassurance-seeking, gradually return to avoided activities, and manage worry. Mindfulness and acceptance-based approaches can reduce struggle with symptoms. Treating co-occurring anxiety or depression helps. Regular, scheduled visits with a single primary care provider can reduce the urge for repeated tests while ensuring new concerns are checked.

Common Questions About Somatic Symptom Disorder

How long do symptoms need to last for this diagnosis?

Symptoms usually persist for six months or more, even if the specific symptom changes, and come with excessive thoughts, feelings, or behaviours that disrupt daily life.

Are the symptoms of somatic symptom disorder real?

Yes. The symptoms are genuinely experienced. The disorder is defined by how much they consume a person's thoughts, time, and energy.

How is somatic symptom disorder different from illness anxiety?

Somatic symptom disorder involves prominent physical symptoms, while illness anxiety centres on fear of having a disease with few or mild symptoms.

Can I have somatic symptom disorder and a medical condition?

Yes. The diagnosis can apply alongside a medical condition when the response to symptoms is excessive and disabling.

Why do medical tests only reassure me for a short time?

Reassurance eases worry briefly, but the underlying anxiety returns, often leading to more checking and more tests.

How does stress cause physical symptoms?

Stress affects muscles, digestion, heart rate, and pain processing, which can produce or intensify physical symptoms.

What is somatosensory amplification?

It describes the tendency to notice body sensations strongly and interpret them as threatening, which can make them feel more intense.

Can therapy help when symptoms and worry have taken over daily life?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the most studied option. Mindfulness, acceptance-based approaches, and coordinated medical care also help.

Should I keep seeing my doctor if I have somatic symptom disorder?

Yes. Regular visits with one trusted primary care provider help ensure new concerns are checked while reducing repeated tests.

How can family members support someone with somatic symptoms?

Acknowledge that symptoms are real, avoid excessive reassurance, encourage treatment, and support gradual return to activities.
