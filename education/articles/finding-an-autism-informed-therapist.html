Finding an Autism-Informed Therapist

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

An autism-informed therapist understands how autism affects communication, sensory experience, social energy, and daily life, and adapts therapy so it fits autistic ways of thinking rather than trying to make clients appear neurotypical. This matters because therapy that is not adapted can feel confusing, exhausting, or even harmful, for example by interpreting direct communication as rudeness or pushing eye contact and small talk. An autism-informed therapist offers clear communication, sensory accommodations, flexibility in structure, and respect for autistic identity. Finding the right fit can make therapy far more helpful and less draining.

Why Autism Experience Matters

Many autistic adults have had experiences of being misunderstood, including in health care. Some were misdiagnosed for years, or received therapy aimed at changing harmless autistic traits. An autism-informed therapist understands how autism shows up in adults, including those who mask, as described on the page about autism masking. They recognize common co-occurring experiences, such as anxiety, burnout, and depression, and adapt treatment accordingly.

Autism-informed therapy is closely linked with a neurodiversity-affirming stance, described on the page about neurodiversity-affirming therapy. Approaches that tend to work well when adapted are covered on the page about therapy approaches for autistic adults.

What to Look For

Specific Autism Experience

Look for therapists who describe training or experience with autistic adults, not just children, and who mention neurodiversity-affirming practice.

Adapted Communication

An informed therapist will use clear, direct language, check understanding, offer written summaries, and allow time to process.

Sensory and Format Flexibility

They may adjust lighting, allow stimming or movement, accept reduced eye contact, and offer virtual or text-supported sessions.

Respect for Identity

Therapy should focus on your goals, such as managing anxiety or burnout, not on making you seem less autistic.

Understanding of Co-Occurring Conditions

Many autistic adults also have ADHD, anxiety, depression, or trauma. A good therapist can address these with autism in mind.

Questions to Ask

  • What experience do you have working with autistic adults?
  • How do you adapt therapy for autistic clients?
  • How do you approach masking and stimming?
  • Can you offer written summaries or other supports?
  • Do you offer or refer for autism assessment?

It is reasonable to ask these questions by email before booking, which many autistic adults find easier.

Common Obstacles

Autism-informed therapists can be harder to find, and wait times and costs vary. Virtual therapy can widen options within BC. The process of searching, emailing, and booking can itself be draining, so breaking it into small steps, or asking a trusted person to help, may help. Past negative therapy experiences can make it hard to try again. A different therapist with autism experience may feel very different. Some adults are not yet sure whether they are autistic. Some therapists can help you explore this or refer for an assessment, as described on the page about how autism is diagnosed in adults.

Feeling understood is the most important factor. If a therapist does not feel like a fit after a few sessions, it is okay to try someone else. Self-advocacy skills, described on the page about self-advocacy for autistic adults, can help you ask for what you need in therapy too.

When More Support Is Needed

If you are experiencing autistic burnout, severe anxiety or depression, or thoughts of suicide, a broader care team may help, including a physician. Occupational therapists can help with sensory and daily living needs. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Finding an Autism-Informed Therapist

How is an autism-informed therapist different from a general therapist?

An autism-informed therapist understands how autism shapes communication and sensory needs and adapts sessions to fit autistic clients.

Why might therapy not work well for autistic adults if it is not adapted?

Unadapted therapy can misread autistic communication, push eye contact or small talk, or aim to change harmless autistic traits.

How do I screen a therapist for autism experience before my first session?

Ask by email about their experience with autistic adults, how they adapt therapy, their views on masking and stimming, and whether they offer written summaries.

Can I contact an autism-informed therapist by email first?

Yes. Many autistic adults find it easier to ask questions by email before booking, and that is reasonable.

Will an autism-informed therapist let me stim during sessions?

Yes. An autism-informed therapist will respect stimming and other regulation needs during sessions.

I only suspect I am autistic. Can I still see an autism-informed therapist?

Yes. Many autism-informed therapists work with people exploring whether they are autistic and can help with assessment referrals.

Could video sessions reduce the sensory load of therapy for an autistic adult?

Often yes. Video sessions let autistic adults control lighting, noise, and setting at home, and they also widen the choice of therapists.

What if a past therapist did not understand my autism?

That experience can make therapy feel risky, but an autism-informed therapist may feel very different and more supportive.

How do I know if an autism-informed therapist is the right fit?

Feeling understood and respected matters most. If an autism-informed therapist does not feel right after a few sessions, try someone else.

Can an autism-informed therapist help with ADHD and anxiety too?

Yes. Many autism-informed therapists address co-occurring ADHD, anxiety, depression, and trauma with autism in mind.
