Why Do I Struggle to Get Rid of Things I Don't Need

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You pick up an old magazine, a broken lamp, or a bag of cables, fully intending to toss it. Then the thoughts start. You might need it someday. It was a gift. Throwing it away feels wasteful. Maybe it is worth something. Before long, it goes back on the pile. Over time, piles turn into rooms that are hard to use, and the idea of tackling them feels overwhelming, even embarrassing.

Many people hold onto more than they need. Understanding why can help you decide whether it is a habit worth adjusting or a pattern that could use professional support.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Sentimental attachment. Objects often carry memories of people, places, and times in our lives. After a death or major change, letting go of belongings can feel like letting go of the person, which is why decluttering is often hardest during grief.

Frugality and scarcity. People who grew up with financial hardship, or whose parents lived through it, may have learned that discarding usable things is wasteful or risky.

Environmental values. Some people keep things because they genuinely do not want to add to landfill, and struggle to find ways to donate or recycle.

ADHD. Clutter often builds for people with ADHD because sorting and decision-making are hard to start and finish, not necessarily because they are attached to the objects. That pattern is explored in Why Is My Room Always a Mess No Matter What I Try.

Depression. Low energy and motivation can make clearing out feel impossible, so things accumulate.

OCD. Some people with OCD keep items because of fears that something bad will happen if they throw them away, or because discarding feels "not right," much like the rituals described in Why Do I Feel Like I Have to Do Things a Certain Number of Times.

When It Looks More Like Hoarding Disorder

Hoarding disorder is a recognized mental health condition, distinct from collecting or simple clutter. Its core feature is persistent difficulty discarding possessions, regardless of their value, because of a strong perceived need to save them and distress about letting them go.

Key signs include:

  • Intense distress, anxiety, or even grief when trying to discard items, including things with little practical or monetary value.
  • Possessions accumulating to the point that rooms cannot be used for their intended purpose, such as a kitchen you cannot cook in or a bed covered with items.
  • Difficulty making decisions about what to keep, often with every item feeling important.
  • Frequently acquiring more, through shopping, free items, or collecting things others throw out.
  • Feeling ashamed or avoiding having people over because of the state of your home.

Hoarding difficulties often begin in adolescence or early adulthood and gradually worsen over time, often becoming most visible in middle age or later. Many people with hoarding disorder also experience depression, anxiety, or ADHD.

Hoarding can bring real safety concerns, including fire risk, falls, blocked exits, and pest or sanitation problems. If your home has become unsafe, please reach out for help, as there are practical and compassionate options available.

What Can Help

  • Start very small. One drawer, one shelf, or ten minutes at a time is more sustainable than trying to clear a whole room.
  • Set simple decision rules. For example, if you have not used an item in a year and could replace it easily, it goes.
  • Keep the memory, not the object. Photographing sentimental items before letting them go can ease the sense of loss.
  • Slow down acquiring. Pausing before bringing new items home is often as important as discarding.
  • Get support rather than a surprise clean-out. Having others clear your space without your involvement tends to increase distress and rarely lasts.

Common Questions About Difficulty Getting Rid of Things

What is the difference between clutter and hoarding disorder?

Clutter is common and usually does not stop rooms from being used. Hoarding disorder involves persistent difficulty discarding possessions because of strong distress, to the point that living spaces cannot be used as intended and daily life is affected.

Why does throwing things away make me so anxious?

For some people, discarding items triggers fear of losing something important, making a wrong decision, being wasteful, or losing a connection to memories. That distress can feel intense, making it easier to keep things than to face the discomfort.

Can ADHD cause clutter that looks like hoarding?

Yes. People with ADHD often accumulate clutter because sorting, organizing, and making decisions are hard to start and finish. Unlike hoarding disorder, there may not be strong distress about discarding once they get to it.

Is collecting things the same as hoarding?

No. Collecting is usually organized, focused on specific items, and a source of pride or enjoyment. Hoarding tends to involve a wide range of items, disorganized accumulation, and distress, and it often prevents normal use of living spaces.

Can grief make it hard to let go of a loved one's belongings?

Very much so. Belongings can feel like a connection to the person, and letting them go may feel like losing them again. Many people go through a loved one's things gradually, keeping a few meaningful items and photographing others.

Is hoarding a form of OCD?

Hoarding disorder is now recognized as its own condition, separate from OCD, though they are related. Some people with OCD keep items because of specific feared consequences, which is a different pattern from the attachment and decision difficulties typical of hoarding disorder.

Why doesn't a big clean-out by family fix hoarding?

Forced or surprise clean-outs usually cause intense distress and do not change the underlying difficulty with discarding or acquiring. Clutter often returns. Gradual, collaborative approaches that involve the person tend to work much better.

When does hoarding become a safety concern?

Hoarding becomes a safety concern when it blocks exits, creates fire risks near heat sources, causes tripping hazards, attracts pests, or makes cooking, bathing, or sleeping difficult. At that point, getting help is important.

Does cognitive behavioural therapy help with hoarding?

Yes. CBT adapted for hoarding is the most researched treatment. It works on decision-making, beliefs about possessions, tolerating the discomfort of discarding, reducing new acquiring, and practical sorting skills.

How can I support a family member who struggles to get rid of things?

Approach with compassion rather than criticism, focus on safety first, and let them make decisions about their belongings. Encouraging professional support and offering practical help at their pace tends to be far more effective than pressure.
