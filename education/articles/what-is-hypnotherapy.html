What Is Hypnotherapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Hypnotherapy is the use of hypnosis within therapy. Hypnosis is a natural state of focused attention and deep relaxation, similar to being absorbed in a book or a daydream, in which people can become more open to helpful suggestions and to exploring thoughts, feelings, and memories. In clinical hypnotherapy, a trained therapist guides this state to support goals such as reducing anxiety, managing pain, changing habits, or working with inner conflict. People stay aware and in control throughout. Hypnosis cannot make anyone do something against their will. Hypnotherapy is usually used as an add-on to other therapy approaches rather than on its own.

How Hypnotherapy Works

During hypnosis, attention narrows and the usual critical, analytical voice tends to quiet. Many people feel calm, heavy, or pleasantly detached, while still hearing everything and being able to respond. Brain research suggests hypnosis changes activity in networks related to attention, body awareness, and self-monitoring, though scientists are still learning how it works.

In this state, a hypnotherapist may use several techniques:

  • Suggestion. Offering helpful ideas, such as feeling calmer in a feared situation or noticing pain as less intense.
  • Imagery and rehearsal. Imagining coping well with a challenge, which can build confidence.
  • Relaxation training. Teaching the body to settle, which can reduce stress and anxiety.
  • Parts work. Exploring different parts of the self, such as a part that wants to change a habit and a part that resists. Parts work is a long-standing technique in clinical hypnotherapy, including approaches such as parts therapy and ego state therapy, which grew out of hypnosis. Many hypnotherapists also integrate ideas from Internal Family Systems (IFS) therapy, which was developed separately and is usually practised without trance.
  • Self-hypnosis. Learning to enter a calm, focused state independently, so benefits continue between sessions.

What Sessions Are Like

A first session usually involves talking about your goals, history, and any concerns about hypnosis. The therapist explains what to expect and answers questions, since understanding the process helps people feel comfortable. The hypnosis portion usually begins with an induction, such as slow breathing, relaxing muscles, or focusing on the therapist's voice. The therapist then guides you through suggestions, imagery, or parts work related to your goals. Afterward, you return to ordinary awareness and talk about what you noticed.

You remain aware of your surroundings and can open your eyes, speak, or stop at any time. Most people remember the session. Some people enter hypnosis easily, while others find it takes practice. Hypnotherapy is often short-term, sometimes a handful of sessions for a specific goal, and it is commonly combined with other approaches such as cognitive behavioural or trauma-informed therapy. Recordings for self-hypnosis practice may be offered.

Who It Can Help

Research suggests hypnotherapy can be a helpful add-on for some concerns, including irritable bowel syndrome, chronic pain, and some anxiety presentations. Its use for pain is linked with the ideas described on the page about the mind-body connection in chronic pain. It is also used for procedure-related anxiety, sleep difficulties, habit change, and preparing for stressful events, though evidence for some of these uses is more limited. Research on parts work within hypnotherapy is limited, and research on IFS itself is promising but still early. For anxiety, hypnotherapy is usually combined with other evidence-based approaches, such as those described on the page about anxiety disorders.

Hypnotherapy is not suitable for everyone. It is generally not recommended as a main treatment for psychosis, and people with some dissociative conditions or severe trauma may need stabilization first. It should not be used to recover memories, since hypnosis can make memories feel vivid without making them accurate. A trauma-informed hypnotherapist will pace the work carefully and keep safety central.

Common Questions About Hypnotherapy

What does hypnosis feel like during hypnotherapy?

Most people feel calm, focused, and relaxed during hypnosis, similar to being absorbed in a book, while still hearing everything.

Can a hypnotherapist make me do something against my will?

No. During hypnotherapy you stay aware and in control, and hypnosis cannot make anyone act against their will.

Will I remember what happens in a hypnotherapy session?

Most people remember their hypnotherapy sessions and can speak, open their eyes, or stop at any time.

What conditions does hypnotherapy have good evidence for?

Hypnotherapy has reasonable evidence as an add-on for irritable bowel syndrome, chronic pain, and some anxiety presentations.

How is IFS used within hypnotherapy?

Many hypnotherapists integrate IFS-style parts work, since parts work has a long history in hypnosis, though IFS itself is usually practised without trance.

Did Internal Family Systems come from hypnosis?

No. IFS was developed by Richard Schwartz from family systems therapy. Hypnotherapy has its own separate tradition of parts work.

Can hypnotherapy help recover lost memories?

Hypnotherapy should not be used to recover memories, since hypnosis can make memories feel vivid without making them accurate.

What is self-hypnosis and how is it taught?

Self-hypnosis is entering a calm, focused state on your own, often taught by a hypnotherapist and practised with recordings.

Is hypnotherapy safe for everyone?

Hypnotherapy is not recommended as a main treatment for psychosis, and people with some dissociative conditions may need stabilization first.

How many hypnotherapy sessions do people usually need?

Hypnotherapy is often short-term, sometimes a handful of sessions for a specific goal, and is usually combined with other therapy.
