Understanding Anxiety in Children and Teens

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If a child or teen talks about wanting to die or harming themselves, or you are worried about their immediate safety, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. Call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Young people can reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Anxiety is a normal part of childhood and adolescence. Young children may fear the dark, monsters, or being separated from parents. Older children may worry about school, friendships, or performance. Teens may feel anxious about how they are seen, their future, or world events. These fears usually come and go. Anxiety becomes a concern when it is intense, persistent, and interferes with school, friendships, family life, or activities. Anxiety disorders are the most common mental health conditions in children and teens, and they respond well to treatment, especially when caught early.

What Anxiety Can Look Like in Children and Teens

  • Frequent worries or asking many what-if questions.
  • Stomach aches, headaches, or nausea, especially before school.
  • Clinginess or difficulty separating from parents.
  • Avoiding school, activities, or social situations.
  • Trouble falling asleep or frequent nightmares.
  • Irritability, tantrums, or meltdowns when facing feared situations.
  • Needing a lot of reassurance.
  • Perfectionism or fear of making mistakes.
  • In teens, withdrawal, panic attacks, or using screens or substances to escape worry.

Children often show anxiety through behaviour and physical complaints rather than words. What looks like defiance may be fear. The page on signs your child may need mental health support offers more guidance.

Why It Happens

Anxiety in young people develops from a mix of temperament, genetics, and experiences. Some children are born more cautious and sensitive. Anxiety often runs in families, through both genes and learned patterns. Stressful events such as moves, family conflict, illness, bullying, academic pressure, or loss can trigger anxiety. Social media, online comparison, and exposure to worrying news can increase anxiety in teens.

Parents' responses can unintentionally shape anxiety. When parents protect children from every feared situation, or provide constant reassurance, anxiety may be relieved in the moment but reinforced over time. This is called accommodation and is a very natural response to seeing a child distressed. Parents who are anxious themselves, as described on the page about worrying about your child all the time, may find this especially hard to avoid. Adolescence brings its own pressures, explored on the page about parenting a teenager.

Anxiety in children often shows up at predictable times, such as Sunday evenings before school, before tests, or at bedtime. Noticing these patterns can help parents plan support and recognize when anxiety is increasing.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign of bad parenting. Anxiety is common in loving families.
  • Not something children always outgrow. Untreated anxiety can persist or worsen.
  • Not attention-seeking. Physical complaints and avoidance often reflect real fear.
  • Not solved by avoiding every fear. Gradually facing fears with support builds confidence.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor, paediatrician, or school counsellor can be a first point of contact, and a psychologist can assess anxiety and related conditions. Cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for children and teens is the most effective treatment. It teaches young people to understand anxiety, challenge worried thoughts, and gradually face fears. Parent involvement is important. Programs that teach parents to reduce accommodation and support brave behaviour can be as effective as working directly with the child. Relaxation skills, regular sleep, physical activity, and limiting screen time before bed help. For moderate to severe anxiety, medication may be considered. In British Columbia, Child and Youth Mental Health services also offer support. More information on types of anxiety is on the page about anxiety disorders.

Common Questions About Anxiety in Children and Teens

How can I tell if my child's worries are normal or anxiety?

Consider whether worries are intense, persistent, and interfere with school, friendships, or family life for several weeks.

Why does my child get stomach aches before school?

Anxiety often shows up as physical symptoms in children, especially before feared situations like school.

What is accommodation in childhood anxiety?

Accommodation is when parents change routines or provide reassurance to reduce a child's anxiety, which can reinforce it over time.

Should I let my anxious child avoid things that scare them?

Constant avoidance usually strengthens anxiety. Gradual, supported steps toward fears build confidence.

How does anxiety look different in teens?

Teens may withdraw, become irritable, have panic attacks, or use screens or substances to escape worry.

Can social media increase anxiety in teens?

It can, through comparison, online pressure, and exposure to distressing content, though effects vary.

How do psychologists usually treat an anxious child?

Most use cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for young people, often with parent involvement, which has the strongest evidence.

Can parent programs help with a child's anxiety?

Yes. Programs that help parents reduce accommodation and encourage brave behaviour can be very effective.

How can I support my anxious child at bedtime?

Keep a calm routine, acknowledge worries briefly, avoid lengthy reassurance, and praise small steps of independence.

Where can families find help for childhood anxiety in British Columbia?

Family doctors, school counsellors, psychologists, and Child and Youth Mental Health teams offer support.
