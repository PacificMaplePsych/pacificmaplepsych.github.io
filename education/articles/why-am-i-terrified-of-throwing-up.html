Why Am I Terrified of Throwing Up

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Nobody enjoys being sick. But for you, the fear goes much further. You might avoid restaurants, travel, crowds, or anyone who seems unwell. You check expiry dates repeatedly, wash your hands constantly during flu season, or avoid alcohol entirely. The slightest wave of nausea sends you into panic. Some people with this fear have not actually vomited in years, yet the dread shapes their entire life.

An intense fear of vomiting is called emetophobia. It is more common than many people realize, often starts in childhood, and is frequently kept secret because people feel embarrassed by it. It is also very treatable.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Digestive conditions. Reflux, irritable bowel syndrome, or other gut problems can cause frequent nausea, which understandably makes people more anxious about vomiting. A medical check can help.

A frightening past experience. A bad bout of illness, food poisoning, or seeing someone else be very sick can create a lasting fear, much like the way other specific fears form after a single event.

Anxiety and panic. Anxiety commonly causes nausea, and people who feel sick to their stomach before big events may start to fear the nausea itself.

Health anxiety. Constant worry about illness can focus on stomach bugs and vomiting, often with frequent checking and searching symptoms online.

Eating concerns. Restricting foods, eating very small amounts, or avoiding meals with others because of fear of being sick can overlap with avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder. This connects to avoiding eating in front of other people.

When It Looks More Like a Specific Phobia

Emetophobia is a type of specific phobia, an intense, persistent fear focused on a particular thing, as described in Why Do I Feel Terrified of a Specific Thing Everyone Else Seems Fine With. What sets it apart from ordinary dislike is how much time and energy the fear takes up and how much it restricts daily life.

Common signs include:

  • Constant monitoring of your stomach for any hint of nausea.
  • Avoiding foods, restaurants, travel, parties, pregnancy, or people who might be ill.
  • Strict food safety rituals, such as checking dates repeatedly or overcooking food.
  • Carrying items for safety, such as mints, water, or anti-nausea medication.
  • Panic in response to mild nausea, which can make the nausea stronger.

That last point creates a trap. Anxiety causes nausea, the nausea triggers more fear, and the fear increases nausea further. Avoidance brings short-term relief but keeps the fear strong, as with many patterns described in Why Do I Avoid Situations That Make Me Nervous.

What Can Help

  • Understand the anxiety-nausea loop. Knowing that anxiety itself causes nausea can make the sensation less frightening.
  • Notice safety behaviours. Track the things you do to avoid vomiting. These are what therapy gradually works on.
  • Calm the body. Slow breathing can ease anxiety-related nausea.
  • Avoid adding restrictions. Cutting out more foods or places tends to strengthen the fear over time.
  • Seek specialized help. Emetophobia responds well to cognitive behavioural therapy with gradual exposure, even when it has lasted many years.

Common Questions About Fear of Vomiting

How is emetophobia different from simply hating being sick?

Most people dislike vomiting, but emetophobia is an intense, persistent fear of vomiting, seeing others vomit, or feeling nauseous that takes up a lot of mental energy and leads to significant avoidance of foods, places, travel, and social situations. It is a recognized type of specific phobia.

How common is fear of vomiting?

Emetophobia is more common than many people think and is often hidden due to embarrassment. It frequently begins in childhood or adolescence and can continue for many years without treatment.

Why does my nausea get worse when I'm scared of being sick?

Anxiety itself causes nausea by changing how the gut works. When you fear nausea, anxiety rises, which increases nausea, which increases fear. Breaking this loop is a key part of treatment.

Can emetophobia affect what I eat?

Yes. Many people with emetophobia avoid certain foods, eat very small portions, or avoid eating out. In some cases this overlaps with ARFID, an eating disorder driven by fear rather than body image.

Can fear of vomiting affect decisions about pregnancy?

For some people, yes. Fear of morning sickness can lead people to delay or avoid pregnancy. Treating the phobia can help people make these decisions based on their wishes rather than fear.

Is emetophobia related to OCD?

They can overlap. Some people with emetophobia develop checking and washing rituals to avoid illness. A clinician can help determine whether OCD, a specific phobia, or both are involved.

How is emetophobia treated?

Cognitive behavioural therapy with gradual exposure is the most effective approach. It involves slowly approaching feared situations and sensations in a planned, supported way until the fear decreases.

Will exposure therapy make me throw up?

No. Exposure therapy for emetophobia does not involve making you vomit. It focuses on gradually facing related thoughts, images, words, sensations, and situations at a pace you agree to.

Can children have a fear of vomiting?

Yes. Emetophobia often begins in childhood. Parents can help by responding calmly to illness and avoiding excessive accommodation, and a psychologist can provide guidance and treatment.

Can I overcome emetophobia after many years?

Yes. Even long-standing emetophobia can improve significantly with treatment. Many people are able to eat, travel, and live more freely after working through the fear gradually.
