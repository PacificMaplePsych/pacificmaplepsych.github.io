How Therapy Helps With Perfectionism

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Therapy helps with perfectionism by helping people understand where their high standards and fear of mistakes come from, loosen rigid rules, test what actually happens when things are good enough, and replace harsh self-criticism with a kinder, steadier inner voice. Approaches such as cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism, compassion-focused therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, and schema therapy all have something to offer. Perfectionism is not the same as caring about quality. It becomes a problem when standards are so high and rigid that they cause anxiety, procrastination, burnout, or a constant sense of not being enough. With support, people can keep their strengths while letting go of what weighs them down.

The Main Treatment Options

CBT for perfectionism. CBT adapted for perfectionism has good research support. It helps people identify perfectionistic beliefs, such as "if it is not perfect, it is a failure," and the behaviours that maintain them, such as over-checking, procrastinating, or avoiding tasks. Behavioural experiments, such as deliberately making a small mistake or submitting work that is good enough, test whether feared consequences actually happen.

Compassion-focused therapy. Many perfectionists are driven by a harsh inner critic. Compassion-focused therapy helps build a supportive inner voice, which research suggests can reduce the distress linked with perfectionism.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people notice perfectionistic thoughts without obeying them, and act on values even when discomfort shows up.

Schema therapy. For long-standing perfectionism rooted in early experiences, such as conditional approval or high pressure growing up, schema therapy explores deeper patterns such as unrelenting standards.

Treating related concerns. Perfectionism often occurs alongside anxiety, depression, OCD, eating disorders, and burnout, which may need attention too.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A therapist will explore how perfectionism shows up in your life, such as at work, in appearance, in relationships, or at home, and what it costs you. The patterns are described on the page about perfectionism. They will look at related concerns, since perfectionism and anxiety often reinforce each other, as explained on the page about anxiety and perfectionism. When perfectionism has led to exhaustion, the links described on the page about burnout and perfectionism may shape the plan. Some people prefer structured, practical work, while others want to explore the roots of their perfectionism more deeply. Many therapists blend approaches.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress often begins with noticing perfectionistic rules and how much time and energy they take. Over time, people may find they can finish tasks without excessive checking, procrastinate less, tolerate small mistakes, and feel less crushed by criticism. Many notice more time for rest and relationships. Interestingly, many people also find their work quality stays high or improves, since less energy goes into anxiety. Change is gradual, often over several months, and old habits may return under stress. Each return is a chance to practise again.

Therapy also pays attention to the hidden benefits perfectionism seems to offer, such as feeling in control or avoiding criticism. Understanding these makes it easier to find other ways of meeting the same needs, so letting go feels less risky.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Between sessions, try small experiments, such as sending an email without rereading it three times or leaving a minor task imperfect, and notice what happens. Set time limits for tasks. Define good enough before starting. Notice self-critical thoughts and respond as you would to a friend. Celebrate effort and progress, not just results. Protect rest, since tiredness strengthens perfectionistic thinking.

Common Questions About Therapy for Perfectionism

What changes when perfectionism is treated in therapy?

Therapy for perfectionism helps people loosen rigid standards, reduce fear of mistakes, procrastinate less, and replace harsh self-criticism with a steadier inner voice.

Which therapy works best for perfectionism?

CBT for perfectionism has good research support, and compassion-focused therapy, ACT, and schema therapy can also help.

Why would a therapist treating my perfectionism ask me to send an email with a typo on purpose?

Small planned mistakes are a CBT experiment for perfectionism. Noticing that feared consequences rarely happen helps loosen rigid standards.

Will letting go of perfectionism make my work worse?

Many people find their work quality stays high or improves when perfectionism eases, since less energy goes into anxiety.

Why does perfectionism make me procrastinate?

Fear of not meeting perfect standards can make starting feel risky, so perfectionism often leads to delay and avoidance.

How does compassion-focused therapy help perfectionists?

Compassion-focused therapy helps perfectionists build a supportive inner voice, which can reduce distress driven by a harsh inner critic.

Why might my perfectionism trace back to how approval worked when I was a child?

When approval felt conditional on achievement, perfectionism can develop as a way to stay safe and accepted. Temperament and high pressure also play a part.

Will perfectionism go away after a few therapy sessions?

Usually not that quickly. Change in perfectionism is gradual, often over several months, and old habits may return under stress.

How does therapy for perfectionism protect against burnout?

By loosening impossible standards and reducing over-checking, therapy for perfectionism frees energy that would otherwise drain toward burnout.

What can I practise between therapy sessions for perfectionism?

Try small experiments, set time limits, define good enough before starting, and respond to self-criticism as you would to a friend.
