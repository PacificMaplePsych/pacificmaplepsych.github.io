Why Do I Feel Like I'm Always Running Late

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You set the alarm earlier. You lay out your things the night before. You still end up rushing out the door, apologizing again, wondering how this keeps happening when you genuinely do not want to be the person who is always late. If chronic lateness has followed you despite real, repeated effort to fix it, it is worth understanding what might actually be driving it, rather than continuing to chalk it up to being disorganized or not caring enough.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Overcommitment. Simply scheduling too much into too little time, back to back commitments with no buffer, produces lateness for almost anyone, independent of any individual difference. This tends to be more solvable through calendar changes than through personal strategies.

Anxiety about arriving too early. For some people, a mild anxiety about being the first one somewhere, or having to wait around, leads to unconsciously timing departures closer to the edge than necessary.

Genuine underestimation of logistics. Not accounting realistically for traffic, transit delays, or how long getting ready actually takes is a common, ordinary planning gap that does not require any deeper explanation.

Depression. Low energy and motivation can make the whole process of getting ready and out the door take longer than it used to, and can also reduce the sense of urgency that normally helps someone leave on time.

Perfectionism about finishing tasks. Some people delay leaving because they want to complete "just one more thing" before departing, a pattern that can apply to anyone but is worth naming on its own.

When It Looks More Like ADHD

Chronic lateness in ADHD is closely connected to what is sometimes called time blindness, a genuine difficulty sensing how much time has passed or how long something will take, rather than a lack of caring or effort. This affects both the planning stage, consistently underestimating how long getting ready or travelling will take, and the in-the-moment stage, losing track of time during a seemingly quick task right before needing to leave, sometimes called the "last five minutes" trap, where one final small task turns into fifteen minutes without the person noticing.

What distinguishes this from ordinary planning gaps is that it tends to persist despite real effort and despite the person genuinely valuing punctuality. Many people with ADHD-related time blindness have tried numerous strategies, earlier alarms, laying things out in advance, giving themselves extra buffer, and still find themselves running late, because the underlying issue is not a lack of strategy but an unreliable internal sense of time that keeps undermining even good strategies.

Another common pattern is that lateness is often worse for low-stakes or self-imposed deadlines than for ones with real external consequences, since urgency itself tends to sharpen time awareness temporarily. Someone who is chronically late to casual plans but can make an important flight is not being selectively inconsiderate. They are experiencing how urgency compensates, unreliably, for an internal sense of time that does not track well without it.

What Can Help

Building in a buffer significantly larger than feels necessary, setting a firm "leave by" alarm separate from a "start getting ready" alarm, and avoiding starting any new task in the final stretch before needing to leave, even a quick one, can all help interrupt the last five minutes trap. Visual, ambient time cues, like a clock in every room used while getting ready, also help translate an unreliable internal sense of time into something concrete and visible.

Common Questions About Chronic Lateness

Is chronic lateness always about not caring enough?

No, and this is one of the most common misconceptions about it. For many people, especially those with ADHD-related time blindness, chronic lateness persists despite genuinely caring a great deal about being on time and trying multiple strategies to fix it.

What is the “last five minutes trap”?

It refers to starting one more small task right before needing to leave, which then unexpectedly stretches out and causes lateness, even when someone was otherwise ready ahead of schedule. It is a common and specific pattern in ADHD-related time blindness.

Why can I make an important flight but be late to casual plans?

This reflects how urgency temporarily sharpens time awareness. High-stakes situations provide enough urgency to compensate for an unreliable internal sense of time, while lower-stakes plans do not generate the same compensatory focus.

Could this be about overcommitting rather than an attention difference?

Yes, this is a genuine and common cause on its own. Scheduling too much into too little time with no buffer produces lateness regardless of any underlying attention pattern, and is often solvable through calendar adjustments alone.

Does depression affect punctuality too?

It can. Reduced energy and motivation can slow down the whole process of getting ready, and a general dulling of urgency can make it harder to leave on time, separate from any attention-related cause.

Why do earlier alarms and extra buffer time sometimes not fix the problem?

For ADHD-related time blindness specifically, the underlying issue is an unreliable internal sense of time, not a lack of strategy. Good strategies can still get undermined in the moment if time awareness itself is not tracking accurately.

Is time blindness a real, recognized concept?

Yes. It describes a genuine, well recognized difficulty accurately sensing the passage of time, connected to executive function differences seen in ADHD, rather than being an informal excuse.

Can visual time cues actually make a meaningful difference?

Yes, for many people. Visible clocks and separate leave-by alarms translate an unreliable internal sense of time into something external and concrete, which tends to be more effective than relying on internal time awareness alone.

Is it possible to be great at time management in some areas and terrible in others?

Yes, this is common. Time blindness can be inconsistent depending on urgency, interest, and external structure, which is why someone can manage certain deadlines well while struggling significantly with everyday punctuality.

What would an assessment look at regarding chronic lateness?

An assessment would consider how long the pattern has been present, whether it has persisted despite genuine effort and multiple strategies, whether it is worse for low-stakes situations, and whether other explanations like overcommitment or depression better account for it.
