Therapy Approaches for Substance Use

Stopping some substances suddenly, such as alcohol or certain medications, can cause dangerous withdrawal. Talk to a doctor before cutting down, or call 8-1-1 in British Columbia for advice. If someone is unresponsive, has trouble breathing, or you suspect an overdose, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Therapy approaches for substance use include motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioural therapy, relapse prevention, contingency management, acceptance and commitment therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy skills, and family or couples approaches. They help people explore their reasons for change, understand triggers, manage cravings, build coping skills, and create a life that supports their goals, whether that is cutting back or stopping. Therapy is often combined with medical care, medication prescribed by a physician, peer support, and harm reduction. People with substance use concerns deserve the same respect and care as anyone else, and change is possible at any stage.

The Main Treatment Options

Motivational interviewing. This collaborative approach helps people explore mixed feelings about change without pressure or judgment. It is especially helpful early on, when someone is unsure whether or how to change.

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps people identify thoughts, feelings, and situations linked with substance use, develop coping strategies, and practise new responses.

Relapse prevention. Based on CBT, this focuses on high-risk situations, early warning signs, and plans for handling lapses, as described on the page about relapse prevention planning.

Contingency management. This approach provides tangible rewards for meeting goals, such as negative drug tests, and has strong evidence, particularly for stimulant use.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people notice cravings without acting on them and move toward their values.

DBT skills. Emotion regulation and distress tolerance skills, described on the page about DBT skills for emotional regulation, help people who use substances to cope with intense feelings.

Family and couples approaches. Involving loved ones can improve communication, reduce conflict, and support change.

Medication and medical care. For some substances, such as alcohol and opioids, medications prescribed by a physician can reduce cravings, support withdrawal, or lower the risk of relapse and overdose.

Peer support. Mutual support groups offer connection and shared experience.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan starts with understanding the substance or substances involved, patterns of use, physical dependence, withdrawal risks, and how use affects health, work, and relationships. Goals matter. Some people want to stop entirely, while others want to reduce use or use more safely, and therapy can support any of these. Co-occurring conditions, such as depression, anxiety, ADHD, or trauma, are common and are often treated at the same time, since substances are frequently used to cope with them. Practical factors such as access, cost, and support at home also shape choices. More intensive options, such as day programs or residential treatment, may be recommended for severe or complex situations. Alcohol-specific strategies are covered on the page about cutting back on drinking.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress is often gradual and uneven. Early signs can include more awareness of triggers, fewer days of use, reduced amounts, better sleep, or feeling more hopeful. Lapses are common and do not erase progress. They can offer useful information about triggers and needs. Over months, many people build new routines, relationships, and coping skills that make change more stable. Recovery is often described as a process rather than a single event.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Tracking use and cravings helps you notice patterns. Planning for high-risk situations, such as social events or stressful days, reduces the chance of being caught off guard. Building activities and connections that do not involve substances fills the space that use once occupied. Looking after sleep, meals, and movement supports mood and resilience. Being honest with your therapist and doctor, including about lapses, helps them support you. Treating yourself with compassion, rather than shame, makes it easier to keep going.

Common Questions About Therapy for Substance Use

Which therapies work best for substance use?

Motivational interviewing, CBT, relapse prevention, contingency management, and ACT all have evidence for substance use, often combined with medical care.

What is motivational interviewing for substance use?

Motivational interviewing helps people explore mixed feelings about changing their substance use without pressure or judgment.

Do I have to quit completely to get therapy for substance use?

No. Therapy for substance use can support goals of stopping, cutting back, or using more safely.

What is contingency management in addiction treatment?

Contingency management provides rewards for meeting goals, such as negative drug tests, and has strong evidence for stimulant use.

Can medication help alongside therapy for substance use?

For some substances, such as alcohol and opioids, medications prescribed by a physician can reduce cravings or relapse risk.

Why does therapy for substance use often address anxiety or trauma too?

Substances are often used to cope with anxiety, depression, or trauma, so treating these together supports lasting change.

Does a lapse mean my substance use treatment has failed?

No. Lapses are common in substance use recovery and can offer useful information about triggers and needs.

How can family members be involved in substance use treatment?

Family and couples approaches can improve communication, reduce conflict, and support someone changing their substance use.

Do I need a doctor involved before I stop using a substance I take every day?

Often yes. Stopping some substances suddenly, such as daily alcohol, can cause dangerous withdrawal, so talk to a doctor before cutting down.

When is more intensive substance use treatment recommended?

Day programs or residential treatment may be recommended for severe substance use, complex health needs, or when outpatient therapy is not enough.
