What Is Structured Literacy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Structured literacy is an approach to teaching reading, spelling, and writing that is explicit, systematic, and cumulative. It directly teaches the building blocks of written language, including the sounds in words, how letters represent those sounds, syllable types, word parts such as prefixes and suffixes, sentence structure, and meaning. Skills are taught in a logical order, practised until they become automatic, and reviewed often, with teachers checking progress and adjusting instruction. Structured literacy is grounded in research on how the brain learns to read, often called the science of reading. It is the recommended approach for students with dyslexia and benefits most beginning readers.

The Key Elements

What Is Taught

  • Phonology. Hearing and working with sounds in spoken words.
  • Sound-symbol connections. Linking sounds to letters and letter patterns, often called phonics.
  • Syllables. Recognizing syllable types to decode longer words.
  • Morphology. Understanding prefixes, suffixes, and roots.
  • Syntax. How words combine into sentences.
  • Semantics. Understanding meaning and vocabulary.

How It Is Taught

  • Explicit. Concepts are taught directly rather than left to be discovered.
  • Systematic and cumulative. Skills follow a clear sequence, building on what was learned before.
  • Diagnostic. Teachers monitor progress closely and adjust instruction.
  • Multi-sensory. Many programs combine seeing, hearing, saying, and writing.

Why It Works

Reading is not natural in the way spoken language is. The brain has to build connections between sounds and written symbols. Children with dyslexia have difficulty forming these connections, as described on the page about what dyslexia is, and they benefit from instruction that makes every step clear. Approaches that rely mainly on guessing words from pictures or context can leave gaps that become more obvious as reading demands increase. Reviews such as Ontario's Right to Read inquiry have highlighted the importance of explicit, evidence-based reading instruction.

Common Myths About Reading Instruction

Several long-standing beliefs about reading can delay effective help. One is that children will learn to read naturally if they are surrounded by books. Many children do, but a significant number need explicit teaching of how sounds and letters connect. Another is that guessing words from pictures or the first letter is a useful strategy. For struggling readers, this habit can hide decoding difficulties and break down as texts become more complex. A third is that a child will "catch up" with time. While some do, children with persistent difficulties usually need targeted instruction, and earlier support tends to work better. Understanding these myths can help families ask informed questions about the reading support their child receives.

What to Look For in Reading Support

  • A clear, structured scope and sequence of skills
  • Direct teaching of phonics and word structure
  • Regular progress monitoring using standardized measures, ideally at the start, middle, and end of an intervention
  • Enough intensity, such as several sessions per week
  • Instructors trained in structured literacy approaches
  • Attention to comprehension and vocabulary, not only decoding

Intervention often needs to continue for months or longer. Progress is usually gradual, so tracking results helps families and schools see whether an approach is working.

Where Assessment Fits

A psychoeducational assessment identifies whether a child has a reading disorder, which skills need the most support, and other factors such as attention or language, as described on the page about signs your child may have a learning disability. The recommendations can guide both school planning and outside reading support, as described on the page about school supports in BC after a psychoeducational assessment.

What It Is Not

Structured literacy is not a single brand or program. Many programs follow its principles. It is also not only for young children. Older students and adults with reading difficulties can benefit too, as described on the page about learning disabilities in adults.

Common Questions About Structured Literacy

What is structured literacy?

Structured literacy is an explicit, systematic, and cumulative approach to teaching reading, spelling, and writing based on research about how the brain learns to read.

Is structured literacy good for dyslexia?

Yes. Structured literacy is the recommended approach for students with dyslexia.

What is the science of reading?

The science of reading refers to research on how people learn to read, which supports explicit teaching of sounds, letters, and word structure.

What is the difference between structured literacy and phonics?

Phonics is one part of structured literacy, which also teaches phonology, syllables, morphology, syntax, and meaning.

Is structured literacy only for young children?

No. Older students and adults with reading difficulties can also benefit from structured literacy.

Is Orton-Gillingham structured literacy?

Orton-Gillingham-based approaches are among the programs that follow structured literacy principles.

How long does structured literacy intervention take?

Structured literacy intervention often continues for months or longer, with progress monitored regularly.

How do I know if a reading program is working?

A reading program is working if standardized progress measures show improvement, ideally checked at the start, middle, and end.

What should I look for in a reading tutor for dyslexia?

Look for a reading tutor trained in structured literacy who follows a clear sequence and monitors progress.

Do I need an assessment before starting structured literacy?

An assessment is not always required, but it can identify specific needs and guide the right reading support.
