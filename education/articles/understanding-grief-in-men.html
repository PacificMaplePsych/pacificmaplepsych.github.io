Understanding Grief in Men

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If grief has led to thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department.

Grief is a universal response to loss, but the way it is expressed varies widely. Many men grieve in ways that are less visible, such as keeping busy, focusing on practical tasks, or processing feelings privately. Social expectations to stay strong, provide for others, or avoid showing vulnerability can shape how grief is expressed and whether support is sought. As a result, grief in men is often overlooked, by others and sometimes by the person grieving. Understanding these patterns can help men and those around them recognize grief and find support that fits.

How Grief May Show Up

  • Staying busy with work, projects, or tasks related to the loss.
  • Irritability or anger that feels out of proportion.
  • Feeling numb after a loss rather than openly sad.
  • Withdrawing from friends or family and preferring to cope alone.
  • Increased drinking, risk-taking, or other ways of escaping feelings.
  • Physical symptoms, such as fatigue, tension, or sleep problems.
  • Grief that surfaces suddenly, sometimes long after the loss.
  • Honouring the person through action, such as building something or fundraising.

Researchers sometimes describe two broad styles of grieving. Intuitive grieving centres on expressing and sharing emotions. Instrumental grieving centres on thinking, doing, and problem-solving. Many men lean toward the instrumental style, though anyone can grieve either way and most people use a mix.

Why It Happens

From an early age, many boys receive messages to be tough, avoid crying, and handle problems independently. These norms can make it harder to name feelings, ask for help, or grieve openly. Men may also feel pressure to support others, such as a partner or children, and set aside their own grief. Losses that men experience are sometimes less acknowledged socially, such as pregnancy loss, the death of a friend, or the end of a relationship, a pattern known as disenfranchised grief.

Instrumental grieving is a valid way of processing loss. Problems arise when grief is suppressed entirely or channelled into harmful coping, such as heavy drinking or isolation. Men also have higher rates of suicide, and grief combined with isolation and substance use can increase risk. Recognizing grief in its less obvious forms can open the door to support.

Friendships can play an important role. Many men find it easier to connect side by side, through shared activities, rather than face to face in emotional conversations. A walk, a fishing trip, or working on a project together can create space for grief to come up naturally. Friends and family who understand this may be better able to offer support that feels welcome.

What It Is Not

  • Not a lack of caring. Quiet or action-focused grief can be just as deep as tearful grief.
  • Not weakness to feel or show emotion. Expressing grief is a healthy part of processing loss.
  • Not something that disappears if ignored. Suppressed grief often resurfaces as anger, low mood, or physical symptoms, sometimes years later.
  • Not the same as depression, though prolonged grief can lead to depression that needs treatment.

How Grief Is Supported

Support works best when it fits the person's style. Some men benefit from talking with a counsellor one to one, while others prefer peer support groups, activity-based connection, or practical tasks that honour the person who died. Therapy can help people put words to feelings, process guilt or anger, and adjust to life after loss, without forcing a particular way of grieving. If grief is prolonged, intense, or linked with substance use or thoughts of suicide, professional help is especially important.

Common Questions About Grief in Men

Do men grieve differently than women?

Many men lean toward action-focused or private grieving, shaped partly by social expectations, but individual styles vary widely.

What is instrumental grieving?

Instrumental grieving focuses on thinking, doing, and problem-solving, such as keeping busy or taking on practical tasks related to the loss.

Why do some men show grief as anger?

Anger can feel more acceptable than sadness for some men, and it may express pain, helplessness, or guilt after a loss.

Is it unhealthy to keep busy after a loss?

Not always. Keeping busy can be a valid way to cope, but it becomes a concern if it is used to avoid grief entirely for a long time.

Why is men's grief often overlooked?

Grief that is quiet or action-focused can be less visible, and social norms may discourage men from asking for or receiving support.

Can grief increase suicide risk in men?

Grief combined with isolation, heavy drinking, or depression can raise risk. Warning signs should always be taken seriously.

How can I support a grieving man in my life?

Offer steady presence, invite them to shared activities, listen without pushing, and check in over time rather than just at first.

Does grief counselling work for people who do not like talking about feelings?

Yes. Counselling can be adapted to include practical and action-focused approaches, not only emotional discussion.

What is disenfranchised grief?

It is grief that is not openly acknowledged or supported, such as after pregnancy loss, the death of a friend, or a relationship ending.

Are grief support groups helpful for men?

Many men find peer groups helpful, especially activity-based or men's groups that reduce the pressure to talk face to face.
