What to Expect in Your First Session for Disordered Eating

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Eating disorders can affect physical health. If you experience fainting, chest pain, a racing or irregular heartbeat, confusion, or severe weakness, seek medical care right away or call 911. You can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse at HealthLink BC. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time. Youth can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

In your first therapy session for disordered eating, the therapist will usually ask about your eating patterns, thoughts and feelings about food, weight, and your body, any behaviours such as restricting, bingeing, purging, or compulsive exercise, and how these affect your life. They will also ask about your physical health and whether you are connected with a doctor, since eating disorders can have medical effects. The session is a non-judgmental space, and you will not be weighed or asked to change your eating right away. Many people feel ashamed or ambivalent about getting help, and therapists who work with eating concerns understand this.

What to Expect

  1. Getting to know you. Your background, current life, what prompted you to seek help, and your goals.
  2. Eating patterns. What a typical day of eating looks like, any rules you follow, and times when eating feels out of control.
  3. Behaviours. Questions about restricting, bingeing, vomiting, laxative use, or exercise. Answering honestly helps the therapist understand your needs.
  4. Thoughts and feelings. How you think about your body, weight, and shape, and how eating connects to emotions or stress.
  5. Physical health. Symptoms such as dizziness or fatigue, and whether you have recent medical checks.
  6. Mental health and safety. Mood, anxiety, self-harm, and suicide, which are asked about routinely.
  7. Next steps. What treatment might look like and whether other care is needed.

Practical Details

Medical monitoring. Eating disorders are best treated with a team. Your therapist will likely encourage you to see a family doctor for physical checks, which may include blood work and heart monitoring.

Treatment approaches. Enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E), family-based treatment for adolescents, and other approaches have good evidence, as described on the page about therapy approaches for eating disorders.

Dietitian support. Working with a registered dietitian experienced in eating disorders is often recommended.

Level of care. If medical risk is high or symptoms are severe, more intensive treatment may be needed, as explained on the page about higher levels of care. Specialized public eating disorder programs in BC usually need a referral.

Weight-inclusive care. Many therapists take a weight-inclusive approach and focus on behaviours and wellbeing rather than numbers.

Choosing a therapist. Specialized training matters, as described on the page about finding an eating disorder-informed therapist.

How to Prepare

  • Write a few notes. About your eating patterns, behaviours, and how long things have been this way, if speaking about them feels hard.
  • Note physical symptoms. Dizziness, fainting, missed periods, or fatigue.
  • List medications and health providers. Including any recent tests.
  • Think about what you want. Even mixed feelings about change are useful to share.
  • Bring support if helpful. A parent or partner may join part of the session, especially for younger clients.
  • Be gentle with yourself. Reaching out is a meaningful first step.

Common Questions About the First Session for Disordered Eating

What happens in a first therapy session for disordered eating?

In a first session for disordered eating, the therapist asks about eating patterns, behaviours, thoughts about your body, physical health, and goals.

Will I be weighed in my first session for an eating disorder?

Usually not. Therapists generally do not weigh clients in a first eating disorder session, though a doctor may check weight as part of medical monitoring.

Will I have to change my eating right away?

No. The first session for disordered eating focuses on understanding your experience. Changes to eating are planned gradually and collaboratively.

Why does a therapist ask about my physical health for eating concerns?

Eating disorders can affect the heart, electrolytes, and other body systems, so physical health checks are an important part of safe eating disorder care.

Should I see a doctor as well as a therapist for disordered eating?

Yes. A family doctor can monitor physical health with blood work and other checks, which is recommended alongside therapy for disordered eating.

What if I feel unsure about wanting to change my eating?

Mixed feelings about changing eating behaviours are common. Sharing them with your therapist helps shape a plan that fits where you are.

What treatment approaches might be suggested after a first eating disorder session?

Enhanced CBT, family-based treatment for adolescents, and other evidence-based therapies are often suggested for eating disorders, usually alongside dietitian support and medical monitoring.

Can a parent attend a teen's first eating disorder session?

Yes. Parents often join part of a teen's first eating disorder session, and family-based treatment involves parents closely.

When is more intensive eating disorder treatment needed?

More intensive eating disorder treatment may be needed if there is high medical risk, rapid weight change, fainting, or symptoms that are not improving.

Is it normal to feel ashamed about getting help for binge eating?

Yes. Shame is very common with binge eating, and therapists who work with eating concerns offer a non-judgmental space.
