Why Do I Feel Detached From Reality Sometimes

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

For a stretch of time, sometimes just a few minutes, sometimes longer, the world takes on a strange, distant quality. Colours might feel muted, sounds slightly far away, familiar surroundings oddly unfamiliar, as though you are viewing everything through glass or watching it on a screen rather than actually being in it. This can be a genuinely disorienting and even frightening experience, especially the first few times. If this happens to you, it is worth understanding what it is and knowing that it has a name and an explanation.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Exhaustion. Severe sleep deprivation or prolonged physical depletion can produce a dreamlike, detached quality to perception, the brain's way of signalling that its usual processing capacity is significantly reduced.

Panic and acute anxiety. This experience, clinically called derealization, is a well recognized feature of panic attacks and severe anxiety, often arriving suddenly alongside other physical symptoms like a racing heart or shortness of breath.

Substance effects. Certain substances, including alcohol and cannabis, can produce a similar detached, unreal quality to perception as a direct pharmacological effect.

Migraine with aura. Some people experience perceptual changes, including a sense of unreality, as part of a migraine, which is a neurological rather than psychological phenomenon worth mentioning to a doctor if it recurs alongside headache symptoms.

When It Looks More Like Trauma-Related Dissociation

When this experience recurs regularly, particularly in response to stress or reminders of a difficult past experience, it points toward dissociation connected to trauma, specifically a form called derealization. Similar to depersonalization, which involves feeling detached from one's own body, derealization is a protective mechanism that can develop when an experience was overwhelming enough that the mind created distance, not from the body this time, but from the surrounding reality itself, as a way of getting through something unbearable.

What distinguishes trauma-related derealization from an isolated panic episode is both its recurrence and its connection, sometimes obvious, sometimes not immediately clear, to stress or to something that echoes the original overwhelming experience. Many people describe this happening during specific triggers, an argument, a particular environment, a reminder of the past, though for others it can seem to arrive without any clear pattern, which can itself add to the distress of the experience.

It is genuinely important to know that derealization, while frightening in the moment, is not dangerous and does not mean anything is wrong with your perception of reality in any lasting sense. It is a temporary, protective state that resolves, usually within minutes to hours, and responds well to appropriate treatment once the underlying pattern driving it is addressed.

What Can Help

In the moment, grounding techniques that firmly anchor you in concrete, present sensory detail, naming what you can see, touch, and hear right now, can help the episode pass more quickly and feel less disorienting. Reminding yourself, even in the moment, that this is a known, temporary, non-dangerous experience can reduce the fear that often compounds it. For recurring derealization connected to trauma, working with a therapist who understands dissociation, often using trauma-focused or somatic approaches, addresses the underlying pattern directly rather than only managing individual episodes.

Common Questions About Feeling Detached From Reality

Is feeling detached from reality a sign that something is seriously wrong?

No. While it can feel frightening, this experience, known as derealization, is a well understood, temporary, and non-dangerous state. It is worth discussing with a professional if it happens often, but it does not indicate anything is fundamentally wrong with your mind.

What is derealization exactly?

Derealization is the experience of the surrounding world feeling unreal, distant, or dreamlike, as though viewed through glass or a screen, distinct from depersonalization, which involves feeling detached from one's own body specifically.

Can exhaustion alone cause this feeling?

Yes. Severe sleep deprivation or prolonged physical depletion can produce a similar dreamlike, detached quality to perception, reflecting significantly reduced processing capacity rather than a deeper psychological pattern.

Is this connected to panic attacks?

Yes, derealization is a well recognized feature of panic attacks and severe anxiety, often arriving suddenly alongside other physical symptoms like a racing heart, and it can occur as an isolated experience without necessarily connecting to trauma.

Why would the mind create distance from reality rather than just from the body?

Similar to depersonalization, derealization is understood as a protective response that can develop when an experience is overwhelming, with the mind creating distance from the surrounding environment as a way of getting through something unbearable.

How long do these episodes usually last?

This varies, but episodes are typically temporary, often resolving within minutes to hours. If episodes are becoming more frequent or prolonged, it is worth exploring with a professional what might be sustaining the pattern.

Can substances cause this experience too?

Yes, certain substances, including alcohol and cannabis, can produce a similar detached, unreal quality to perception as a direct effect, which is distinct from dissociation connected to trauma or anxiety.

Does this always connect to a specific trigger?

Not always clearly. Some people notice a clear connection to stress or reminders of a difficult experience, while for others the episodes can seem to arise without an obvious pattern, which is itself worth exploring with a professional.

What can I do in the moment when this happens?

Grounding techniques that anchor you in concrete present sensory detail, naming what you can currently see, touch, or hear, can help the episode pass more quickly and reduce how disorienting it feels.

What kind of treatment helps with recurring episodes?

For derealization connected to trauma specifically, working with a therapist who understands dissociation, often using trauma-focused or somatic approaches, addresses the underlying pattern directly rather than only managing individual episodes as they occur.
