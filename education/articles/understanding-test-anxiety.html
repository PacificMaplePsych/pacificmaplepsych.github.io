Understanding Test Anxiety

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Test anxiety is a form of performance anxiety in which worry and physical stress before or during exams interfere with a person's ability to show what they know. People may experience racing thoughts, going blank, a pounding heart, nausea, or trouble sleeping before a test, and they may avoid studying or the exam itself. Some anxiety can sharpen focus, but intense test anxiety uses up working memory and attention, so performance drops. Test anxiety is common in students of all ages and often occurs alongside perfectionism, learning disabilities, or ADHD, especially when someone has struggled with tests before. It responds well to practical strategies and to cognitive behavioural therapy.

Signs of Test Anxiety

  • Thoughts. "I am going to fail," "Everyone else knows this," or going blank during the test.
  • Body. A racing heart, sweating, shaking, stomach aches, headaches, or nausea.
  • Emotions. Dread, panic, irritability, or tearfulness.
  • Behaviour. Avoiding studying, procrastinating, cramming, missing exams, or checking answers repeatedly.
  • After the test. Replaying mistakes or feeling hopeless.

The page about understanding performance anxiety describes related experiences.

Why It Happens

Pressure and High Stakes

Exams that affect grades, scholarships, or admission can feel like threats.

Past Experiences

Struggling on earlier tests can lead to expecting failure.

Learning Differences

Students with dyslexia, slow processing speed, or ADHD may have good reason to find timed tests stressful, as described on the pages about what dyslexia is and slow processing speed. Without accommodations, anxiety can grow.

Perfectionism

High standards and fear of mistakes intensify worry, as described on the page about anxiety and perfectionism.

Avoidance

Putting off studying brings short-term relief but leaves people less prepared, which increases anxiety.

What It Is Not

Test anxiety is not a sign of low intelligence or poor effort, and it does not mean someone does not care. It is also different from being underprepared, though the two can occur together. When test anxiety appears alongside unexplained academic struggles, it is worth considering whether a learning difference is also involved.

What Helps

  • Prepare in spaced sessions. Studying in short sessions over several days is more effective than cramming.
  • Practise under test-like conditions. Timed practice tests reduce uncertainty.
  • Calm the body. Slow breathing with a longer exhale can help before and during the test, as described on the page about breathing exercises that help with anxiety.
  • Challenge catastrophic thoughts. Replace "I will fail" with "I have prepared, and I can do my best," as described on the page about how to challenge anxious thoughts.
  • Use test strategies. Skim the test, start with easier questions, and move on if stuck.
  • Look after basics. Sleep, food, and movement affect concentration.
  • Ask about accommodations. Students with documented needs may get extra time or a quieter room.

On the Day of the Test

On the day of a test, small routines can help. Arrive a little early, avoid last-minute cramming, and bring water and a snack. When you sit down, take a few slow breaths before starting. Read instructions carefully, then answer questions you know first to build confidence. If your mind goes blank, put your pen down, breathe slowly, and move to another question before coming back. After the test, try not to replay every answer. Noticing what went well, as well as what you would change next time, helps reduce anxiety for future exams.

When to Get Help

If test anxiety is severe, leads to avoiding exams, or affects grades despite preparation, cognitive behavioural therapy can help. If learning difficulties may be involved, a psychoeducational assessment can clarify needs and support accommodations, as described on the page about academic accommodations at college or university in BC.

Common Questions About Test Anxiety

Is test anxiety a form of performance anxiety?

Yes. Test anxiety is a form of performance anxiety in which worry and physical stress before or during exams interfere with showing what you know.

What are symptoms of test anxiety?

Symptoms of test anxiety include racing thoughts, going blank, a racing heart, nausea, sweating, dread, and avoiding studying or exams.

Why do I go blank during tests?

Going blank during tests happens because anxiety uses up working memory and attention, making it harder to retrieve information.

Is test anxiety a real condition?

Yes. Test anxiety is a recognized form of performance anxiety that can significantly affect results.

Can test anxiety be linked to a learning disability?

Yes. Students with learning disabilities, ADHD, or slow processing speed often develop test anxiety, especially without accommodations.

How can I calm test anxiety before an exam?

To calm test anxiety, prepare in spaced sessions, practise under test conditions, use slow breathing, and challenge catastrophic thoughts.

Can students get accommodations for test anxiety?

Some students with documented anxiety or learning needs can get accommodations, such as extra time or a quieter room.

Is CBT effective for exam anxiety in students?

Yes. Cognitive behavioural therapy is effective for test and exam anxiety in students.

Is cramming bad for test anxiety?

Cramming often increases test anxiety. Studying in shorter sessions over several days is more effective.

How can parents help a child with test anxiety?

Parents can help a child with test anxiety by staying calm, supporting regular study, focusing on effort, and teaching calming skills.
