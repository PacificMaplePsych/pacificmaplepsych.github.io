What Happens in an Initial Consultation

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

An initial consultation is a first conversation with a clinician about what brings you in, what you hope to change, and what kind of support may help. It is a chance for the clinician to understand your concerns and for you to ask questions and get a sense of whether the fit feels right. Depending on the clinic, it may be a brief call or a full first session. You will usually be asked about your current difficulties, your background, your goals, and any safety concerns. By the end, you should have a clearer idea of the recommended next step, whether that is ongoing therapy, an assessment, or a referral elsewhere.

What to Expect

  1. Introductions and consent. The clinician explains how they work, how confidentiality applies and its legal limits, and how your information is stored. You may be asked to review and sign consent forms.
  2. What brings you in. You describe your main concerns, how long they have been present, and how they affect work, relationships, sleep, and daily life.
  3. Background questions. These may cover your health, past therapy, medications, family history, substance use, and important life events. You can share only what you feel ready to share.
  4. Safety check. Clinicians routinely ask about thoughts of self-harm or suicide. This is a standard part of care, not a sign that anything is assumed.
  5. Goals. Together you start to clarify what you would like to be different.
  6. Recommendations and questions. The clinician shares initial thoughts on what might help and answers your questions about the process.

Practical Details

Length. A full initial consultation often lasts about 50 to 60 minutes, though some clinics begin with a shorter call to check fit and availability.

Format. Consultations may be offered in person or by secure video. Virtual sessions generally require you to be located in BC at the time of the appointment, and a private, quiet space helps.

Referrals. In BC, you can usually book a consultation with a private psychologist or counsellor without a doctor's referral, as explained on the page about referrals to see a psychologist. Some benefit plans ask for one before reimbursing sessions.

Fees and coverage. Fees and cancellation policies vary, and private psychology services are not covered by MSP. Check your extended health plan and ask the clinic about fees and receipts before booking.

Couples and assessments. Consultations for couples therapy or psychological assessment follow a similar pattern but focus on the relationship or on the questions an assessment would answer.

How to Prepare and What Happens Next

  • Jot down your main concerns. A few notes about what is bothering you, when it started, and examples can help you stay focused if you feel nervous.
  • Think about your goals. Even a rough sense, such as "I want to sleep better" or "argue less with my partner," is useful.
  • Gather relevant information. A list of medications, past diagnoses, or reports from earlier assessments.
  • Prepare your own questions. For example, about the clinician's approach, experience with your concern, session frequency, and fees.
  • Check the logistics. Confirm the time, location or video link, and any forms to complete in advance.

After the consultation, you may book regular sessions, begin an assessment, or receive suggestions for other services if another option fits better. Early sessions often lead to a treatment plan that sets out goals and approach. You can also discuss how often to attend and how long therapy may take. It is okay to take time to decide, and it is okay to look for a different clinician if the fit does not feel right. If you are in crisis before your appointment, call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Initial Consultations

What happens in an initial consultation with a therapist?

In an initial consultation, you discuss your concerns, background, and goals, review consent and confidentiality, and hear the clinician's initial recommendations for support.

How many minutes is a typical initial consultation?

A full initial consultation often lasts about 50 to 60 minutes, although some clinics start with a shorter call to check fit and availability.

Do I have to share everything in my first consultation?

No. You can share only what you feel ready to share in a consultation, and you can return to difficult topics later as trust builds.

Why does the clinician ask about suicide in an intake appointment?

Asking about self-harm and suicide is a routine part of an intake appointment for every client. It helps keep care safe and does not mean anything is assumed.

Can I have an initial consultation by video?

Many clinics offer consultations by secure video. You generally need to be located in BC at the time of a virtual appointment with a BC clinician.

Do I need a referral to book a consultation with a psychologist?

Usually not in BC. You can book a consultation with a private psychologist directly, although some benefit plans require a referral for reimbursement.

What should I bring to an initial consultation?

Bring notes on your concerns and goals, a list of medications, any past assessment reports, and your own questions for the consultation.

What questions can I ask during a consultation?

You can ask about the clinician's approach, experience with your concern, session frequency, fees, and how confidentiality works during the consultation.

What happens after the initial consultation?

After the consultation, you may book regular sessions, begin an assessment, or be referred to another service, and early sessions often lead to a treatment plan.

What if the first consultation does not feel like a good fit?

It is okay to look for another clinician if a consultation does not feel like a good fit. Feeling comfortable with your therapist matters for progress.
