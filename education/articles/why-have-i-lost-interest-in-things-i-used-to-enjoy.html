Why Have I Lost Interest in Things I Used to Enjoy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

The guitar sits in the corner gathering dust. Your favourite show plays in the background while you scroll without really watching. Friends suggest plans you would once have jumped at, and you make excuses. It is not that you actively dislike these things now. They just do not seem to do anything for you anymore, and it is hard to remember why they ever did.

Losing interest in things you used to enjoy can be a normal part of change, or it can be a signal that something deeper is going on. Paying attention to how it feels and what else is happening helps tell the difference.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Growing and changing. Interests shift across a lifetime. Moving on from a hobby, especially when something new is taking its place, is a normal part of development rather than a problem.

Too little time or energy. A new baby, a demanding job, or caregiving can crowd out hobbies. The interest may still be there, buried under exhaustion.

Burnout. When work stress has drained you for a long time, there may be nothing left for enjoyment. Burnout often brings cynicism about work that can spread to other areas.

Grief. After a loss, pleasure often feels flat or even wrong for a while. This can be part of feeling numb after a loss.

Medications and substances. Some medications, including certain antidepressants, can dull emotional highs as well as lows. Regular alcohol or cannabis use can also blunt pleasure over time, something we discuss in Why Do I Rely on Alcohol to Unwind.

Trauma. Emotional numbing and feeling cut off from life can follow traumatic experiences, sometimes alongside feeling detached from reality.

ADHD. Some people with ADHD cycle through intense interests that fade once the novelty wears off, which is different from a general loss of pleasure and is covered in Why Do I Get So Bored So Easily.

When It Looks More Like Depression

A marked loss of interest or pleasure is one of the two core symptoms of depression, alongside low mood. Clinicians call it anhedonia. It reflects changes in the brain's reward system, making it harder both to look forward to things and to enjoy them in the moment.

Signs that depression is likely involved include:

  • The loss of interest is broad, affecting many activities and people, not just one hobby.
  • It has lasted at least two weeks and represents a real change from how you used to be.
  • It comes with other symptoms, such as low mood, fatigue, changes in sleep or appetite, trouble concentrating, guilt, or struggling to get out of bed.
  • Even when you do an activity, it feels flat, like going through the motions.

One tricky feature of anhedonia is that it removes the motivation that would normally help you recover. Waiting until you feel like doing something can mean waiting a long time.

What Can Help

  • Act before the feeling arrives. Schedule small doses of activities you used to enjoy, even if they feel flat. Interest often returns gradually through doing, not before it.
  • Lower the bar. Ten minutes of a hobby counts. So does watching one episode with full attention.
  • Notice small shifts. Rating enjoyment from zero to ten after activities can reveal small improvements that are easy to miss.
  • Stay connected. Time with people, even when it does not feel rewarding yet, tends to support recovery.
  • Review medications with your doctor if emotional flatness began after starting or changing one.

Common Questions About Losing Interest in Things

Is losing interest in hobbies a sign of depression?

It can be. A loss of interest or pleasure, called anhedonia, is one of the core symptoms of depression. It is more likely to be depression when it is broad, lasts two weeks or more, and comes with other symptoms such as low mood, fatigue, or sleep changes.

What is anhedonia?

Anhedonia is a reduced ability to feel interest or pleasure in activities that were once enjoyable. It can affect both anticipating good things and enjoying them in the moment, and it is a key symptom of depression.

Is it normal to lose interest in a hobby as I get older?

Yes. Interests naturally shift over time, especially when new priorities or passions emerge. It becomes more of a concern when the loss of interest is widespread, is not replaced by anything new, and comes with low mood or exhaustion.

Can antidepressants make me lose interest in things?

Some people notice emotional blunting on certain antidepressants, where both lows and highs feel muted. If this happens, talk to your prescriber rather than stopping medication on your own, since adjustments are often possible.

Can burnout cause me to lose interest in things outside of work?

Yes. Burnout can drain energy so thoroughly that there is little left for hobbies or social life. If rest and time off restore your interest, burnout may be the main driver. If not, depression may also be present.

How is ADHD boredom different from losing interest because of depression?

With ADHD, interest often fades when novelty wears off, but the capacity for enthusiasm remains and quickly attaches to new things. With depression, the ability to feel interest or pleasure itself is reduced across many areas at once.

Should I force myself to do things I no longer enjoy?

Gently scheduling small amounts of previously enjoyed activities is often helpful, since interest tends to return through doing rather than before. The aim is not forcing enjoyment but giving it a chance to come back.

Can grief make me lose interest in everything?

Yes. After a significant loss, it is common for activities to feel flat or meaningless for a while. This usually eases gradually. If it persists for a long time or deepens, grief counselling or therapy can help.

Does alcohol or cannabis use make activities feel less enjoyable?

Regular use can blunt the brain's reward system over time, making everyday pleasures feel less satisfying. Some people notice their interest in hobbies returns after cutting back.

How long does it take for interest in things to come back once depression is treated?

It varies. Some people notice small improvements within weeks of starting treatment, while full return of interest can take longer. Tracking small changes helps, because early progress is often subtle.
