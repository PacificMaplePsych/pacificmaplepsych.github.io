Understanding Autism Support Options for Children

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Support options for autistic children include behavioural approaches such as applied behaviour analysis (ABA), developmental and relationship-based approaches such as DIRFloortime and Relationship Development Intervention (RDI), naturalistic approaches that blend both, speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, social skills groups, parent coaching, and counselling. Each has a different focus, and many families combine several. There is no single best approach for every child. The right mix depends on your child's age, strengths, needs, and interests, and on your family's values. Increasingly, families and professionals look for neurodiversity-affirming care that respects autistic identity, focuses on wellbeing and communication, and avoids trying to make children appear less autistic.

The Main Types of Support

Behavioural Approaches (ABA)

Applied behaviour analysis uses principles of learning, such as reinforcement, to build skills and reduce behaviours that cause harm or distress. It is typically designed by a Board Certified Behaviour Analyst and delivered with behaviour interventionists. ABA has research support for building certain skills, but it has also faced criticism from autistic adults, especially older, rigid forms that focused on compliance or suppressing harmless behaviours such as stimming. Many current practitioners use more play-based, child-led, and assent-based methods.

Developmental and Relationship-Based Approaches

DIRFloortime focuses on following a child's lead in play to build connection, communication, and emotional development. RDI focuses on helping parents guide children in flexible thinking and shared experiences. These approaches emphasize relationships and emotional growth.

Naturalistic Developmental Behavioural Interventions

These blend behavioural and developmental principles in play and everyday routines, often with parents closely involved. Examples include the Early Start Denver Model and JASPER.

Speech-Language Therapy

Supports communication in all its forms, including spoken language, alternative and augmentative communication (AAC), and social communication.

Occupational Therapy

Supports sensory processing, motor skills, and daily living skills, as described on the page about occupational therapy for sensory processing.

Parent Coaching

Helps parents understand their child and use strategies at home, as described on the page about parent coaching.

Counselling and Therapy

Helps autistic children and teens with anxiety, emotional regulation, and self-understanding, and supports parents and siblings.

Social Groups

Small, structured groups where children practise connection and communication with peers in a supportive setting.

How to Choose

  • Start with your child. What are their strengths, interests, and biggest needs right now?
  • Choose priorities. Focus on one or two goals, such as communication or managing distress.
  • Ask about goals and methods. Good providers explain what they are working on and why, and involve you in decisions.
  • Look for respect and assent. Your child should seem comfortable, engaged, and not frightened or distressed by sessions.
  • Watch for progress. Review goals regularly and adjust if something is not helping.
  • Check funding rules. In BC, Autism Funding covers different services depending on age, as described on the page about what happens after your child is diagnosed with autism.

Including Your Child's Voice

As children grow, involving them in decisions about their own support becomes increasingly important. Even young children can show through their behaviour which activities they enjoy and which cause distress. Older children and teens can share preferences directly. Respecting your child's voice builds trust and self-advocacy, and support is usually more effective when the child feels comfortable and engaged.

What It Is Not

No approach cures autism, and good support does not aim to. Be cautious of treatments that promise recovery, require large payments up front, or lack research support. Support should help your child communicate, learn, feel safe, and thrive as themselves, as described on the page about neurodiversity-affirming therapy.

Common Questions About Autism Support Options

What are the main support options for autistic children?

Support options for autistic children include ABA, DIRFloortime, RDI, naturalistic approaches, speech therapy, occupational therapy, parent coaching, counselling, and social groups.

What is ABA therapy for autism?

ABA uses learning principles such as reinforcement to build skills and reduce harmful behaviours in autistic children, usually designed by a behaviour analyst.

Why is ABA controversial?

ABA is controversial because some autistic adults criticize older, compliance-focused forms that suppressed harmless behaviours such as stimming.

What is DIRFloortime?

DIRFloortime is a play-based, relationship-focused approach that follows a child's lead to build connection, communication, and emotional development.

What is RDI for autism?

Relationship Development Intervention helps parents guide autistic children in flexible thinking and shared experiences.

How can I tell if an autism therapy provider is neurodiversity-affirming?

A neurodiversity-affirming autism provider respects your child's communication style, focuses on wellbeing rather than compliance, and does not try to stop harmless behaviours such as stimming.

Can Autism Funding pay for ABA or DIRFloortime in BC?

BC Autism Funding can pay for behaviour consultants and other approved services, with rules that differ for children under 6 and ages 6 to 18.

How do I choose the right autism support for my child?

Choose autism support based on your child's strengths, interests, and needs, start with one or two priorities, and look for respectful providers.

Is there a cure for autism?

No. Autism is not something to cure. Good support helps autistic children communicate, learn, and thrive as themselves.

Can counselling help autistic children?

Yes. Counselling can help autistic children with anxiety, emotional regulation, and self-understanding, and can support parents.
