Supporting a Parent With Dementia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Supporting a parent with dementia can be one of the most emotionally demanding experiences of adult life. As memory, thinking, and personality change, adult children often experience grief for the parent they knew, even while that parent is still alive, along with stress, guilt, frustration, and exhaustion. Many also juggle caregiving with work and their own families. Practical planning, learning communication strategies, sharing responsibilities, accessing community supports, and caring for your own wellbeing can make the journey more manageable. Support for caregivers is not a luxury. It helps both you and your parent.

The Emotional Side of Caregiving

Anticipatory and Ambiguous Grief

Many caregivers grieve gradually as their parent changes, as described on the pages about anticipatory grief and grieving someone who is still alive.

Role Reversal

Caring for the person who once cared for you can feel disorienting and sad.

Guilt

Caregivers often feel guilty about losing patience, needing breaks, or considering residential care.

Family Conflict

Siblings may disagree about care, finances, or decisions, or contribute unequally.

Exhaustion

The demands of caregiving can lead to burnout, as described on the page about caregiver burnout.

Communication Strategies

  • Keep sentences short and simple.
  • Avoid arguing or correcting when your parent is confused. Respond to the feeling behind their words.
  • Use their name and make eye contact.
  • Offer choices between two options rather than open questions.
  • Use routines and familiar objects to reduce confusion.
  • Stay calm during agitation, and look for triggers such as hunger, pain, or overstimulation.

Finding Moments of Connection

Even as dementia progresses, moments of connection are often still possible. Music, familiar photos, gentle touch, shared activities such as folding laundry or gardening, and simply sitting together can bring comfort. These moments matter for both of you and can become meaningful memories during a difficult time.

Practical Planning

  • Legal and financial planning early, while your parent can still participate, such as powers of attorney and representation agreements.
  • Health care planning, including discussing wishes for future care.
  • Community supports, such as home support, adult day programs, and respite care through your health authority.
  • Dementia support organizations, such as the Alzheimer Society of B.C., which offer education and caregiver support.
  • Considering care options as needs change, including assisted living or long-term care.

Protecting Your Wellbeing

  • Share the load with siblings, family, and community services.
  • Take regular breaks, including respite care.
  • Keep some of your own life, such as friendships and activities.
  • Set boundaries around what you can realistically do, as described on the page about how to set boundaries with family.
  • Connect with other caregivers who understand.
  • Watch for signs of burnout, as described on the page about managing burnout as a caregiver.

What It Is Not

Feeling frustrated, resentful, or relieved at times does not mean you do not love your parent. These are normal reactions to an extraordinarily hard situation. Choosing residential care is also not abandonment. It is often the safest and most loving option when needs exceed what can be provided at home.

When to Get Support

Consider therapy if you feel overwhelmed, depressed, anxious, guilty, or isolated, or if family conflict is adding to stress. Therapy can help you grieve, set boundaries, make decisions, and find moments of connection with your parent.

Common Questions About Supporting a Parent With Dementia

How do I cope with a parent with dementia?

Cope with a parent's dementia by learning about the condition, planning early, sharing responsibilities, using community supports, and caring for yourself.

Why do I feel like I am losing my parent to dementia before they die?

Dementia gradually changes memory and personality, so many adult children grieve the parent they knew long before death, which is called anticipatory or ambiguous grief.

How should I talk to a parent with dementia?

Use short, simple sentences, avoid arguing or correcting, respond to feelings, and offer simple choices.

Is it normal to lose patience as a dementia caregiver for a parent?

Yes. Losing patience is a common, human reaction for adult children caring for a parent with dementia, and it does not mean you do not love them.

Can I get respite care for a parent with dementia in BC?

Yes. BC health authorities and community programs offer respite options, such as adult day programs and short stays, so caregivers of people with dementia can rest.

When should a parent with dementia move to long-term care?

Long-term care may be needed when safety or care needs exceed what can be provided at home, a decision often made with health professionals.

What planning should we do after a dementia diagnosis?

After a dementia diagnosis, plan early for legal, financial, and health care decisions while your parent can still participate.

How can siblings share caregiving for a parent with dementia?

Siblings can share caregiving by dividing tasks, holding regular family meetings, and using outside supports to reduce conflict.

How do I know if caring for my parent with dementia is burning me out?

Exhaustion, irritability, resentment, poor sleep, withdrawing from your own life, and feeling hopeless can mean dementia caregiving is burning you out.

Can therapy help dementia caregivers?

Yes. Therapy can help dementia caregivers cope with grief, guilt, stress, boundaries, and difficult decisions.
