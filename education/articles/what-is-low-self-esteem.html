What Is Low Self-Esteem

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Self-esteem is the overall opinion you hold about yourself and your worth as a person. Low self-esteem means that this opinion is persistently negative. People with low self-esteem often believe they are not good enough, less capable, or less deserving than others, even when evidence suggests otherwise. Everyone has moments of self-doubt. Low self-esteem is different because it is ongoing, colours how people interpret everyday experiences, and affects relationships, work, and wellbeing. It is not a diagnosis, but it is closely linked with anxiety and depression. Self-esteem can change, and many people build a kinder and more balanced view of themselves.

What Low Self-Esteem Looks Like

  • Feeling like you are never good enough.
  • A harsh inner critic, similar to being very hard on yourself.
  • Struggling to accept compliments or dismissing achievements.
  • Comparing yourself to others and coming up short.
  • Avoiding challenges for fear of failure or embarrassment.
  • Difficulty saying no or expressing needs.
  • Feeling responsible when things go wrong.
  • Seeking reassurance or approval from others.
  • Putting up with poor treatment in relationships.

Some people with low self-esteem work very hard to prove their worth, through perfectionism or achievement. Others withdraw and avoid situations where they might be judged. Both patterns can keep low self-esteem going.

Why It Happens

Self-esteem develops through experiences, especially early ones. Frequent criticism, harsh punishment, neglect, bullying, or being compared unfavourably with siblings can teach children that they are not good enough. Growing up with high expectations and conditional approval can lead to a belief that worth depends on performance. Trauma, discrimination, and difficult relationships in adulthood can also erode self-esteem. Some people carry a sense of being different because of learning differences or neurodivergence, for example with ADHD.

Psychologists describe how core beliefs, such as I am unlikeable or I am a failure, lead to rules for living, such as I must never make mistakes. When those rules are broken, the core belief feels confirmed. People also tend to notice evidence that fits their beliefs and overlook evidence that does not. Over time, this cycle can make low self-esteem feel like the truth rather than a learned view.

Low self-esteem often shows up differently in different areas of life. Someone may feel confident at work but insecure in relationships, or comfortable with friends but anxious about their appearance. Identifying where self-doubt is strongest can help focus change on the areas that matter most.

Social media can add pressure, since curated images of others' lives invite constant comparison. Many people with low self-esteem find that limiting time on certain platforms, or unfollowing accounts that leave them feeling worse, eases self-criticism.

What It Is Not

  • Not an accurate measure of your worth. It is a learned perspective, often shaped by past experiences.
  • Not the same as humility. Humility is balanced, while low self-esteem is harsh and distorted.
  • Not fixed by positive affirmations alone. Lasting change usually involves examining beliefs and behaviour.
  • Not a diagnosis, though it often accompanies anxiety, depression, and eating disorders.
  • Not permanent. Self-esteem can grow at any age.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Therapy can help people identify and challenge negative core beliefs and build a more balanced view of themselves. Cognitive behavioural therapy for low self-esteem, often based on the work of Melanie Fennell, helps people notice self-critical thinking, test anxious predictions, and collect evidence of their strengths. Compassion-focused therapy helps soften the inner critic and develop self-kindness. Schema therapy addresses long-standing patterns rooted in childhood. Practical steps include keeping a record of positive qualities, practising assertiveness, setting realistic goals, and spending time with people who treat you well.

Common Questions About Low Self-Esteem

What causes low self-esteem?

Early criticism, neglect, bullying, conditional approval, trauma, and difficult relationships can all contribute to low self-esteem.

How might low self-worth show up in everyday life?

It often shows up as a harsh inner critic, dismissing compliments, comparing yourself to others, avoiding challenges, and seeking approval.

Is low self-esteem a mental illness?

It is not a diagnosis, but it is closely linked with anxiety, depression, and other mental health difficulties.

What are core beliefs in low self-esteem?

Core beliefs are deep ideas about yourself, such as I am not good enough, that shape how you interpret experiences.

Why can I not accept compliments?

When compliments conflict with negative self-beliefs, they can feel untrue or uncomfortable, so they are dismissed.

Do positive affirmations help low self-esteem?

They may help some people, but lasting change usually involves examining beliefs and practising new behaviours.

Why do I put up with being treated badly by people close to me?

When you doubt your worth, poor treatment can feel deserved or safer than being alone. Low self-esteem can also fuel people-pleasing and fear of rejection.

How can I be less harsh with myself?

Compassion-focused approaches help people notice the inner critic and respond with the warmth they would offer a friend.

Is it too late to feel better about myself as an adult?

No. With therapy and practice, many adults develop a more balanced and positive view of themselves.

How is low self-esteem different from humility?

Humility is a balanced view of yourself. Low self-esteem involves harsh, distorted beliefs about your worth.
