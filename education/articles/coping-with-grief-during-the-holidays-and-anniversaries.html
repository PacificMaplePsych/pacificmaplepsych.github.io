Coping With Grief During the Holidays and Anniversaries

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Coping with grief during holidays and anniversaries usually starts with expecting that these days may be hard, planning ahead, and giving yourself permission to do things differently. Special days often bring the absence of a loved one into sharp focus. Birthdays, the anniversary of a death, family celebrations, and cultural or religious holidays can stir intense sadness, even years later. Some people also feel dread in the weeks beforehand. These reactions are a normal part of grief. There is no right way to handle these days, and small, thoughtful choices can make them more manageable.

Why Planning Ahead Helps

Holidays and anniversaries carry memories, rituals, and expectations. They may involve traditions the person was part of, family gatherings where their absence is obvious, or social pressure to feel cheerful. Grief can resurface in waves, sometimes called anniversary reactions, bringing sadness, irritability, fatigue, or trouble sleeping around significant dates. The anticipation is often harder than the day itself.

Planning ahead gives you a sense of choice at a time when grief can feel overwhelming. It helps you decide what feels meaningful, what to change, and what support you need. Understanding that grief does not move in a straight line, as described on the page about grief and its stages, can also ease the worry that you are going backward.

Strategies to Try

1. Expect Mixed Feelings

You may feel sadness, joy, guilt, and relief in the same day. All of these can exist together. Laughing or enjoying yourself does not mean you have stopped caring.

2. Decide What to Keep and What to Change

Some people find comfort in keeping traditions, while others need to change them. You might celebrate somewhere new, scale down, or create a different routine this year. It is fine to choose one approach now and another in later years.

3. Create a Way to Remember

Lighting a candle, sharing stories, cooking a favourite recipe, visiting a meaningful place, looking at photos, or making a donation can honour the person. Some families set a place at the table or share memories during a meal.

4. Plan the Day

Having a rough plan, including time with people and time alone, can reduce uncertainty. Let others know what you need.

5. Give Yourself Permission to Say No

You do not have to attend every event. Leaving early, taking breaks, or declining invitations is okay.

6. Lean on Others

Tell a trusted friend or family member when a hard date is coming. Arrange to check in with someone. A grief group can also offer understanding from others facing similar days.

7. Look After Your Body

Grief is tiring. Rest, meals, movement, and time outside support coping. Be cautious with alcohol, which can intensify low mood.

8. Talk Openly With Children

Children also grieve on special days. Including them in remembrance rituals and answering questions honestly can help.

Common Obstacles

Family members may grieve differently, with some wanting to talk about the person and others avoiding the topic. Talking openly about different needs before the day can reduce conflict. Some people feel pressure to be happy, especially during holidays centred on celebration. It can help to share with one person how you are really feeling. Others worry that grief should have eased by now. Grief often returns around special dates for years, and this is normal. People grieving losses that others do not recognize, such as the death of an ex-partner, a pet, or a pregnancy, may feel especially alone, as described on the page about disenfranchised grief.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If grief feels as intense as it did early on for a long time, if you are unable to function in daily life, feel hopeless, or are using alcohol or other substances to cope, professional support can help. Signs of prolonged grief include persistent longing, difficulty accepting the death, and feeling that life has lost meaning. Grief counselling can help you find ways to carry your loss while living your life. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Grief on Holidays and Anniversaries

Why does my grief come back so strongly around the anniversary of the death?

Significant dates stir memories and highlight absence, which can bring waves of grief, sometimes called anniversary reactions.

Is it okay to skip holiday traditions after a loss?

Yes. You can keep, change, or skip traditions, and your choice may be different in later years.

How can I honour my loved one on their birthday?

You might share stories, cook their favourite meal, visit a meaningful place, or make a donation in their name.

Why do I feel dread in the weeks before a holiday since my loss?

Anticipation is common and often harder than the day itself. Planning ahead can ease some of the dread.

Is it wrong to enjoy myself during the holidays while grieving?

No. Joy and grief can exist together, and enjoying moments does not mean you have stopped caring.

How do we handle family members who grieve differently during holidays?

Talk openly beforehand about different needs, and find ways to include both remembering and space.

How can I help my children cope with grief at holiday time?

Include them in remembrance rituals, answer questions honestly, and let them know all feelings are okay.

Should I avoid alcohol during hard grief anniversaries?

Being cautious is wise, since alcohol can intensify low mood and disrupt sleep.

Will anniversaries always be this painful?

Many people find the intensity eases over time, though special dates may still bring some grief.

What signs tell me my holiday grief needs more than time and family support?

If grief stays intense for a long time, affects daily functioning, or brings hopelessness, counselling can help.
