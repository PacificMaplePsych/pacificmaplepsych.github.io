Why Do I Have Scary Intrusive Thoughts About My Baby

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You are holding your baby at the top of the stairs, and an image flashes through your mind of them falling. You are bathing them, and a horrifying thought appears out of nowhere. Maybe it is a picture of something going terribly wrong, or a thought about harm coming to them, even harm you would never want to cause. The thought is so upsetting that you feel sick, ashamed, and terrified of what it might mean. Many parents never tell anyone.

If this is happening to you, please know you are far from alone. Unwanted, frightening thoughts about a baby are one of the most common and least discussed experiences of new parenthood, and they happen to mothers, fathers, and non-birthing parents alike.

Seek help immediately by calling 911 or going to your nearest emergency department if you feel any urge or intent to act on thoughts of harm, if the thoughts feel acceptable or make sense to you, or if you or someone close to you notices confusion, seeing or hearing things others do not, beliefs that seem out of touch with reality, or being unable to sleep even when you have the chance. You can also call or text 9-8-8 at any time in Canada.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

A normal protective brain. After a baby arrives, the brain's threat detection system becomes highly tuned to danger. Research suggests that most new parents experience at least some unwanted thoughts about accidental harm to their baby, and many experience thoughts about harm they might cause, which they find disturbing. These thoughts reflect how much you want to protect your baby.

Sleep deprivation. Exhaustion makes the mind more prone to intrusive thoughts and makes them feel more convincing and harder to dismiss.

Postpartum anxiety. Constant worry about the baby's health and safety, restlessness, and a racing mind are hallmarks of perinatal anxiety, which can include frequent frightening thoughts.

Postpartum depression. Low mood, hopelessness, guilt, and feeling disconnected from the baby can come with intrusive thoughts as well.

A difficult or traumatic birth. After a frightening delivery or medical complications, intrusive images and on-edge feelings can be part of a trauma response.

When It Looks More Like Perinatal OCD or Anxiety

For some parents, intrusive thoughts become frequent, intense, and consuming. When they drive rituals and avoidance, this may be perinatal or postpartum OCD. It is a treatable anxiety-related condition, and it is important to understand what it is and what it is not.

The defining feature of these thoughts is that they are unwanted and deeply distressing. People with perinatal OCD are horrified by their thoughts precisely because the thoughts go against everything they value. Research indicates that these ego-dystonic intrusive thoughts are not associated with an increased risk of harming the baby. The distress itself is a sign that the thoughts do not reflect what you want.

Signs that OCD or anxiety may be involved include:

  • Thoughts that repeat, feel sticky, and cause intense fear or guilt.
  • Avoiding bathing the baby, using knives, or being alone with the baby in case you might do something.
  • Repeatedly checking the baby's breathing, locks, or appliances, similar to what we describe in Why Can't I Stop Checking Things Over and Over.
  • Seeking constant reassurance that you are a good parent or would never cause harm.
  • Mentally reviewing, praying, or "undoing" thoughts to neutralize them.

For more on unwanted thoughts outside the perinatal period, see Why Do I Have Thoughts That Scare Me and I Don't Know Why.

This is very different from postpartum psychosis, a rare but serious emergency in which a person may lose touch with reality, and thoughts of harm may not feel wrong to them. That is why the warning signs in the box above call for immediate help.

What Can Help

  • Label the thought. Telling yourself "that is an intrusive thought, not a wish or a plan" can reduce its power.
  • Do not try to push thoughts away forcefully. Suppression tends to make intrusive thoughts return more often.
  • Gently reduce avoidance of caring for your baby where it is safe to do so, ideally with guidance from a therapist.
  • Protect sleep as much as possible, sharing night feeds when you can.
  • Tell someone you trust, such as your partner, doctor, midwife, or public health nurse. Speaking the thoughts aloud often reduces shame.

Common Questions About Intrusive Thoughts in New Parents

Are intrusive thoughts about harming my baby common in new parents?

Yes, very common. Research suggests that most new parents experience unwanted thoughts about accidental harm to their baby, and many also have disturbing thoughts about causing harm. Most never talk about them because of shame, which can make parents feel much more alone than they are.

Do intrusive thoughts about my baby mean I will act on them?

Unwanted intrusive thoughts that horrify you are not associated with an increased risk of harming your baby. The distress they cause reflects that they go against your values. However, if you ever feel urges or intent to act, or the thoughts feel acceptable, seek emergency help right away.

What is postpartum OCD?

Postpartum or perinatal OCD involves frequent, distressing intrusive thoughts, often about harm coming to the baby, along with compulsions such as checking, avoiding, or seeking reassurance to reduce anxiety. It is treatable, especially with CBT and exposure and response prevention.

How is postpartum OCD different from postpartum psychosis?

In postpartum OCD, parents recognize their thoughts as unwanted and are horrified by them. Postpartum psychosis is a rare emergency that can involve confusion, hallucinations, beliefs out of touch with reality, and severe sleeplessness, and thoughts of harm may not feel wrong. Psychosis needs immediate medical care.

Can fathers and partners have intrusive thoughts about the baby too?

Yes. Intrusive thoughts and perinatal anxiety or OCD can affect fathers, adoptive parents, and non-birthing partners. The brain changes that heighten threat awareness in new parenthood are not limited to the person who gave birth.

Why do I avoid bathing my baby or being alone with them?

When intrusive thoughts feel frightening, avoiding situations linked to them can bring short-term relief. Unfortunately, avoidance usually strengthens the fear over time. Therapy can help you safely and gradually return to those everyday caregiving tasks.

Should I tell my doctor about scary thoughts about my baby?

Yes. Doctors, midwives, and public health nurses are familiar with intrusive thoughts in new parents and can help you access support. Sharing them is an important step toward getting the right care, not a reason to fear judgment.

Why do frightening thoughts about the baby get stronger after a bad night?

Yes. Exhaustion makes the mind more vulnerable to intrusive thoughts and makes them feel more real and harder to let go of. Protecting sleep, including sharing night feeds when possible, can reduce their frequency and intensity.

Can postpartum anxiety cause constant frightening thoughts?

Yes. Postpartum anxiety often involves persistent worry about the baby's safety, racing thoughts, and difficulty relaxing, which can include frequent frightening images or what-if scenarios. It is common and very treatable.

What treatment helps with intrusive thoughts after having a baby?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, especially exposure and response prevention for OCD, is highly effective. It helps parents change their relationship with intrusive thoughts and reduce avoidance and checking. Medication may also be considered with a doctor, including during breastfeeding.
