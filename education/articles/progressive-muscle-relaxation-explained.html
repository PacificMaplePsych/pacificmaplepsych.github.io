Progressive Muscle Relaxation Explained

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Progressive muscle relaxation (PMR) is a technique in which you tense a group of muscles for a few seconds, then release them and notice the feeling of relaxation, moving through the body one area at a time. It was developed in the early twentieth century and remains one of the most widely used relaxation skills. PMR can reduce physical tension, calm the stress response, and help with sleep. Research suggests it can help reduce stress and anxiety for many people, and it is often taught as part of cognitive behavioural therapy. It takes practice, but it needs no equipment and can be done almost anywhere.

Why Progressive Muscle Relaxation Helps

When people feel stressed or anxious, muscles often tighten without them noticing, especially in the shoulders, jaw, neck, and stomach. This tension sends signals back to the brain that something is wrong, which can keep the stress response switched on. Tension also contributes to headaches, fatigue, and aches.

PMR works in two ways. First, deliberately tensing and releasing muscles teaches you to notice the difference between tension and relaxation, so you can catch tension earlier in daily life. Second, releasing tension signals safety to the nervous system, supporting the body's natural calming response. Over time, with practice, many people find they can relax more quickly. The broader mind-body link is described on the pages about the stress response and physical symptoms of anxiety.

Strategies to Try

Full Progressive Muscle Relaxation

Set aside about fifteen to twenty minutes in a quiet place. Sit or lie comfortably. For each muscle group, breathe in and tense the muscles firmly but not painfully for about five seconds, then breathe out and release all at once. Notice the change for about fifteen seconds before moving on. A common sequence is:

  1. Hands: make fists.
  2. Arms: bend your elbows and tighten your upper arms.
  3. Shoulders: raise them toward your ears.
  4. Face: scrunch your eyes and forehead, then clench your jaw gently.
  5. Neck: press your head gently back.
  6. Chest and upper back: take a deep breath and hold it while squeezing your shoulder blades together.
  7. Stomach: tighten your abdominal muscles.
  8. Legs: tighten your thighs, then point your toes to tense your calves.
  9. Feet: curl your toes.

Finish by taking a few slow breaths and noticing how your whole body feels.

Shorter Versions

Once the full version feels familiar, you can combine muscle groups, for example arms and hands together, then face and neck, then torso, then legs, which takes about five to seven minutes. Later, some people move to release-only relaxation, scanning the body and letting go of tension without tensing first.

Quick Tension Checks

During the day, pause and check common tension spots such as your jaw, shoulders, and hands. Tense them for a moment, then release. This can be done at a desk, in a car, or before a stressful conversation.

Using PMR at Bedtime

Practising PMR in bed can help quiet the body before sleep. It is often combined with slow breathing. Other sleep strategies are covered on the page about insomnia.

Common Obstacles

  • Feeling restless or bored. This is common at first. Guided audio recordings can help you stay focused.
  • Racing thoughts. Minds wander. Gently bring attention back to the muscles without judging yourself.
  • Not feeling relaxed right away. PMR is a skill that improves with regular practice. Daily practice for a couple of weeks often makes a noticeable difference.
  • Pain or injury. Skip or gently tense areas that are injured or painful. Check with a health professional if you have a medical condition, such as high blood pressure, muscle problems, or chronic pain.
  • Feeling more anxious when relaxing. Some people, especially those with trauma histories, find that relaxing brings up uncomfortable feelings. Keeping your eyes open, shortening the practice, or trying another approach can help.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

PMR is a helpful tool, but it is rarely enough on its own when anxiety, stress, or sleep problems are persistent or severe. If anxiety is interfering with work, relationships, or daily life, or you are having panic attacks, avoiding situations, or struggling with ongoing insomnia, therapy can help address the causes. A therapist can also tailor relaxation skills to your needs and combine them with other approaches. If you notice chest pain, trouble breathing, or symptoms that feel new or severe, seek medical care or call 911.

Common Questions About Progressive Muscle Relaxation

How long does a full progressive muscle relaxation session take?

A full session usually takes about fifteen to twenty minutes, and shorter versions can take five to seven minutes.

How tightly should I tense my muscles during PMR?

Tense firmly but not painfully, about five seconds, then release all at once.

Why tense my muscles first instead of just relaxing them?

Tensing first helps you notice the contrast between tension and relaxation, making it easier to spot tension in daily life.

How often should I practise PMR to see a difference?

Daily practice for a couple of weeks often helps, and the skill becomes quicker with time.

Can I use progressive muscle relaxation to fall asleep?

Yes. Many people practise it in bed to calm the body before sleep, often with slow breathing.

Is PMR safe if I have an injury or chronic pain?

Skip or gently tense painful areas, and check with a health professional if you have a medical condition.

Why do I feel more anxious when I try to relax?

Some people find relaxing brings up uncomfortable feelings. Shortening the practice or keeping your eyes open can help.

Do I need a recording to do PMR?

No, but guided recordings can help you stay focused when you are learning.

Which muscles hold the most stress tension?

The jaw, shoulders, neck, hands, and stomach are common spots, though it varies from person to person.

Is progressive muscle relaxation enough to treat anxiety?

It can help, but persistent or severe anxiety usually needs therapy that addresses its causes.
