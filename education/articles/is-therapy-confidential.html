Is Therapy Confidential

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Yes, therapy is confidential. What you discuss with a regulated therapist in BC is kept private and is not shared without your consent, with a small number of exceptions set out in law and professional standards. These generally include situations where there is a serious and imminent risk of harm to you or someone else, when a child may be at risk of abuse or neglect, when records are ordered by a court, and certain other legal or regulatory requirements. Your therapist should explain confidentiality and its limits at the start of therapy and answer any questions. Knowing these rules in advance can help you feel safer speaking openly.

How It Works

Your consent. In general, your therapist will only share information with others, such as your doctor, employer, insurer, or family, if you give consent, usually in writing. You can decide what is shared and with whom, and you can withdraw consent.

Records. Therapists keep clinical records, which are stored securely and protected under privacy laws. In BC, private clinics are generally governed by provincial privacy legislation. You usually have the right to request access to your records.

Professional standards. Regulated professionals, such as psychologists, follow codes of conduct from their regulatory body that require confidentiality.

Consultation. Therapists sometimes consult with colleagues or supervisors to provide the best care. These discussions are kept confidential and often do not include identifying details.

Practical Details

Common limits to confidentiality.

  • Risk of serious harm. If there is a serious and imminent risk that you may harm yourself or someone else, a therapist may need to take steps to keep people safe.
  • Child protection. In BC, anyone who has reason to believe a child is being abused or neglected has a legal duty to report it.
  • Court orders. Records can be ordered by a court in some legal proceedings.
  • Other legal requirements. Such as certain regulatory or public health obligations.

Benefits and insurance. When you submit receipts to an insurer, basic information such as dates and type of service is shared, but session content usually is not. Some third-party payers may request reports, which require your consent.

Couples and family therapy. Policies about information shared privately vary. Ask how individual disclosures are handled, as explained on the page about whether both partners need to attend every session.

Young people. In BC, minors who are able to understand the nature and consequences of treatment may be able to consent to their own care, which affects what is shared with parents. Ask the therapist how this works.

Virtual sessions. Clinics use secure video platforms, and you can protect your privacy by choosing a private space, as discussed on the page about virtual versus in-person therapy.

Assessments. Assessment reports are confidential and shared only with your consent, as noted on the page about assessment reports.

What to Ask Your Therapist

  • How is my information stored, and who can access it?
  • What are the specific limits of confidentiality in your practice?
  • What happens if I mention thoughts of self-harm?
  • What information goes to my insurer?
  • How do you handle communication with my doctor or other providers?
  • In couples therapy, what is your policy on information shared privately?

Talking about suicidal thoughts in therapy does not automatically lead to a breach of confidentiality. Therapists usually work with you on safety first. If you are in crisis, call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Common Questions About Therapy Confidentiality

Is everything I say in therapy confidential?

Almost everything you say in therapy is confidential, with limited legal exceptions such as serious risk of harm, child protection concerns, or court orders.

When can a therapist break confidentiality in BC?

A therapist in BC may need to break confidentiality if there is serious and imminent risk of harm, a child may be at risk, or a court orders records.

Is information shared with my doctor without my permission?

Your therapist generally shares information with your doctor only with your consent, and you can decide what is shared.

Does using workplace benefits put my therapy privacy at risk?

No. Using workplace benefits does not mean your employer is told about your therapy, and session content is not shared without your consent.

What details are disclosed to my insurer when I claim therapy?

Insurers usually receive basic information such as dates and type of service, not the content of your therapy sessions.

Does mentioning suicidal thoughts mean my therapist must disclose it?

Not automatically. Therapists usually work with you on safety first, and confidentiality is broken only if risk is serious and imminent.

Can I see my own therapy records?

You usually have the right to request access to your therapy records under privacy laws, with some limited exceptions.

Is couples therapy confidential between partners?

Couples therapy is confidential from others, but policies on what one partner shares privately vary, so ask your therapist about this.

Can a teenager have confidential therapy in BC?

In BC, minors who understand the nature and consequences of treatment may consent to their own care, which affects what is shared with parents.

Are virtual therapy sessions confidential?

Virtual therapy sessions are confidential and use secure platforms. Choosing a private space at home also helps protect your privacy.
