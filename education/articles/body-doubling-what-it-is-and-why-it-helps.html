Body Doubling: What It Is and Why It Helps

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Body doubling is a strategy where you do a task while another person is present, either in the same room, on a video call, or in a virtual co-working session. The other person does not need to help with the task. Their presence alone can make it easier to start, stay focused, and finish. Body doubling is especially popular among people with ADHD, and many autistic people and others who struggle with motivation or focus also find it helpful. Formal research on body doubling specifically is still limited, but it draws on well-understood effects of accountability, social presence, and structure. For many people, it turns tasks that felt impossible into tasks that simply get done.

Why Body Doubling Helps

ADHD affects executive functions, such as starting tasks, sustaining attention, and managing time. Many people with ADHD know what they need to do but struggle to begin, especially with boring or overwhelming tasks. This can lead to the stuck feeling described on the page about ADHD paralysis.

Having someone else present can help in several ways. It adds gentle accountability, since you have committed to working at a certain time. It creates a sense of structure and a clear start and end. Social presence may help some people feel more alert and focused. It also reduces the isolation and self-criticism that can come with struggling alone. For people with AuDHD or other neurodivergent profiles, a quiet, predictable body double can be especially calming.

Strategies to Try

1. Choose a Body Double

This could be a friend, partner, family member, or coworker. Choose someone whose presence feels comfortable and not distracting.

2. Try In-Person Sessions

Work at the same table, or in the same room, while each of you does your own tasks, such as paperwork, cleaning, or studying.

3. Use Video Calls

Set up a video call with a friend and keep cameras on while you both work. Microphones can be muted.

4. Join Virtual Co-Working Sessions

Online co-working platforms and communities offer scheduled sessions where people work quietly together, often with short check-ins at the start and end.

5. Set a Clear Task and Time

Before starting, say what you plan to work on and for how long, such as "I will sort the mail for twenty-five minutes."

6. Check In at the End

Share what you got done. Acknowledging progress builds momentum.

7. Use It for Hard-to-Start Tasks

Body doubling is especially useful for tasks you tend to avoid, such as taxes, emails, cleaning, or getting organized.

8. Combine With Other Tools

Timers, task lists, and short work sprints fit well with body doubling. More ideas are on the page about time management tools for ADHD.

Common Obstacles

Some people feel embarrassed asking for a body double. It can help to explain simply, "I focus better when someone else is working nearby. Would you like to work together for an hour?" Many people enjoy the shared time. Others find a body double distracting if the person talks too much. Agreeing on quiet time, with brief check-ins, solves this. Scheduling can be tricky, so virtual co-working sessions offer more flexibility. Body doubling may also stop working as well once it becomes routine, so varying people, places, or formats can help.

Body doubling can make it easier to rely on others without building other systems. Using it alongside other strategies, such as those on the page about non-medication strategies for managing ADHD, keeps you flexible.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If difficulties starting and finishing tasks are causing ongoing problems at work, school, or home, or if you feel stuck, overwhelmed, or low, more support can help. An ADHD assessment can clarify what is going on. ADHD-informed therapy, coaching, and medication prescribed by a physician are all options that can work alongside body doubling.

Common Questions About Body Doubling

Is body doubling just working next to someone?

Essentially, yes. Body doubling means doing a task while another person is present, in person or online, which makes it easier to start and stay focused.

Why does body doubling help with ADHD?

Body doubling adds accountability, structure, and social presence, which can help people with ADHD start and stay on tasks.

Does the body double need to help with my task?

No. A body double usually works on their own tasks. Their presence alone is what helps.

Can body doubling work over video calls?

Yes. Many people use body doubling over video calls, keeping cameras on while each person works quietly.

What are virtual body doubling or co-working sessions?

Virtual co-working sessions are scheduled online sessions where people body double by working quietly together, often with short check-ins.

Is there research showing body doubling works?

Formal research on body doubling specifically is limited, but it draws on well-understood effects of accountability and social presence.

How do I ask someone to be my body double?

Explain simply that you focus better with someone working nearby, and invite them to do their own tasks alongside you as a body double.

What tasks is body doubling best for?

Body doubling is especially helpful for tasks you tend to avoid, such as paperwork, cleaning, emails, or studying.

Can body doubling help autistic people too?

Yes. Many autistic people and those with AuDHD find a calm, predictable body double helpful for focus and starting tasks.

What if my body double distracts me?

Agree on quiet time with brief check-ins at the start and end, or try a different body double or format.
