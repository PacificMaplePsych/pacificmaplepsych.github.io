What Is a Safety Plan for Suicidal Thoughts

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are thinking about suicide right now, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department. Young people can also contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868.

A safety plan is a short, personalized, written list of steps and contacts to help you stay safe when suicidal thoughts become strong. It includes your warning signs, ways to cope on your own, people and places that help, professionals and crisis lines to contact, and steps to make your surroundings safer. Safety plans are used widely by mental health professionals and are supported by research showing they can reduce suicidal behaviour. They work because suicidal crises often come in waves, and having clear steps decided in advance makes it easier to get through the hardest moments. A safety plan is best created with a therapist or doctor, but you can also start one on your own.

Why a Safety Plan Helps

When suicidal thoughts are intense, it can be very hard to think clearly or remember what helps. Emotions narrow attention, and the pain can make it feel as if nothing will change. A safety plan offers a guide written when you were calmer, so you do not have to figure things out in the moment. It breaks the crisis into steps, starting with things you can do alone and moving toward reaching out to others and professionals.

Many people describe suicidal urges as rising and falling over minutes or hours. Getting through the peak, and reducing access to means during that time, can save lives. More about these experiences is on the page about suicidal thoughts.

Strategies to Try

A safety plan usually includes these steps, written in your own words:

  1. Warning signs. Thoughts, feelings, situations, or behaviours that tell you a crisis may be building, such as feeling trapped, not sleeping, isolating, or thinking "I cannot do this anymore."
  2. Coping strategies you can do alone. Activities that help distract or calm you, such as going for a walk, taking a shower, listening to music, breathing slowly, or holding a comforting object.
  3. People and places that offer distraction. Friends, family, or places such as a coffee shop, library, or community centre where you can be around others.
  4. People you can ask for help. Names and phone numbers of trusted people you can tell that you are struggling.
  5. Professionals and crisis services. Your therapist or doctor, 9-8-8, 911, your nearest emergency department, and in BC, 8-1-1 for health advice.
  6. Making your environment safer. Steps to reduce access to means, such as asking someone to hold medications, locking away or removing items that could be used for harm, and avoiding alcohol or drugs during a crisis.
  7. Reasons for living. Things that matter to you, such as people, pets, values, goals, or experiences you want to have. Some people include photos or notes.

Keep your plan somewhere easy to reach, such as on your phone, in your wallet, or by your bed. Some people share it with a trusted person so they know how to help.

Common Obstacles

It can be hard to believe a plan will help when you are in pain. Using it anyway, one step at a time, can help you get through a difficult moment. Some people feel embarrassed writing coping strategies that seem small, but small actions often make a real difference in a crisis. Others find it difficult to reach out to people. Writing down exactly what to say, such as "I am having a hard time, can you talk?" can make it easier.

Plans work best when they are updated. Review yours after a crisis, when your circumstances change, or with your therapist regularly. A safety plan can sit alongside a relapse prevention plan, which focuses on staying well over time. If you also self-harm, the page about self-harm may be helpful.

If you are supporting someone else, the page about supporting someone with suicidal thoughts explains how you can help them create and use a plan.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

A safety plan is a tool, not a substitute for professional care. If you are having frequent or intense suicidal thoughts, if you have a plan or access to means, or if your safety plan steps are not helping, reach out right away. Call or text 9-8-8, call 911, or go to your nearest emergency department. Ongoing therapy can help address what is driving the pain and build longer-term hope and coping.

Common Questions About Suicide Safety Plans

What should a suicide safety plan include?

Warning signs, coping strategies, people and places for distraction, people to ask for help, crisis contacts, steps to reduce access to means, and reasons for living.

Do safety plans actually reduce suicide risk?

Research suggests safety planning can reduce suicidal behaviour and help people get through crises.

Can I make a safety plan by myself?

Yes, you can start one alone, though creating it with a therapist or doctor is often more helpful.

Where should I keep my safety plan?

Keep it somewhere easy to reach, such as on your phone, in your wallet, or by your bed.

Why does my safety plan start with things I can do alone?

Starting with personal coping steps helps you act quickly, then move toward others and professionals if needed.

How do I reduce access to means during a crisis?

Ask someone to hold medications, lock away or remove items that could be used for harm, and avoid alcohol or drugs.

Should I share my safety plan with someone?

Sharing it with a trusted person can help them know how to support you in a crisis.

What if my safety plan does not help in the moment?

Reach out right away by calling or texting 9-8-8, calling 911, or going to an emergency department.

After a rough night, should I go back and change what is written in my plan?

Yes. Reviewing it after any crisis, when circumstances change, and regularly with your therapist keeps it useful.

What are reasons for living on a safety plan?

They are people, pets, values, goals, or experiences that matter to you and can help you hold on.
