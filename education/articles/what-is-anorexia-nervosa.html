What Is Anorexia Nervosa

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Anorexia can cause life-threatening medical complications. See a doctor promptly for fainting, dizziness, chest pain, an irregular or very slow heartbeat, or severe weakness, and call 911 in an emergency. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Anorexia nervosa is a serious eating disorder characterized by restriction of food intake leading to significantly low body weight for a person's age and health, an intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat, and a disturbance in how body weight or shape is experienced. It affects people of all genders, ages, and backgrounds. Anorexia has one of the highest mortality rates of any mental health condition, but recovery is possible, especially with early, specialized treatment.

What Anorexia Looks Like

  • Strict limits on food, skipping meals, or cutting out whole food groups.
  • Intense fear of weight gain, even when underweight.
  • Seeing oneself as larger than one is, or basing self-worth heavily on weight or shape.
  • Rigid food rituals, such as cutting food into tiny pieces or eating very slowly.
  • Excessive exercise, or in some people, purging.
  • Frequent weighing or body checking.
  • Withdrawal from social events involving food.
  • Physical signs such as feeling cold, fatigue, dizziness, and missed periods.

At home, families may notice changes in eating, mood, and secrecy. At school or work, concentration and energy often drop. People with anorexia may not see themselves as unwell, which can make seeking help difficult.

Why It Happens

Anorexia develops from a combination of factors. Genetics play a significant role. Temperament traits such as perfectionism, anxiety, and a need for control are common. Dieting, puberty, stress, trauma, or life transitions can act as triggers. Cultural pressures that equate thinness with worth, discussed on the page about diet culture and mental health, can contribute. Once restriction begins, starvation itself changes brain function and increases preoccupation with food and body, rigidity, and anxiety, which helps keep the disorder going.

Anorexia often gives a sense of control or achievement, which is part of why it can feel difficult to let go of. Restriction may soothe anxiety in the short term, while making life narrower over time. Friends and family commonly notice mood changes, irritability, and withdrawal before weight change becomes obvious. Early attention to these signs matters, since outcomes tend to be better the sooner treatment starts.

What It Is Not

  • Not a choice or vanity. Anorexia is a serious illness with biological and psychological roots.
  • Not defined by appearance. Atypical anorexia involves the same patterns without being underweight, and it carries similar medical risks.
  • Not ARFID. Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder involves restriction for sensory reasons, fear of choking, or low interest in food, without body image concerns.
  • Not bulimia or binge eating disorder, though people can move between eating disorder patterns over time.
  • Not something that resolves with willpower. Recovery usually requires specialized support.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment involves both medical and psychological evaluation, because anorexia affects the heart, bones, hormones, and other systems. Treatment is usually provided by a team that may include a physician, dietitian, and therapist. For adolescents, family-based treatment has strong evidence. For adults, approaches such as enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy for eating disorders and other specialized therapies are used. Nutritional restoration is essential, and some people need day treatment or hospital care. Recovery is often gradual and may include setbacks, but many people recover fully.

Common Questions About Anorexia Nervosa

What are the main signs of anorexia nervosa?

Main signs include restricting food, significantly low body weight, intense fear of weight gain, and a distorted experience of body size or shape.

What is atypical anorexia?

Yes. Atypical anorexia involves the same restrictive patterns and fears without meeting low-weight criteria, and it can carry serious medical risks.

Why is anorexia considered so serious?

Anorexia can damage the heart, bones, and other organs, and it has one of the highest mortality rates of any mental health condition.

What causes anorexia nervosa?

It develops from a mix of genetics, temperament such as perfectionism and anxiety, dieting, stress, and cultural pressures. No single cause explains it.

Does anorexia only affect teenage girls?

No. Anorexia affects people of all genders, ages, and backgrounds, including boys, men, and older adults.

Why do people with anorexia not see how unwell they are?

Starvation affects thinking, and the disorder can distort body perception and make restriction feel necessary, so people may not recognize the danger.

How is anorexia different from ARFID?

Anorexia involves fear of weight gain and body image concerns. ARFID involves restriction for sensory reasons or fear of choking, without those concerns.

What treatments are used for anorexia?

Treatment usually involves a team, with medical monitoring, nutritional restoration, and therapy such as family-based treatment for adolescents or specialized CBT for adults.

How can I help a loved one who might have anorexia?

Express concern calmly and without comments on appearance, encourage a medical assessment, and learn about eating disorders. Family support is an important part of recovery.

Is full recovery from anorexia possible?

Yes. Many people recover fully, especially with early, specialized treatment, though recovery often takes time and may include setbacks.
