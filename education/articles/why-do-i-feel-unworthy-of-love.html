Why Do I Feel Unworthy of Love

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Even in a relationship where you are genuinely cared for, some part of you does not quite believe it, or waits for the other shoe to drop, or wonders what the other person will eventually see that makes them change their mind. It can feel less like a thought you are having and more like a fact you already know about yourself, one that evidence to the contrary does not seem to touch. If this belief has been with you for a long time, it is worth understanding where it actually comes from.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Depression. Feelings of worthlessness are a core symptom of depression, and this can extend specifically into a felt sense of being undeserving of love or good treatment, particularly during a depressive episode.

Situational low self-esteem. A difficult period, a job loss, a breakup, a specific failure, can temporarily lower someone's sense of worth in ways that touch how deserving of love they feel, without necessarily reflecting a long-standing, deeply rooted pattern.

Social comparison. Constant exposure to curated versions of other people's relationships and lives, particularly through social media, can distort a person's sense of their own worth relative to others in ways that are more about the comparison than an accurate self-assessment.

A specific painful relationship experience. Being treated poorly by one particular person can, for a period, shake someone's sense of worth without necessarily reflecting a broader or earlier pattern, especially if the belief lifts as the person moves further from that specific experience.

When It Looks More Like an Early-Formed Core Belief

When the sense of being unworthy of love feels less like a passing thought and more like a foundational, almost factual belief about yourself, one that persists even amid clear evidence of being genuinely loved and cared for, this often reflects what is sometimes called a core belief, formed early in life and reinforced over time until it feels like simple truth rather than one interpretation among many. This typically develops from childhood experiences where love felt conditional, inconsistent, or was withheld or withdrawn in ways that taught a lasting lesson about one's fundamental worth.

A defining feature of a core belief like this is how resistant it is to contradicting evidence. Being told convincingly, and repeatedly, that you are loved often does very little to shift it, since the belief operates beneath the level of rational evaluation, filtering incoming information in a way that discounts anything that does not confirm it and readily accepts anything that does. Genuine affection can be met with suspicion or dismissed as temporary, while any sign of distance or disappointment, even ordinary and unrelated, can feel like confirmation of what was already believed to be true.

This pattern often coexists with real behavioural consequences, difficulty accepting love when it is offered, subtle self-sabotage in relationships, or a tendency to select partners or situations that end up confirming the belief rather than challenging it. This is not a reflection of actual unworthiness, which is not a real, measurable quality anyone possesses or lacks, but a deeply learned filter through which love and connection get interpreted.

What Can Help

Beginning to notice the belief as a belief, rather than a fact, "I'm having the thought that I don't deserve this" rather than "I don't deserve this", can create a small but meaningful distance from its automatic authority. Deliberately practicing receiving care without immediately deflecting or dismissing it, even when it feels uncomfortable, can slowly build new evidence over time. Therapy that specifically addresses core beliefs, tracing their origin and actively working to update them, tends to be considerably more effective than trying to reason your way out of the belief on your own.

Common Questions About Feeling Unworthy of Love

Is feeling unworthy of love the same as low self-esteem generally?

They are related but not identical. General low self-esteem can touch many areas of life, while a specific, deeply rooted belief about being unworthy of love often feels more foundational and specifically resistant to being changed by evidence in relationships.

Why doesn't being told I'm loved actually change how I feel?

A deeply rooted core belief operates beneath rational evaluation, filtering incoming information to discount anything that contradicts it. This is why reassurance, even sincere and repeated, often fails to shift the underlying belief on its own.

Could this be connected to depression instead of an early belief?

Yes, this is worth considering. Feelings of worthlessness are a core symptom of depression and can extend specifically into feeling undeserving of love, particularly during a depressive episode, which is somewhat distinct from a longstanding core belief.

Where does a belief like this usually come from?

It typically develops from childhood experiences where love felt conditional, inconsistent, or was withheld or withdrawn in ways that taught a lasting lesson about one's fundamental worth, well before adult relationships came into the picture.

Why do I sometimes push away or doubt someone who treats me well?

This reflects how genuine affection can be met with suspicion or dismissed as temporary when it conflicts with an existing core belief, since the belief tends to feel more trustworthy than the actual evidence of being cared for.

Can social media comparison make this feeling worse?

Yes, for many people. Constant exposure to curated versions of other people's relationships can distort a sense of one's own worth relative to others, which can intensify feelings of unworthiness even without being the original source of the belief.

Is unworthiness something a person actually has or lacks?

No. Worthiness is not a real, measurable quality that people possess in varying amounts. The felt sense of unworthiness reflects a learned filter through which love and connection are interpreted, not an accurate assessment of a person's actual value.

What is a “core belief” in this context?

A core belief is a foundational, often unconscious belief about oneself, formed early in life and reinforced over time, that shapes how new experiences and information get interpreted, often operating more like assumed fact than one perspective among many.

Can noticing the belief “as a belief” actually help?

Yes, to a meaningful degree. Framing it internally as a thought you are having, rather than an established fact, can create some distance from its automatic authority, which is often a useful first step before deeper therapeutic work.

What does therapy for this kind of belief typically involve?

It typically involves tracing the belief's origin, understanding how it continues to filter present-day experience, and actively working to build and internalize new, contradicting evidence over time, rather than only managing its symptoms.
