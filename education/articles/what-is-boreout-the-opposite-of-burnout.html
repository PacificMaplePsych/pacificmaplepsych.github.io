What Is Boreout, the Opposite of Burnout

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Boreout is a term used to describe chronic boredom, underload, and lack of meaning or challenge at work. It is sometimes called the opposite of burnout, because instead of too much work, people experience too little of the right kind of work. Yet boreout can be just as draining. People may feel exhausted, unmotivated, and disconnected, even though they are not overworked. Boreout is not a formal diagnosis, but research on work boredom shows it can affect mental health, physical health, and job performance. Recognizing it helps people make changes that restore engagement.

What Boreout Looks Like

  • Feeling bored, restless, or understimulated most of the workday.
  • Having too little to do, or tasks that feel meaningless or far below your skills.
  • Pretending to be busy, stretching out tasks to fill time.
  • Dreading work despite a light workload.
  • Tiredness, low energy, and irritability.
  • Feeling stuck in your career or like your abilities are wasted.
  • Low self-esteem or guilt about not contributing more.
  • Distraction, scrolling, or snacking to pass the time.

People with boreout may be reluctant to talk about it, worried about seeming ungrateful or risking their job. This silence can deepen feelings of isolation.

Why It Happens

Boreout typically develops from three linked experiences: being underchallenged, having too little meaningful work, and lacking interest in the tasks available. It can occur when roles are poorly designed, when responsibilities shrink after reorganization, when skills are not matched to tasks, or when work is highly repetitive. Some people stay in understimulating jobs for security, stability, or because they feel they have few options. Lack of control over tasks and limited opportunities for growth add to the problem.

Some people are more affected by boredom than others. People with ADHD, for example, often find boring, repetitive tasks especially hard and may experience strong restlessness. Boredom is not simply a lack of activity. It reflects a mismatch between the brain's need for engagement and what the environment provides.

Although boreout involves too little challenge, its effects can resemble those of burnout, including exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness. Chronic boredom at work has been linked with stress, low mood, and reduced wellbeing. It can also affect life outside work, leaving people too flat or drained to enjoy their free time.

Remote and hybrid work can sometimes make boreout harder to notice. Without colleagues nearby, people may have fewer chances to pick up new tasks, share ideas, or feel part of a team. Loneliness can then compound boredom. Regular check-ins and deliberate efforts to connect can help.

What It Is Not

  • Not laziness. People with boreout often want to contribute more but lack the opportunity.
  • Not an easy problem to have. Chronic understimulation can be genuinely distressing.
  • Not a formal diagnosis, though it can contribute to depression or anxiety.
  • Not solved by simply working more hours. The issue is meaning and challenge, not quantity.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Addressing boreout often starts with naming what is missing, such as challenge, variety, autonomy, or meaning. Talking with a manager about taking on new projects, learning new skills, or reshaping a role, sometimes called job crafting, can help. Professional development, mentoring, or cross-training can add interest. Outside work, meaningful hobbies, volunteering, or learning can meet needs for engagement. Therapy can help people explore values, address low mood or anxiety, build confidence to ask for change, and consider whether a new role or career path would be a better fit.

Common Questions About Boreout

What is boreout?

Boreout is chronic boredom, underload, and lack of meaningful challenge at work, which can leave people drained and disengaged.

Can too little work be as draining as too much?

Yes. Burnout comes from too many demands, while boreout comes from too little meaningful work, though both can cause exhaustion and cynicism.

What are early signs that work boredom is turning into boreout?

Watch for dreading work despite a light load, pretending to be busy, low energy, and a growing sense that your skills are wasted.

Is boreout a real condition?

It is not a formal diagnosis, but research on work boredom shows real effects on wellbeing and performance.

Why do I pretend to be busy at work?

Many people with boreout feel pressure to look productive, stretching tasks to fill time, which adds to stress.

What is job crafting?

Job crafting means reshaping tasks, relationships, or how you think about your role to make work more engaging and meaningful.

What can I suggest if my role no longer uses my skills?

Focus on wanting to contribute more, suggest specific projects or skills, and ask about development opportunities.

Are people with ADHD more affected by work boredom?

Often, yes. People with ADHD can find understimulating, repetitive tasks especially difficult.

What if feeling underused at work starts affecting my mood at home?

That is a sign to act. Long-term boreout can contribute to low mood and loss of interest, and it is worth seeking support if these persist.

Should I change jobs if I have boreout?

Sometimes reshaping your current role helps. If not, exploring a new role or career may be worth considering.
