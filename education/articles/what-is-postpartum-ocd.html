What Is Postpartum OCD

If you are having thoughts of suicide, or you feel you might act on thoughts of harm, call or text 9-8-8 or call 911 right away. Confusion, hallucinations, or beliefs that feel real after birth may signal postpartum psychosis, which needs emergency care. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse.

Postpartum OCD is a form of obsessive-compulsive disorder that begins or worsens during pregnancy or after birth. It involves intrusive, unwanted thoughts or images, often about harm coming to the baby, such as dropping them, accidentally hurting them, or even deliberately harming them. These thoughts are deeply distressing precisely because they go against everything the parent wants. People with postpartum OCD then use compulsions, such as checking, avoiding, or seeking reassurance, to reduce the anxiety. Postpartum OCD is more common than many people realize. Having these thoughts does not mean a parent will act on them, and the condition is very treatable.

What Postpartum OCD Looks Like

  • Scary, intrusive thoughts about your baby, such as images of the baby being hurt.
  • Fear of accidentally harming the baby through a mistake, contamination, or carelessness.
  • Avoiding bathing, changing, or holding the baby, or avoiding knives or stairs.
  • Repeatedly checking the baby's breathing, bottles, locks, or car seats.
  • Seeking reassurance from a partner, family, or health professionals.
  • Excessive cleaning or sterilizing out of fear of illness.
  • Mental compulsions, such as reviewing, praying, or replacing bad thoughts with good ones.
  • Intense guilt, shame, and fear that the thoughts mean you are a bad parent.

Why It Happens

After a baby arrives, parents become highly attuned to potential danger, which is a natural protective response. For some, this heightened alertness tips into OCD. Hormonal changes, severe sleep deprivation, and the enormous responsibility of caring for a newborn contribute. A personal or family history of OCD or anxiety increases risk. Stress and a difficult pregnancy or birth can also play a role. Non-birthing parents can develop perinatal OCD too.

Almost all new parents experience some intrusive thoughts about harm coming to their baby. In OCD, these thoughts are interpreted as dangerous or meaningful, which triggers intense anxiety and compulsions. Compulsions bring short-term relief but reinforce the belief that the thoughts are significant, keeping the cycle going. Postpartum OCD is one of the conditions described on the page about perinatal mood and anxiety disorders, and it often occurs alongside postpartum anxiety or depression.

Many parents with postpartum OCD suffer in silence because they fear being judged or having their baby taken away. In reality, clinicians experienced in perinatal mental health understand that these thoughts are symptoms of OCD, not intentions. Sharing them is an important step toward relief.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign you will harm your baby. People with postpartum OCD are horrified by their thoughts and go to great lengths to prevent harm.
  • Not postpartum psychosis. In OCD, the person recognizes the thoughts as unwanted. Psychosis involves losing touch with reality and is an emergency.
  • Not a reflection of who you are. Intrusive thoughts target what matters most to you.
  • Not untreatable. Most parents improve significantly with the right care.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician experienced in perinatal mental health will ask about intrusive thoughts, compulsions, avoidance, and mood, and will distinguish OCD from psychosis. Cognitive behavioural therapy with exposure and response prevention is the most effective treatment. It involves gradually facing feared thoughts and situations, such as bathing the baby, while resisting checking, avoidance, and reassurance-seeking. Psychoeducation about intrusive thoughts often brings significant relief. Some medications are considered compatible with breastfeeding and may be recommended. Partner involvement helps reduce accommodation and supports recovery. Practical support with sleep and baby care reduces overall stress.

Common Questions About Postpartum OCD

Why am I suddenly afraid to bathe or hold my newborn?

This may be postpartum OCD, which begins or worsens around pregnancy or birth, often involving intrusive thoughts about harm coming to the baby.

Are parents with these frightening thoughts a danger to their baby?

No. People with postpartum OCD find these thoughts horrifying and go to great lengths to keep their baby safe.

How common is postpartum OCD?

It is more common than many people realize, and intrusive thoughts about the baby are very common in new parents.

How can clinicians tell perinatal OCD apart from psychosis?

They look at insight. In OCD, thoughts are unwanted and recognized as irrational. Psychosis involves losing touch with reality and is an emergency.

Why do I avoid being alone with my baby?

Avoidance is a common compulsion in postpartum OCD, driven by fear of the intrusive thoughts.

Can partners develop perinatal OCD?

Yes. Non-birthing parents can also experience intrusive thoughts and OCD after a baby arrives.

How does treatment help parents stop checking and avoiding?

It might involve gradually bathing or holding the baby while resisting checking and reassurance, with a therapist guiding the pace.

Will my baby be taken away if I tell someone about my thoughts?

Clinicians experienced in perinatal mental health understand that OCD thoughts are symptoms, not intentions, and focus on support.

What role can a co-parent play in recovery from perinatal OCD?

Learn about OCD, avoid giving repeated reassurance, share baby care, and support treatment.

Can I take OCD medication while breastfeeding?

Some medications are considered compatible with breastfeeding. Discuss options with your doctor.
