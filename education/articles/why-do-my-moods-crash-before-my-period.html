Why Do My Moods Crash Before My Period

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

For most of the month, you feel like yourself. Then, sometime in the week or two before your period, something shifts. You become tearful, irritable, anxious, or hopeless, sometimes so intensely that you barely recognize yourself. You might pick fights, withdraw from people, or feel like everything in your life is falling apart. A few days after your period starts, the fog lifts, and you are left wondering what happened and apologizing to the people around you.

Mood changes linked to the menstrual cycle are very common. For some people they are mild and manageable. For others they are severe enough to disrupt relationships, work, and wellbeing every single month.

If you have thoughts of suicide, before your period or at any time, please call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, available anywhere in Canada at any time, or call 911.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Premenstrual syndrome. Many people experience mild to moderate mood changes, bloating, breast tenderness, fatigue, or cravings before their period. This is common and usually manageable with lifestyle adjustments.

A flare of an existing condition. Depression, anxiety, ADHD, and other conditions can worsen before a period. This is sometimes called premenstrual exacerbation. The difference is that symptoms are present throughout the month and simply get worse premenstrually.

Perimenopause. In the years leading up to menopause, hormone levels fluctuate more unpredictably, which can intensify or change premenstrual mood patterns.

Other mood conditions. Bipolar disorder and emotional intensity linked to borderline personality disorder can involve significant mood shifts that are not tied to the cycle, as described in Why Do My Moods Swing So Dramatically and Why Do My Emotions Feel So Intense and Fast-Changing. Tracking helps separate these patterns.

Thyroid and health issues. Thyroid problems and some medications can affect mood and may be worth checking with a doctor.

When It Looks More Like PMDD

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder, or PMDD, is a recognized condition involving severe mood symptoms that appear in the week or so before menstruation and improve within a few days after it begins. It is not simply "bad PMS." Current research suggests PMDD involves an unusual sensitivity in the brain to the normal rise and fall of reproductive hormones, rather than abnormal hormone levels.

Common PMDD symptoms include:

  • Marked mood swings, sudden sadness, or tearfulness.
  • Intense irritability or anger, often causing conflict, similar to what we describe in Why Do I Feel Irritable Instead of Sad.
  • Deep hopelessness, self-criticism, or feeling worthless.
  • Anxiety, tension, or feeling keyed up.
  • Loss of interest, difficulty concentrating, fatigue, sleep changes, and physical symptoms such as bloating.

The key feature is timing. Symptoms appear premenstrually, ease soon after the period starts, and are largely absent in the week after. Diagnosis usually involves tracking symptoms daily for at least two cycles, because memory alone often blurs the pattern.

What Can Help

  • Track daily. A simple daily mood rating alongside cycle days, over two or three months, is one of the most useful things you can bring to a doctor or psychologist.
  • Plan around the pattern. Once you know your difficult days, you can schedule lighter commitments and extra rest during that window.
  • Share the pattern with people close to you. Letting partners or family know what to expect can reduce conflict and increase support.
  • Look after the basics. Regular sleep, movement, and reduced alcohol and caffeine can ease premenstrual symptoms for many people.
  • Talk to your doctor about medical options. Some antidepressants can be very effective for PMDD, sometimes taken only in the premenstrual weeks, and hormonal treatments are another possibility.

Common Questions About Premenstrual Mood Changes and PMDD

What is the difference between PMS and PMDD?

PMS involves mild to moderate emotional and physical symptoms before a period that are usually manageable. PMDD involves severe mood symptoms, such as intense irritability, hopelessness, or anxiety, that significantly disrupt daily life and relationships during the premenstrual phase.

How do I know if my mood changes are linked to my cycle?

The best way is to track your mood daily for at least two menstrual cycles. If symptoms consistently appear in the week or two before your period and ease soon after it starts, the pattern is likely cycle-related.

Is PMDD caused by abnormal hormone levels?

Research suggests people with PMDD usually have normal hormone levels. Instead, their brains appear to be unusually sensitive to the normal monthly rise and fall of reproductive hormones, which triggers severe mood symptoms.

Can PMDD cause thoughts of suicide?

PMDD can bring intense hopelessness, and some people experience suicidal thoughts during the premenstrual phase. This is serious and deserves support. In Canada, you can call or text 9-8-8 at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Can depression or anxiety get worse before my period?

Yes. This is called premenstrual exacerbation. Existing conditions such as depression, anxiety, or ADHD can intensify before a period. Unlike PMDD, symptoms are present throughout the month and simply worsen premenstrually.

Can perimenopause make premenstrual mood changes worse?

Yes. During perimenopause, hormone levels fluctuate more unpredictably, which can intensify premenstrual mood symptoms or bring new mood changes. A doctor can help explore treatment options for this stage.

What treatments help with PMDD?

Common options include certain antidepressants, sometimes taken only during the premenstrual weeks, hormonal treatments, and cognitive behavioural therapy. Lifestyle changes like regular exercise and good sleep can also help. A doctor can advise on medical options.

How can I explain PMDD to my partner?

It can help to describe PMDD as a real, recognized condition where your brain reacts strongly to normal hormone changes. Sharing your tracking and agreeing on a plan for difficult days can reduce conflict and help your partner support you.

Is PMDD related to bipolar disorder?

They are different conditions. PMDD mood changes follow the menstrual cycle and ease soon after the period starts. Bipolar disorder involves mood episodes, including periods of elevated mood, that are not tied to the cycle. Tracking helps clarify which pattern fits.

Can therapy help with premenstrual mood crashes?

Yes. Therapy, particularly cognitive behavioural approaches, can help you recognize early signs, manage intense emotions, reduce self-criticism, and plan around difficult days. It often works best alongside medical care for PMDD.
