Disorganized Attachment Explained

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Disorganized attachment, sometimes called fearful-avoidant attachment in adults, is a pattern in which a person both longs for closeness and feels frightened of it. In relationships, this can create a push and pull: reaching for connection, then withdrawing or reacting strongly when intimacy feels unsafe. The pattern often develops in childhood when the people who were supposed to provide comfort were also, at times, a source of fear, confusion, or unpredictability. Disorganized attachment is not a diagnosis or a fixed trait. With understanding and support, people can develop more secure ways of relating.

What Disorganized Attachment Looks Like

  • Strongly wanting a relationship, then feeling overwhelmed or trapped when it becomes close.
  • Switching between clinging to a partner and pushing them away.
  • Difficulty trusting others, even when they have been reliable.
  • Intense emotional reactions in conflict, sometimes followed by shutting down.
  • Expecting to be hurt or abandoned, while also fearing being engulfed.
  • Feeling confused about what you want from relationships.
  • Being drawn to chaotic or unpredictable relationships because they feel familiar.

People with this pattern often describe feeling as if they have two competing sets of instincts. One says to move closer and the other says to get away. Partners may experience them as hot and cold, which can be confusing for everyone involved.

Why It Happens

Attachment research suggests that infants and children rely on caregivers for safety. When a caregiver is frightening, frightened, or unpredictable, a child faces an impossible problem: the person they need for comfort is also the source of distress. This can happen in homes affected by abuse, neglect, domestic violence, addiction, or a parent's untreated mental illness or unresolved trauma. It can also occur when a caregiver is loving but at times overwhelmed and frightening. Without a consistent strategy for seeking comfort, the child's attachment system becomes disorganized.

These early experiences can shape the nervous system and beliefs about relationships. In adulthood, closeness may trigger both a longing for care and an alarm that care will lead to harm. Disorganized attachment is also more common among people with trauma histories, and it can overlap with generational trauma when patterns pass through families.

What It Is Not

  • Not a personality disorder. Attachment styles describe relationship patterns, not diagnoses.
  • Not a life sentence. Attachment patterns can shift through healthy relationships and therapy, a process sometimes called earned security.
  • Not the same as anxious attachment or avoidant attachment. Disorganized attachment tends to combine elements of both.
  • Not your fault. These patterns formed as ways of surviving difficult early environments.
  • Not something online quizzes can confirm. Attachment is complex, and a clinician can offer a more careful understanding.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

A therapist may explore relationship history, childhood experiences, and how you respond to closeness and conflict. Trauma-informed therapy is often central, because disorganized attachment frequently has roots in early trauma. Helpful approaches include attachment-focused therapy, emotionally focused therapy for couples, schema therapy, and trauma therapies such as EMDR. Skills for emotional regulation and recognizing when your nervous system feels threatened can reduce reactivity. The therapeutic relationship itself can become a place to practise trust and safe connection. Over time, many people learn to stay present in closeness without being overwhelmed.

Partners can support the process by offering predictable, calm responses and by learning about attachment together. Couples therapy can help both people understand the cycle rather than blaming each other for it.

Common Questions About Disorganized Attachment

What is disorganized attachment?

Disorganized attachment is a pattern of both seeking and fearing closeness, often linked with early caregiving that was a source of both comfort and fear.

Is disorganized attachment the same as fearful-avoidant attachment?

The terms are often used together. Disorganized describes the childhood pattern, while fearful-avoidant is commonly used for adults.

What causes disorganized attachment in childhood?

It often develops when caregivers are frightening, frightened, or unpredictable, for example in homes affected by abuse, addiction, or unresolved trauma.

Why do I want love but pull away when I get it?

For people with disorganized attachment, closeness can activate both longing and fear, leading to a push and pull in relationships.

Can disorganized attachment change?

Yes. Therapy and healthy relationships can help people develop earned security and more stable ways of relating.

How does disorganized attachment affect romantic relationships?

It can lead to hot and cold behaviour, intense conflict, difficulty trusting, and fear of both abandonment and being overwhelmed.

Is disorganized attachment linked with trauma?

Yes. It is more common among people who experienced childhood trauma, abuse, or chaotic caregiving.

What therapy helps with a fearful-avoidant pattern?

Trauma-informed, attachment-focused, schema, and emotionally focused therapies can help, along with emotional regulation skills.

How can a partner support someone with disorganized attachment?

Consistency, calm responses, patience, and open conversations about triggers can help. Couples therapy supports both partners.

Does having disorganized attachment mean I will repeat my parents' patterns?

Not necessarily. Awareness and support make it possible to break intergenerational cycles and parent differently.
