What Is Social Anxiety Disorder

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Social anxiety disorder is an anxiety disorder involving intense, persistent fear of social or performance situations in which a person might be judged, embarrassed, or rejected. It goes beyond shyness or ordinary nervousness. People with social anxiety disorder often avoid situations they fear or endure them with significant distress, and the anxiety interferes with work, school, relationships, or daily life. It is one of the most common anxiety disorders, often beginning in adolescence, and it is highly treatable.

What Social Anxiety Disorder Looks Like

  • Fear of speaking in meetings, public speaking, or being the centre of attention.
  • Anxiety about eating, drinking, or writing in front of others.
  • Dread of meeting new people, parties, or dating.
  • Worry about blushing, sweating, or trembling, as described on the page about sweaty palms before talking to people.
  • Feeling judged constantly, even in ordinary situations.
  • Avoiding phone calls, eye contact, or asking questions.
  • Replaying conversations afterward and focusing on what went wrong.

Some people have social anxiety mainly around performance, such as public speaking. Others experience it across most social situations. At work, it can limit career growth. In relationships, it can make it hard to form friendships or share opinions.

Why It Happens

Social anxiety disorder develops from a mix of genetic temperament, such as behavioural inhibition in childhood, and experiences such as bullying, criticism, rejection, or humiliation. Parenting that is overprotective or critical can play a role. Once established, several processes maintain it. People focus inward on how they appear, which makes them feel more anxious and less able to notice others' real reactions. They use safety behaviours, such as rehearsing or avoiding eye contact, which prevent learning that feared outcomes are unlikely. Avoidance brings relief, reinforcing the fear, and post-event rumination strengthens negative beliefs.

Social anxiety often co-occurs with depression, other anxiety disorders, and alcohol use, since some people drink to cope with social situations. These overlapping problems are worth addressing together.

Many people with social anxiety are warm, funny, and interested in others, yet this is hidden behind self-protection. They may be seen as aloof or uninterested when they are actually anxious. Recognizing that others usually notice much less than an anxious mind expects, a phenomenon known as the spotlight effect, is often an early relief in treatment.

What It Is Not

  • Not shyness. Shyness is a common trait. Social anxiety disorder involves intense fear and impairment.
  • Not introversion. Introverts may prefer quiet settings but do not necessarily fear judgment.
  • Not autism, though they can look alike. Autistic social differences stem from differences in communication and processing, while social anxiety stems from fear of judgment. Both can coexist.
  • Not avoidant personality disorder alone. The two overlap, but avoidant personality disorder is more pervasive, as explored on the page about avoiding people despite wanting connection.
  • Not agoraphobia. Agoraphobia centres on fear of panic in places hard to escape, not fear of judgment.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A psychologist assesses social anxiety through interviews and questionnaires, exploring feared situations, avoidance, safety behaviours, and impact. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the leading treatment. It helps people shift attention outward, test anxious predictions through behavioural experiments, drop safety behaviours, and gradually face feared situations. Group therapy can be especially effective for social anxiety. Medication prescribed by a physician may help some people. Many people see significant improvement within a few months of treatment.

Common Questions About Social Anxiety Disorder

How is social anxiety disorder different from shyness?

Shyness is a common personality trait. Social anxiety disorder involves intense, persistent fear of judgment that leads to avoidance or significant distress and affects daily life.

What are common triggers for social anxiety?

Common triggers include public speaking, meeting new people, eating in front of others, speaking in meetings, phone calls, and being the centre of attention.

When does social anxiety disorder usually begin?

It often begins in childhood or adolescence, though some people develop it later, sometimes after a humiliating experience.

What keeps social anxiety going?

Self-focused attention, safety behaviours, avoidance, and rumination after social events all maintain social anxiety by preventing new learning.

Can social anxiety disorder be mistaken for autism?

They can look similar, but autistic social differences come from communication and processing differences, while social anxiety comes from fear of judgment. Both can coexist.

Why do people with social anxiety drink before social events?

Alcohol can temporarily reduce anxiety, which makes it tempting, but relying on it can increase anxiety over time and lead to problematic drinking.

Is group therapy good for social anxiety?

Yes. Group CBT for social anxiety can be very effective, offering real social practice in a supportive setting.

What is performance-only social anxiety?

Some people experience social anxiety mainly around performance situations, such as public speaking, while feeling comfortable in everyday social interactions.

How does CBT treat social anxiety disorder?

CBT helps people shift attention outward, test anxious predictions, drop safety behaviours, and gradually face feared situations to build confidence.

Can people fully overcome social anxiety disorder?

Many people improve substantially with treatment, and some become largely free of social anxiety, though occasional nervousness is normal.
