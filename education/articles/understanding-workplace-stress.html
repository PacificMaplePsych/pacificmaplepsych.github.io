Understanding Workplace Stress

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Workplace stress is the physical and emotional strain that arises when job demands exceed a person's resources or control. Some pressure at work is normal and can even be motivating. Stress becomes harmful when it is intense, ongoing, or combined with little support or influence over how work is done. Common sources include heavy workloads, unclear expectations, conflict, job insecurity, long hours, and poor leadership. Workplace stress affects mental and physical health and, if prolonged, can lead to burnout. Understanding its sources helps both individuals and organizations reduce it.

What Workplace Stress Looks Like

  • Feeling tense, irritable, or anxious about work, including on days off.
  • Dreading going to work or Sunday evenings.
  • Difficulty switching off after work.
  • Trouble sleeping, headaches, or stomach upset.
  • Reduced concentration and more mistakes.
  • Withdrawing from colleagues, or conflict with them.
  • Relying on caffeine, alcohol, or other substances to cope.

Stress at work often spills into home life, affecting patience with family, sleep, and energy for relationships and hobbies.

Why It Happens

Research on work stress highlights several key factors. High demands combined with low control, meaning a lot to do with little say in how, is especially stressful. An imbalance between effort and reward, including pay, recognition, and security, increases strain. Other contributors include unclear roles, conflicting demands, poor communication, bullying or harassment, lack of support from managers or colleagues, unfair treatment, and constant connectivity through email and messaging. Organizational changes, such as restructuring or layoffs, create uncertainty. Personal factors, such as perfectionism or difficulty setting boundaries, can add to stress but rarely explain it alone.

Different jobs carry different stressors. Helping professions involve emotional demands. Shift work disrupts sleep. Precarious or gig work brings insecurity. Recognizing which factors apply to you helps target change.

Workplace stress also tends to build gradually. A new project, a staffing gap, or a difficult manager may seem temporary at first, so people push through. When the pressure never lifts, the extra effort becomes the new normal. Warning signs that stress is becoming harmful include feeling tired even after rest, losing interest in work that once felt meaningful, and noticing that small tasks now feel overwhelming. Catching these signs early makes it easier to change course.

What It Is Not

  • Not a personal weakness. Workplace stress is often a reasonable response to difficult conditions.
  • Not the same as burnout, though it can lead there. Burnout involves exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness after prolonged stress.
  • Not always solved by individual coping. Changes to workload, roles, or culture may be needed.
  • Not anxiety or depression, necessarily. However, ongoing work stress can contribute to both, and they may need treatment.
  • Not something to accept if it involves harassment. Bullying and harassment should be addressed through workplace and legal protections.

How It Is Assessed and Addressed

A psychologist can help identify sources of work stress, assess related anxiety, depression, or burnout, and build strategies. Helpful approaches include clarifying priorities, discussing workload with managers, setting boundaries around hours and communication, taking breaks, protecting sleep and exercise, and building supportive relationships at work. Cognitive behavioural and mindfulness-based approaches can help manage stress responses. When stress stems from organizational problems, employee assistance programs, human resources, or workplace health and safety resources may help. Common Questions About Workplace Stress

What are the most common causes of workplace stress?

Common causes include heavy workloads, lack of control, unclear roles, conflict, poor support, job insecurity, long hours, and constant connectivity.

Why is having little control at work so stressful?

High demands combined with little say over how work is done is one of the strongest predictors of work stress and related health problems.

How does workplace stress affect physical health?

Ongoing work stress can contribute to sleep problems, headaches, digestive issues, muscle tension, and over time, cardiovascular and other health risks.

How is work stress different from burnout?

Work stress involves feeling pressured and overloaded. Burnout develops after prolonged stress and includes exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness.

Should I talk to my manager about work stress?

Often it helps. You can focus on workload, priorities, or resources without sharing personal health details. A clear, solution-focused conversation tends to work best.

What is an employee assistance program?

An employee assistance program is a workplace benefit that offers confidential counselling and support services, often at no cost to employees.

Can workplace bullying cause mental health problems?

Yes. Bullying and harassment are linked with anxiety, depression, and trauma responses. They should be addressed through workplace and legal channels.

How can I set boundaries around work email after hours?

Turn off notifications, remove work email from your phone, set clear availability, and discuss expectations with your team or manager.

Does working from home reduce or increase stress?

It depends. Working from home can reduce commuting stress but blur boundaries and increase isolation. Clear routines help.

When should I see a professional about work stress?

Consider support if stress affects your sleep, health, mood, or relationships, or if you notice signs of burnout, anxiety, or depression.
