Understanding Problematic Technology and Social Media Use

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Technology is part of daily life, connecting people, supporting work, and offering entertainment. For some, however, use of smartphones, social media, video games, streaming, or other online activities becomes hard to control and starts to cause problems. Problematic technology use describes patterns where time online crowds out sleep, work, relationships, or activities that matter, and where cutting back feels difficult. Gaming disorder is recognized as a diagnosis in the World Health Organization's ICD-11, while broader problematic internet and social media use is widely studied but not a formal diagnosis. Understanding these patterns can help people build healthier habits.

What Problematic Technology Use Looks Like

  • Scrolling for much longer than intended.
  • Checking the phone automatically, even during conversations or meals.
  • Staying up late online and feeling tired the next day.
  • Feeling anxious, restless, or irritable without access to a device.
  • Using technology to escape stress, boredom, or difficult feelings.
  • Neglecting work, study, relationships, or hobbies.
  • Failed attempts to cut back.
  • Feeling worse after social media use, through comparison or conflict.
  • Spending money on games, apps, or online shopping, which can overlap with compulsive buying.

Why It Happens

Many apps and games are designed to capture attention, using features such as infinite scroll, notifications, likes, streaks, and unpredictable rewards. These features tap into the brain's reward system, which responds strongly to novelty and uncertainty. Unpredictable rewards, similar to those in gambling, can be especially compelling, which is why some patterns resemble gambling disorder. Over time, checking becomes habitual and automatic.

Technology can also become a way of coping. People may reach for their phone to avoid boredom, loneliness, anxiety, or low mood, much like other forms of using something to cope. This brings short-term relief but can make the underlying feelings harder to face. People with ADHD, anxiety, depression, or social anxiety may be more vulnerable. Social media can also affect self-esteem through comparison and exposure to curated images.

Young people may be particularly affected, as their brains are still developing self-regulation and they may face intense social pressures online. At the same time, technology offers real benefits, such as connection, learning, and community, especially for people who feel isolated offline. The goal is not to eliminate technology but to use it in ways that support wellbeing.

Families often struggle with conflict over screen time. Rules that are clear, consistent, and applied to everyone in the household, including adults, tend to work better than sudden bans. Talking openly about how different apps make each person feel can help families make shared decisions.

What It Is Not

  • Not all screen use. Much technology use is healthy and meaningful.
  • Not a lack of willpower. Many platforms are designed to be hard to put down.
  • Not always a formal diagnosis. Gaming disorder is recognized, while broader problematic use is described as a pattern.
  • Not solved only by deleting apps. Understanding what technology is helping you avoid is often key.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Helpful first steps include tracking screen time, noticing triggers, and identifying what needs technology is meeting. Practical changes include turning off non-essential notifications, keeping phones out of the bedroom, setting app limits, and scheduling screen-free times. Replacing some screen time with activities that bring connection, movement, or rest can help. Cognitive behavioural therapy can address habits, triggers, and underlying anxiety or low mood. Motivational interviewing helps people clarify goals for change. For gaming disorder, structured treatment may be needed. Family approaches can help when technology use causes conflict at home.

Common Questions About Problematic Technology Use

What is problematic technology use?

It is technology use that feels hard to control and starts to affect sleep, work, relationships, or wellbeing.

Is social media addiction a real diagnosis?

Broader social media and internet addiction are not formal diagnoses, though gaming disorder is recognized in ICD-11.

Why does my feed feel impossible to put down?

Apps use features like infinite scroll and unpredictable rewards that strongly engage the brain's reward system.

Which signs suggest screen habits are getting out of hand?

Watch for using devices longer than intended, neglecting important activities, feeling restless without it, and failed attempts to cut back.

When does a love of video games become a clinical concern?

When it becomes gaming disorder, which involves impaired control over gaming, prioritizing it over other activities, and continuing despite harm.

Can social media affect my mood?

Yes. Comparison, conflict, and exposure to curated images can lower mood and self-esteem for some people.

Why do I reach for my phone when I feel anxious?

Scrolling can bring quick distraction and relief, which reinforces the habit when difficult feelings arise.

How can I reduce screen time without feeling deprived?

Turn off notifications, set app limits, keep phones out of the bedroom, and plan enjoyable offline activities.

How should families handle screen time conflict?

Clear, consistent rules applied to everyone, including adults, and open conversations tend to work better than sudden bans.

Can therapy help with problematic technology use?

Yes. CBT and motivational approaches can address habits, triggers, and underlying anxiety or low mood.
