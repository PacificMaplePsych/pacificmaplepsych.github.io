What Is Purely Obsessional OCD (Pure O)

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Purely obsessional OCD, often called Pure O, is a popular term for a presentation of obsessive-compulsive disorder in which obsessions are prominent and compulsions are mostly hidden. People with Pure O experience distressing intrusive thoughts, often about harm, sexuality, relationships, morality, or identity, and respond with mental rituals rather than visible behaviours such as washing or checking locks. The name can be misleading, because compulsions are still present. They simply happen inside the mind. Pure O is not a separate diagnosis, but the term has helped many people recognize that their experience is OCD. It responds to the same effective treatments.

What Pure O Looks Like

  • Repeated, distressing intrusive thoughts that feel out of character.
  • Intense fear about what the thoughts mean about you.
  • Mentally reviewing past events to check whether you did something wrong.
  • Analyzing feelings or bodily reactions to test whether a thought is true.
  • Neutralizing a thought by replacing it with a good one or repeating a phrase.
  • Seeking reassurance from others or searching online for answers.
  • Avoiding people, places, or media that trigger thoughts.
  • Spending hours a day caught in these mental loops.

Because the rituals are silent, people with Pure O may appear calm while feeling intensely distressed. Many describe feeling as if they are constantly arguing with their own mind.

Why It Happens

Pure O follows the same cycle as all OCD. An intrusive thought triggers anxiety, guilt, or doubt. A mental compulsion, such as reviewing or reassuring oneself, brings brief relief. That relief teaches the brain that the thought was important and the ritual necessary, which strengthens the cycle. The mental rituals are described in more detail on the page about mental compulsions.

Obsessions in Pure O often target what a person values most, which is why they feel so distressing. A loving parent may fear harming their child, or a committed partner may doubt their relationship. The page on intrusive thoughts and OCD explains why these thoughts do not reflect hidden desires. As with other forms of OCD, genetics, brain differences, and stress contribute.

People with Pure O often go years without diagnosis. They may not realize that their mental rituals count as compulsions, or they may be too frightened or ashamed to describe their thoughts. Some worry that a clinician will misunderstand. Clinicians experienced in OCD recognize these themes and understand that the thoughts are symptoms, not intentions.

What It Is Not

  • Not OCD without compulsions. The compulsions are mental and hidden, but they are there.
  • Not a sign that your thoughts are true. Obsessions reflect anxiety, not desire or character.
  • Not a separate condition. It is a presentation of OCD, not its own diagnosis.
  • Not ordinary worry. The thoughts are ego-dystonic, meaning they feel alien, and they drive rituals.
  • Not untreatable. Pure O responds to the same therapy as other OCD.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician experienced in OCD will ask about obsessions, all forms of compulsions including mental ones, avoidance, and reassurance-seeking. Identifying hidden rituals is a key part of assessment. Exposure and response prevention is the most effective treatment. For Pure O, exposure might involve writing scripts about feared thoughts, listening to recordings, or deliberately approaching triggers, while practising response prevention by not reviewing, analyzing, or seeking reassurance. Learning to allow uncertainty is central. Acceptance and commitment therapy and inference-based cognitive behavioural therapy can also help. Medication may reduce symptoms for some people.

Common Questions About Pure O

Where does the nickname Pure O come from?

It is short for purely obsessional, a popular term for OCD where obsessions are prominent and compulsions are mainly mental, rather than visible behaviours.

Does Pure O have compulsions?

Yes. Compulsions are present but happen in the mind, such as reviewing, analyzing, neutralizing, or self-reassurance.

Is Pure O a separate diagnosis?

No. It is a presentation of OCD, not a distinct condition, and it is treated the same way.

What kinds of thoughts are common in Pure O?

Common themes include harm, sexuality, relationships, morality, religion, and identity, often targeting what a person values most.

Why do my thoughts feel so real and frightening?

OCD makes thoughts feel significant and dangerous, creating intense anxiety, even though the thoughts do not reflect your desires.

What does ego-dystonic mean?

Ego-dystonic describes thoughts that feel alien and go against your values, which is characteristic of OCD obsessions.

How is exposure therapy done for Pure O?

Exposure may involve scripts, recordings, or approaching triggers while resisting mental rituals and allowing uncertainty.

Why does checking my feelings to be sure never settle the doubt?

Checking is a mental compulsion. It brings brief relief but teaches the brain that the doubt matters, so it returns stronger.

Why did it take so long for me to realize I had OCD?

Many people with Pure O do not recognize mental rituals as compulsions or feel too ashamed to describe their thoughts.

What is inference-based CBT?

Inference-based cognitive behavioural therapy focuses on how obsessional doubts arise from faulty reasoning and helps people trust their senses and judgment.
