How Couples Therapy Works

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Couples therapy is a form of therapy in which two partners meet together with a trained therapist to understand their relationship patterns, communicate more effectively, resolve conflict, and rebuild closeness. The therapist does not take sides or decide who is right. Instead, they treat the relationship itself as the focus, helping each partner feel heard and helping both see the cycles that keep them stuck. Couples come to therapy for many reasons, including frequent arguments, emotional distance, infidelity, life transitions, or wanting to strengthen a good relationship. Research suggests couples therapy helps many couples improve satisfaction and communication.

How Couples Therapy Works

Most couples therapy is built on the idea that problems arise from patterns between partners, not from one person alone. A common pattern is the cycle described on the page about the pursue-withdraw pattern, where one partner pushes to connect and the other pulls away. Each reaction makes sense but fuels the other. Therapy helps couples see these cycles, understand the feelings and needs beneath them, and respond differently.

Couples therapists draw on several evidence-based approaches:

  • Emotionally focused couples therapy focuses on attachment needs and helps partners share vulnerable feelings, as explained on the page about emotion-focused therapy.
  • The Gottman Method uses research on couples to build friendship, manage conflict, and create shared meaning, as described on the page about the Gottman Method.
  • Integrative behavioural couple therapy combines acceptance of differences with practical behaviour change.
  • Cognitive behavioural couples therapy focuses on communication skills, problem-solving, and unhelpful thought patterns.

Many therapists blend approaches to fit each couple.

What Sessions Are Like

Couples therapy usually begins with an assessment. The therapist meets with both partners to hear the story of the relationship, what brings them in, and what they hope will change. Many therapists also meet with each partner individually once, which helps them understand each person's perspective and check for safety concerns. Questionnaires about the relationship may be used.

Later sessions are usually joint. The therapist may slow down difficult conversations, help each partner express feelings without blame, point out patterns as they happen, and coach new ways of communicating. Couples often practise skills in the session, such as listening and reflecting, and try exercises at home. Sessions can be emotional, and it is normal for things to feel harder briefly before they improve.

The length of couples therapy varies. Some couples attend for a few months, while others with longer-standing difficulties or betrayals continue longer. Sessions may be weekly at first, then less frequent. Premarital work is usually shorter, as described on the page about premarital counselling.

Who It Can Help

Couples therapy can help partners who argue often, feel distant, struggle with communication, are recovering from infidelity, are navigating big changes such as a new baby or retirement, or are considering whether to stay together. It can help couples of any age and relationship stage, whether married or not. It also helps when one partner's mental health, such as depression, anxiety, or ADHD, is affecting the relationship, often alongside individual therapy.

Couples therapy is not recommended when there is ongoing abuse, coercive control, or fear of a partner. In these situations, joint sessions can be unsafe, and individual support and safety planning come first. Resources are available on the page about abuse resources. Couples therapy also works best when both partners are willing to take part, though one partner can begin with individual therapy if the other is not ready.

Common Questions About Couples Therapy

What actually happens in a couples therapy session?

In couples therapy, the therapist helps partners discuss difficult topics, points out patterns as they happen, and coaches new ways of communicating.

Will a couples therapist take sides?

No. A couples therapist focuses on the relationship and helps both partners feel heard rather than deciding who is right.

Why does a couples therapist sometimes meet each partner alone?

Individual meetings help the couples therapist understand each perspective and check for safety concerns before joint work continues.

How many months do most couples stay in couples therapy?

Couples therapy often lasts a few months, though longer-standing difficulties or betrayals may need more time.

Which approaches do couples therapists use?

Common couples therapy approaches include emotionally focused therapy, the Gottman Method, integrative behavioural couple therapy, and CBT for couples.

Is couples therapy worth it if we are thinking about separating?

Yes. Couples therapy can help partners clarify whether to stay together and, if they separate, do so with less conflict.

What if my partner will not come to couples therapy?

You can start with individual therapy to work on your own patterns, and your partner may choose to join couples sessions later.

When is couples therapy not safe?

Couples therapy is not recommended when there is ongoing abuse, coercive control, or fear of a partner. Individual support comes first.

Is it normal for couples therapy to feel harder before it helps?

Yes. Couples therapy can bring difficult feelings to the surface briefly before communication and closeness improve.

My partner is depressed and it is straining our marriage. Should we try couples therapy?

It can help. Couples therapy can help partners understand how depression affects the relationship, often alongside individual treatment for the depressed partner.
