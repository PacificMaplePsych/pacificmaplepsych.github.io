Building a Values-Based Life After Loss

Building a values-based life after loss means gradually reconnecting with what matters most to you, such as love, kindness, creativity, family, learning, or faith, and taking small actions guided by those values, even while grief remains. After the death of someone close, or another major loss, life can lose its shape and sense of purpose. Many people feel they are simply getting through the days. Values do not replace the person or undo the loss. They offer a compass for moving forward in ways that feel meaningful, and often in ways that honour the person who died. This approach draws on acceptance and commitment therapy and meaning-centred grief work.

Why Values Help After Loss

Grief can make everything feel pointless. Routines built around the person may have disappeared, and goals may no longer make sense. People sometimes wait until grief lifts before trying to rebuild their lives, but grief rarely leaves completely, as explained on the page about grief and its stages. Waiting can mean long periods of emptiness.

Values are different from goals. A goal can be completed, while a value is a direction you keep moving in, like heading west. Acting on values provides meaning even when feelings are painful. Acceptance and commitment therapy teaches that people can make room for grief while still choosing actions that matter. Many grieving people find that their values became clearer through the loss. For example, the death of a parent might deepen a value of family, or a sudden loss might sharpen a value of living fully.

Strategies to Try

1. Notice What Matters

Reflect on questions such as: What did I love about the person I lost? What did they bring out in me? What kind of person do I want to be in this chapter? What matters most in my relationships, work, health, and community?

2. Name a Few Core Values

Choose three to five values that feel important now, such as connection, compassion, courage, creativity, or service.

3. Choose One Small Action

Pick a small action for one value this week. If connection matters, call a friend. If creativity matters, spend twenty minutes on a project. Small, consistent actions build momentum.

4. Honour the Person Through Values

Many people find meaning in carrying forward what mattered to the person who died, such as volunteering for a cause they cared about, cooking their recipes, or living out qualities they admired.

5. Make Room for Grief

Acting on values does not mean pushing grief away. Expect sadness to come along. Both can be present at once.

6. Revisit and Adjust

Values may shift over time. Check in every few weeks about what feels meaningful now.

7. Reconnect With Community

Values often come alive through relationships and shared activities. Grief groups, faith communities, and volunteer work can offer both support and purpose.

Common Obstacles

Early in grief, it may feel impossible or wrong to think about the future. There is no rush. Values work is often most helpful once the initial shock has eased, and it can be done in very small steps. Some people feel guilty for finding meaning or enjoyment after a loss, as if it betrays the person. Many find it helps to see meaningful living as a way of honouring them. Others may realize that important values, such as a shared plan, are no longer possible. Grieving these lost hopes is part of the process.

When grief stays intense and disabling for a long time, it may be prolonged grief, which responds to specialized treatment, as described on the page about treatment approaches for prolonged grief. Low energy and depression can also make action hard. Starting with tiny steps and seeking support helps.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If you feel stuck, empty, or hopeless for a long time after a loss, if you cannot find any sense of purpose, or if you have thoughts of suicide, support can help. Grief counselling, described on the page about grief counselling, and values-based therapy can help you process the loss and rebuild meaning. If you have thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About Values and Life After Loss

How can values help me rebuild my life after a loss?

Values offer a direction for meaningful action after a loss, even while grief remains, so life can take shape again without waiting for grief to end.

What is the difference between values and goals after a loss?

A goal can be completed, while a value is an ongoing direction. After a loss, values can guide you even when old goals no longer fit.

Is it too soon to think about values when I am newly grieving?

Values work is often most helpful once the initial shock of a loss has eased, and there is no rush to begin.

How can I honour someone who died through the way I live?

Many grieving people carry forward what mattered to the person who died, such as their causes, recipes, or qualities they admired.

Why do I feel guilty when I find meaning after a loss?

Guilt after a loss is common. Many people come to see meaningful living as a way of honouring the person rather than betraying them.

What questions help me find my values after a death?

Ask what you loved about the person, what they brought out in you, and what kind of person you want to be as you grieve.

Why would ACT ask me to make room for grief instead of trying to feel better?

Struggling against grief often adds suffering. ACT helps grieving people make room for painful feelings while choosing actions guided by their values.

How do I start living by my values when grief leaves me exhausted?

Choose one very small action for one value each week, since tiny steps are more manageable when grief drains energy.

What if the life I planned is no longer possible after my loss?

Grieving lost hopes and plans is part of loss. Values can help you find new directions that still feel meaningful.

When should I get help if life still feels empty long after a loss?

If emptiness or hopelessness lasts a long time after a loss, grief counselling or values-based therapy can help.
