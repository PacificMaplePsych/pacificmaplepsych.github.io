Why Can't I Focus at Work Anymore

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you used to concentrate well at work and now find it hard, the change usually points to something that has shifted, such as prolonged stress, burnout, poor sleep, low mood, or changes in the job itself. A new loss of focus is different from lifelong attention difficulties, and the fact that it has a clear before and after is an important clue. Understanding what changed is the first step toward getting your concentration back.

People describe reading the same email several times, drifting during meetings, taking far longer to finish tasks they once handled easily, and ending the day feeling tired without having achieved much.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Sleep. Even modest ongoing sleep loss affects attention, memory, and decision-making. Poor sleep can come from stress, a new baby, shift work, or a sleep disorder.

Depression. Difficulty concentrating is a core symptom of depression, alongside low mood, loss of interest, and fatigue. It can show up at work before someone recognizes feeling depressed.

Anxiety. Worry uses up mental capacity. If your mind is busy with problems at home or work, there is less left for the task in front of you.

Life events. Grief, a divorce, caring for a sick relative, or a health problem can all pull attention away, even when you try to keep work separate.

Medical factors. Thyroid changes, anemia, perimenopause, medication side effects, and long recovery from illness can all affect concentration. If the change was sudden or comes with physical symptoms, check with a doctor.

ADHD. Attention difficulties from ADHD are usually lifelong rather than new. Sometimes, though, increased demands overwhelm strategies that used to work. The page on focus problems that do not respond to effort explains how to tell the difference.

When It Looks More Like Burnout

Burnout is a state of exhaustion that develops after long periods of work stress without enough recovery. It often affects concentration because the brain is running on depleted resources. Signs that burnout may be behind your loss of focus include:

  • Feeling exhausted all the time, even after rest.
  • Growing cynicism about your job or detachment from work you used to care about.
  • A sense that you are less effective, no matter how hard you try.
  • Dreading work, or feeling a heavy weight on Sunday evenings.
  • More frequent illness, headaches, or tension.
  • Difficulty switching off from work in the evening.

Burnout is closely tied to working conditions, such as heavy workload, lack of control, unclear expectations, or values that clash with what the job requires. That means personal strategies help but are rarely enough alone. Recovery often involves changing some aspect of the work itself, along with rest. Burnout and depression can also overlap, so if low mood extends well beyond work, it is worth exploring both.

What Can Help

  • Protect sleep first. It is often the fastest way to improve focus.
  • Work in shorter blocks. Twenty-five to forty-five minutes of focused work followed by a short break suits a tired brain better than long stretches.
  • Reduce interruptions. Turn off notifications, close extra tabs, and block time for demanding tasks when your energy is highest.
  • Write things down. Lists and notes reduce the load on working memory.
  • Look at the workload honestly. If demands have grown, talking with a manager about priorities may help more than trying harder.
  • Take real breaks. Time away from screens, a short walk, or lunch away from your desk helps restore attention.

Common Questions About Losing Focus at Work

Why did I suddenly lose my ability to concentrate at work?

A new loss of focus usually reflects a change, such as ongoing stress, burnout, poor sleep, depression, a life event, or a medical factor. Looking at when it began often reveals the cause.

Can burnout make it impossible to concentrate?

Burnout often affects concentration because the brain is running on depleted resources. Many people with burnout describe foggy thinking, slower work, and difficulty staying on task.

How do I know if losing focus at work is depression or burnout?

Burnout is mainly tied to work and often improves with time away. Depression tends to affect all areas of life, including hobbies and relationships. The two frequently overlap, so a professional can help sort them out.

Could adult ADHD explain focus problems that only started recently?

ADHD symptoms begin in childhood, so a truly new problem usually has another cause. However, rising demands can expose long-standing ADHD that earlier strategies used to manage.

Can perimenopause affect concentration at work?

Yes. Hormonal changes, disrupted sleep, and hot flashes during perimenopause can all affect focus and memory. A doctor can help assess whether this is playing a role.

Why is my focus worst in the afternoon?

Energy naturally dips after lunch, and fatigue from the morning adds up. Poor sleep, heavy meals, or a lack of breaks can make the afternoon slump stronger.

Does working from home make it harder to concentrate?

It can for some people, because of household distractions, blurred boundaries, and less structure. Others focus better at home. A dedicated workspace and clear routine usually help.

Should I tell my manager I am struggling to focus?

It depends on your workplace. Many managers can help with priorities, deadlines, or workload. You do not need to share health details to ask for support with how work is organized.

What quick changes can improve focus at work?

Working in short focused blocks, turning off notifications, tackling hard tasks at your best time of day, writing things down, and taking real breaks can all help.

When should I get help for ongoing concentration problems at work?

Consider support if focus has not improved after addressing sleep and workload, if it affects your performance, or if it comes with exhaustion, low mood, or anxiety.
