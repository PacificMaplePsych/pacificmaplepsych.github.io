Why Can't I Stop Washing My Hands

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You wash your hands, then feel like they are still not clean. You wash again, maybe several times, until it feels right. Touching door handles, money, or shared surfaces makes you uneasy, and you might avoid certain places or objects altogether. Your hands may be dry, cracked, or sore from all the washing, yet the urge keeps coming back.

Handwashing is a healthy habit, and some jobs require frequent washing. When washing becomes repetitive, time-consuming, and driven by distress, it may be something more that deserves attention.

If your hands are cracked, bleeding, or showing signs of infection, such as redness, swelling, or warmth, see a doctor. Skin damage from frequent washing can need medical care.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Work or health requirements. Healthcare, food service, and childcare workers, or people caring for someone with a weakened immune system, may need to wash often.

Recent illness exposure. After an outbreak or illness in the household, it is natural to be more cautious for a while.

Health anxiety. Worry about getting sick can lead to frequent washing, along with searching symptoms online.

Sensory discomfort. Some people, including some autistic people, find sticky or dirty sensations on their hands very uncomfortable and wash to remove them.

Fear of vomiting. People with a fear of throwing up often wash frequently to avoid stomach bugs.

When It Looks More Like OCD

Contamination OCD is one of the most common forms of obsessive-compulsive disorder. It involves intrusive fears about germs, dirt, illness, or chemicals, and compulsions like washing or cleaning to reduce the anxiety. Some people fear spreading contamination to others rather than getting sick themselves.

Signs that OCD may be involved:

  • Washing repeatedly, or in a specific way, until it feels right.
  • Feeling contaminated even after washing.
  • Washing taking up more than an hour a day or causing skin damage.
  • Avoiding places, people, or objects because of contamination fears.
  • Asking others to wash or change clothes before contact.
  • Recognizing the washing is excessive but feeling unable to stop.

OCD can also involve repeated checking, needing things to feel just right, or unwanted thoughts that feel frightening. Washing brings short-term relief, but that relief teaches the brain that washing was necessary, which keeps the cycle going.

Contamination fears do not always make logical sense, and people with OCD often know this. Someone might feel fine touching one surface but deeply uneasy touching another that is objectively no dirtier. The fear can also spread over time, so that more places, objects, and people start to feel contaminated. This is one reason early support can be so helpful. OCD often begins in the teens or early adulthood, but it can start at any age, including after a stressful life event or the birth of a baby.

What Can Help

  • Notice the pattern. Track when you wash, what triggered it, and how long it takes.
  • Follow standard guidelines. Public health guidance recommends washing for about 20 seconds at key times, such as after using the bathroom and before eating.
  • Delay the urge. Waiting a few minutes before washing helps show that anxiety fades on its own.
  • Care for your skin. Use gentle soap and moisturizer to protect damaged skin.
  • Reduce reassurance. Asking others whether something is clean can keep the cycle going.
  • Resist avoidance. Gradually touching things you have been avoiding, with support if needed, helps shrink the fear rather than letting it grow.

Common Questions About Excessive Handwashing

Why do I feel the need to wash my hands over and over?

Repeated handwashing can come from health worries, work requirements, sensory discomfort, health anxiety, fear of vomiting, or contamination OCD.

What is contamination OCD?

Contamination OCD involves intrusive fears about germs, dirt, illness, or chemicals, and compulsions like washing or cleaning to reduce anxiety.

How much handwashing is too much?

Washing becomes a concern when it is repetitive, driven by distress, takes up a lot of time, causes skin damage, or interferes with daily life.

Why do my hands still feel dirty after washing?

In contamination OCD, the feeling of being contaminated can persist even after washing, leading to repeated washing until it feels right.

Can excessive handwashing damage my skin?

Yes. Frequent washing can cause dryness, cracking, bleeding, and dermatitis. Gentle soap and moisturizer help, and a doctor can treat severe damage.

Is excessive handwashing always OCD?

No. It can also come from job requirements, health anxiety, sensory discomfort, or fear of vomiting. A clinician can help identify the cause.

How is contamination OCD treated?

Exposure and response prevention is the most effective treatment. It involves gradually facing contamination fears while resisting washing, so anxiety decreases over time.

Can contamination fears get worse after a pandemic?

Yes. Public health messaging during outbreaks can intensify contamination fears for some people, especially those prone to anxiety or OCD.

How can family members help with excessive handwashing?

Family can help by gently not joining in rituals, such as extra cleaning or reassurance, while staying supportive. A therapist can guide this.

Can children develop contamination OCD?

Yes. OCD, including contamination fears, can begin in childhood. Early support from a psychologist can help children and families manage it.
