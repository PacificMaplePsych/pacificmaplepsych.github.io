What Are Cognitive Processing Therapy and Prolonged Exposure

Cognitive processing therapy (CPT) and prolonged exposure (PE) are two structured, trauma-focused therapies with strong research support for post-traumatic stress disorder. Both are recommended in major treatment guidelines, and both help people process traumatic experiences so that memories become less overwhelming and daily life feels safer. They take different routes. CPT focuses mainly on changing the beliefs people form about themselves, others, and the world after trauma. PE focuses mainly on gradually approaching trauma memories and avoided situations so that fear decreases. Many people improve significantly with either approach, usually within a few months of regular sessions.

How They Work

Cognitive Processing Therapy

After trauma, people often develop beliefs that keep them stuck, such as it was my fault, the world is completely dangerous, or I can never trust anyone. CPT calls these stuck points. Therapy helps people identify stuck points and examine them carefully, looking at the evidence and considering more balanced perspectives. CPT focuses on themes commonly affected by trauma, including safety, trust, power and control, esteem, and intimacy. As beliefs shift, feelings such as guilt, shame, and fear often ease.

Prolonged Exposure

After trauma, people naturally avoid reminders because they bring distress. Avoidance brings short-term relief but prevents the brain from learning that the memory is not dangerous in itself and that the present is safe. PE helps people gradually and safely approach what they have been avoiding. This includes revisiting the trauma memory in detail with a therapist, called imaginal exposure, and gradually facing safe situations that have been avoided, called in vivo exposure. Over time, the memory becomes less intense and trauma triggers lose some of their power.

What Sessions Are Like

Cognitive Processing Therapy

CPT usually involves around twelve weekly sessions, though it can be shorter or longer. Sessions include learning about PTSD, identifying stuck points, and completing worksheets that help examine thoughts. Many people write an account of the trauma, though CPT can also be done without a written account. Practice between sessions is an important part of the work.

Prolonged Exposure

PE usually involves around eight to fifteen weekly sessions. Sessions include learning about common trauma reactions, breathing skills, repeatedly recounting the trauma memory with the therapist, and discussing what comes up. Sessions are often recorded so that people can listen between sessions. People also practise gradually facing avoided situations, such as driving again after an accident, starting with easier steps.

In both approaches, therapy is paced carefully. Therapists check in about distress, and safety and readiness come first. It is normal for symptoms to feel temporarily stronger early on before they improve.

Who They Can Help

Both therapies are designed for people with PTSD after many kinds of trauma, including accidents, assault, combat, disasters, and sudden loss. They can also help with related symptoms such as hypervigilance, nightmares, guilt, and avoidance. People with complex trauma histories may benefit from a period of stabilization first, or from adapted versions. Other approaches may fit better for people who are not yet ready to focus on trauma, or who are in ongoing unsafe situations, where safety planning comes first.

How They Compare

CPT may appeal to people who want to focus on thoughts and meaning, especially when guilt, shame, or self-blame are central. PE may appeal to people whose lives are strongly shaped by avoidance and fear. Research shows both are effective, and neither is clearly better for everyone. Personal preference, the nature of the trauma, and the therapist's training all shape the choice. Some therapists integrate elements of both. Common Questions About CPT and Prolonged Exposure

How does CPT help me question the beliefs I formed after trauma?

CPT is a structured PTSD therapy that helps people identify and change unhelpful beliefs, or stuck points, formed after trauma.

Why would facing trauma reminders on purpose make PTSD better instead of worse?

PE is a PTSD therapy that helps people gradually approach trauma memories and avoided situations so that fear decreases.

How many sessions do CPT and PE usually take?

CPT often involves around twelve sessions, and PE around eight to fifteen, though length varies.

What are stuck points in CPT?

Stuck points are beliefs formed after trauma, such as self-blame or feeling unsafe everywhere, that keep PTSD going.

Will my therapist make me describe every detail of what happened in CPT or PE?

PE involves recounting the memory. CPT can be done with or without a written account. Your therapist will discuss options.

Is it normal to feel worse for a while at the start of CPT or prolonged exposure for PTSD?

Some people notice temporary increases in distress early on, which usually ease as therapy progresses.

Is CPT or PE better for PTSD?

Both have strong evidence. The best fit depends on your preferences, the trauma, and what feels manageable.

What does imaginal exposure involve in prolonged exposure therapy?

Imaginal exposure in prolonged exposure therapy means repeatedly recalling the trauma memory aloud with a therapist so it becomes less distressing over time.

Can CPT help with guilt and shame after trauma?

Yes. CPT directly addresses beliefs linked with guilt, shame, and self-blame.

Are these therapies suitable for complex trauma?

They can help, though some people with complex trauma benefit from stabilization first or adapted versions.
