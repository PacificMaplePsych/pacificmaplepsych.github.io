Mood and Anxiety Changes After a Concussion

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you have a worsening headache, repeated vomiting, confusion, slurred speech, weakness, or seizures after a head injury, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.

Mood and anxiety changes are common after a concussion. People may feel more irritable, tearful, anxious, or low than usual, have a shorter fuse, feel overwhelmed by noise or busy places, or simply not feel like themselves. These changes can come from the injury itself, which temporarily affects how the brain regulates emotion and energy, and from the stress of living with headaches, fatigue, poor sleep, and time away from work or school. For most people, symptoms improve over days to weeks. When emotional symptoms last longer, psychological support can help, and treating anxiety and mood often helps overall recovery.

What Emotional Changes Can Look Like

  • Irritability and a short temper. Snapping at loved ones or feeling frustrated by small things.
  • Anxiety. Worry about recovery, health, or returning to activities, sometimes with panic.
  • Low mood. Sadness, discouragement, or losing interest in usual activities.
  • Emotional sensitivity. Crying more easily or feeling emotions more intensely.
  • Feeling overwhelmed. Struggling with noise, screens, crowds, or multitasking.
  • Sleep changes. Trouble falling asleep, waking often, or sleeping much more than usual.

Why a Concussion Affects Mood

Changes in the Brain

A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury that temporarily disrupts how brain cells work and communicate. This can affect energy, concentration, and emotional control, which is why emotions may feel closer to the surface.

The Stress of Symptoms

Headaches, dizziness, fatigue, and trouble concentrating are hard to live with, and they can make work, school, and social life difficult. Worry about whether you will get better can add to anxiety, as described on the page about health anxiety.

The Event That Caused It

If the concussion happened in a car crash, fall, or assault, the event itself may be traumatic. Flashbacks, nightmares, and feeling on edge may point to PTSD after a car crash or another trauma response, which can overlap with concussion symptoms.

Sleep and Activity Changes

Disrupted sleep and reduced activity can lower mood. Fully resting for a long time used to be advised, but current guidance generally favours a short period of rest followed by a gradual return to activity.

What It Is Not

Emotional changes after a concussion are not a sign of weakness, and they do not mean you are making symptoms up. They are also not always permanent. Most people recover within weeks, and many who have longer-lasting symptoms still improve with time and support. Symptoms that persist for several months are sometimes called persistent post-concussion symptoms. Because anxiety, depression, poor sleep, and pain can keep symptoms going, addressing them is an important part of recovery.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

Your physician should be your first stop after a head injury to rule out more serious problems and guide your return to activity. A psychologist can then help with the emotional side of recovery. Support may include:

  • Understanding the symptoms. Learning what is expected after a concussion can reduce fear.
  • Pacing. Planning a gradual return to work, school, screens, and exercise without pushing through or stopping completely.
  • Managing anxiety and mood. Cognitive behavioural strategies for worry, frustration, and low mood.
  • Sleep support. Approaches such as CBT for insomnia.
  • Trauma-focused therapy. If the event that caused the concussion was traumatic.

Sometimes a neuropsychological or psychological assessment is recommended to clarify thinking and memory changes, as described on the page about what a psychological assessment is. Common Questions About Emotional Changes After a Concussion

Is it normal to feel emotional after a concussion?

Yes. Irritability, tearfulness, anxiety, and low mood are common after a concussion and usually improve as the brain recovers.

Why am I so irritable after a concussion?

Irritability after a concussion can come from temporary changes in how the brain regulates emotion, plus fatigue, headaches, and poor sleep.

Can a concussion cause anxiety?

Yes. A concussion can cause anxiety through brain changes and through worry about symptoms, recovery, and returning to normal life.

Is low mood common in the weeks after a concussion?

Yes. Low mood is common after a concussion and can develop into depression, especially if symptoms last or affect work, sleep, and social life.

How long do mood changes last after a concussion?

Mood changes after a concussion often improve within days to weeks, though some people have longer-lasting symptoms that still improve with support.

Should I rest completely after a concussion?

Current guidance generally favours a short period of rest after a concussion, then a gradual return to activity guided by your physician.

Can a car crash cause both a concussion and PTSD?

Yes. A car crash can cause both a concussion and PTSD, and their symptoms, such as poor sleep and irritability, can overlap.

Can therapy help after a concussion?

Yes. Therapy can help with anxiety, low mood, sleep, pacing, and trauma symptoms after a concussion, supporting overall recovery.

When should I see a doctor about emotional changes after a concussion?

See a doctor if emotional changes after a concussion are severe, worsening, or not improving, and seek emergency care for red-flag symptoms.

Does ICBC cover therapy for concussion symptoms after a crash?

ICBC Enhanced Care generally covers psychology treatment for injuries from a crash in BC, which can include emotional symptoms after a concussion.
