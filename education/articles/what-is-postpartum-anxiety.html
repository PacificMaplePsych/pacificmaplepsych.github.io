What Is Postpartum Anxiety

If you are having thoughts of harming yourself or your baby, or thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse any time of day.

Postpartum anxiety is anxiety that develops or worsens after having a baby. It involves persistent, excessive worry, often about the baby's health and safety, along with physical symptoms such as racing heart, tension, and trouble sleeping, even when the baby is asleep. Some worry is a natural part of caring for a newborn. Postpartum anxiety is different because the worry is intense, hard to control, and interferes with rest, enjoyment, and daily functioning. It is one of the most common perinatal mood and anxiety conditions and can occur on its own or alongside postpartum depression. It is treatable, and recognizing it early helps.

What Postpartum Anxiety Looks Like

  • Constant worry about the baby's breathing, feeding, weight, or health.
  • Repeatedly checking on the baby, even when they are sleeping safely.
  • Racing thoughts and feeling anxious all the time since having a baby.
  • Trouble sleeping even when there is an opportunity to rest.
  • Physical symptoms, such as a racing heart, dizziness, nausea, or muscle tension.
  • Feeling on edge, irritable, or unable to relax.
  • Avoiding leaving the baby with others, or avoiding certain activities out of fear.
  • Panic attacks.
  • Intrusive, frightening thoughts about harm coming to the baby, as described on the page about scary intrusive thoughts about your baby.

Why It Happens

Postpartum anxiety arises from a combination of biological, psychological, and social factors. Dramatic hormonal shifts after birth, combined with severe sleep deprivation, affect mood and stress systems. The enormous responsibility of caring for a newborn naturally heightens alertness to danger. A personal or family history of anxiety or depression, a difficult pregnancy or birth, previous pregnancy loss, a baby with health concerns, limited support, and financial or relationship stress all increase risk.

Many new parents feel pressure to be happy and grateful, which can make it hard to admit they are struggling. Some fear that sharing their worries or intrusive thoughts will lead others to think they are unfit parents. In reality, these experiences are common and treatable. Non-birthing parents can also experience perinatal anxiety and depression, as described on the page about paternal postnatal depression.

Postpartum anxiety can begin during pregnancy, shortly after birth, or months later, including around weaning or returning to work. It may look similar to generalized anxiety, but the focus is often on the baby and parenting.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary new-parent worry. Postpartum anxiety is intense, persistent, and interferes with daily life.
  • Not a sign you are a bad parent. Anxiety often reflects how much you care.
  • Not something to wait out if it is affecting you. Treatment can help you feel better sooner.
  • Not the same as postpartum psychosis, a rare emergency involving confusion, hallucinations, or delusions.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A family doctor, midwife, or public health nurse can screen for postpartum anxiety and depression and refer to further support. Cognitive behavioural therapy is effective, helping parents recognize anxious thoughts, reduce checking and reassurance-seeking, and build coping skills. Interpersonal therapy can address role changes and relationship stress. Practical support, such as help with night feeds, protected sleep, and time for rest, reduces vulnerability. Peer support groups for new parents can reduce isolation. Some medications are considered safe during breastfeeding and may be recommended for moderate to severe anxiety.

Common Questions About Postpartum Anxiety

Why am I consumed by worry since my baby arrived?

You may be experiencing postpartum anxiety, which involves persistent, excessive worry and physical tension after having a baby, often focused on the baby's health and safety.

How is postpartum anxiety different from normal new-parent worry?

Postpartum anxiety is intense, hard to control, and interferes with rest, enjoyment, and daily functioning.

Can postpartum anxiety happen without depression?

Yes. It can occur on its own or alongside postpartum depression.

Why is my body wired at night even when the house is quiet?

Anxiety keeps the mind and body on alert, making it hard to rest even when there is a chance to sleep.

Is it normal to keep checking on my baby while they sleep?

Occasional checking is common. Repeated, anxious checking that interferes with rest may signal postpartum anxiety.

Can perinatal anxiety appear months after birth?

Yes. It can start during pregnancy, soon after birth, or months later, including around weaning or returning to work.

Can partners get perinatal anxiety?

Yes. Non-birthing parents can also experience anxiety and depression after a baby arrives.

What should I ask my doctor about medication if I am nursing?

Ask which options are considered compatible with breastfeeding, how your baby would be monitored, and how therapy could be combined with medication.

What treatment helps postpartum anxiety?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, interpersonal therapy, practical support, peer groups, and sometimes medication are effective.

Who can I talk to about postpartum anxiety in British Columbia?

You can speak with your family doctor, midwife, or public health nurse, or call 8-1-1 for advice.
