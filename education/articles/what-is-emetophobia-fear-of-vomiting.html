What Is Emetophobia (Fear of Vomiting)

If fear of vomiting has led you to eat very little, lose weight, or feel faint or dizzy, speak with a doctor promptly. Call 911 in an emergency.

Emetophobia is an intense and persistent fear of vomiting. The fear may focus on vomiting yourself, seeing or hearing others vomit, or feeling nauseated. It is one of the more common specific phobias, though many people have never heard the name. Because nausea and illness are everyday possibilities, emetophobia can affect far more of life than people expect, shaping what someone eats, where they go, and how they respond to illness in others. Many people with emetophobia have not vomited in years, yet the fear remains strong. It is treatable, and many people experience major improvement.

What Emetophobia Looks Like

  • Intense fear or panic at the thought of vomiting, as in being terrified of throwing up.
  • Constantly monitoring the body for signs of nausea.
  • Avoiding foods seen as risky, such as seafood, leftovers, or restaurant meals.
  • Eating very small amounts or restricting food, which can overlap with ARFID.
  • Avoiding travel, parties, hospitals, pregnancy, or places where people may be ill.
  • Excessive handwashing, checking expiry dates, or avoiding anyone who seems unwell.
  • Anxiety about eating in front of others or in unfamiliar places.
  • Carrying items such as mints, bags, or medication just in case.

Anxiety itself often causes nausea, which can then feel like confirmation that vomiting is about to happen. This creates a frustrating cycle, as the fear produces the very sensation that is feared.

Why It Happens

Emetophobia often begins in childhood or adolescence. Some people can recall a distressing experience, such as vomiting in public, witnessing someone be very ill, or a frightening episode of illness. Others cannot identify a clear starting point. Like other phobias, it involves the brain's threat system learning to treat something as dangerous. Avoidance and safety behaviours then keep the fear in place, because the person never has the chance to learn that they can cope with nausea or even vomiting.

Many people with emetophobia also fear losing control, being humiliated, or being unable to escape. Emetophobia is related to other specific phobias, described on the page about intense fear of a specific thing, and can overlap with health anxiety, panic disorder, and obsessive-compulsive patterns such as contamination fears.

Emetophobia can have significant effects on family and life decisions. Some people avoid pregnancy because of fear of morning sickness, or feel intense anxiety when children become ill. Parents with emetophobia may feel guilty about their reactions when a child is sick. These effects are common and are a strong reason to seek treatment.

What It Is Not

  • Not just disliking being sick. Most people dislike vomiting. Emetophobia involves intense fear and significant avoidance.
  • Not a sign of weakness. It is a recognized anxiety condition.
  • Not the same as an eating disorder, though food restriction can occur and may need attention, as described on the page about ARFID.
  • Not untreatable. Many people improve significantly with therapy.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A clinician will ask about fears, avoidance, safety behaviours, eating, and how the fear affects daily life, and will check for related conditions. Cognitive behavioural therapy with gradual exposure is the main treatment. Exposure is carefully paced and collaborative, beginning with manageable steps such as saying or reading related words, looking at images, or tolerating mild nausea sensations, and building toward harder situations. Reducing safety behaviours and checking is an important part of treatment. Cognitive work addresses beliefs about nausea, vomiting, and coping. With practice, fear usually decreases and life opens up.

Common Questions About Emetophobia

How does a fear of throwing up become a phobia?

It becomes emetophobia when the fear of vomiting, seeing others vomit, or feeling nauseated is intense, persistent, and leads to significant avoidance.

How common is emetophobia?

It is one of the more common specific phobias, though many people do not know it has a name.

Why do I feel nauseous when I am anxious about vomiting?

Anxiety itself can cause nausea, which feels like proof that vomiting is coming and fuels the fear cycle.

Can emetophobia cause restricted eating?

Yes. Some people avoid many foods or eat very little, which can overlap with ARFID and may need medical attention.

Why does emetophobia make me avoid sick people?

Fear of catching an illness that could cause vomiting leads many people to avoid anyone who seems unwell.

Does emetophobia affect decisions about pregnancy?

For some people, fear of morning sickness affects decisions about pregnancy. Treatment can reduce this fear.

Why do I carry mints and check expiry dates so often?

These are safety behaviours. They bring short-term comfort but keep the fear of vomiting going by preventing new learning.

What does exposure therapy for fear of vomiting involve?

It involves gradual, collaborative steps, such as reading related words or tolerating mild nausea, building toward harder situations.

How can a parent with emetophobia cope when their child is sick?

Having a plan, support from another adult, and working on the fear in therapy can make these moments more manageable.

Can emetophobia be cured?

Many people experience major improvement with cognitive behavioural therapy and gradual exposure.
