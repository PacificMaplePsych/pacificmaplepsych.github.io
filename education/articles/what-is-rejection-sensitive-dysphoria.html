What Is Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD) is a term used to describe intense, sudden emotional pain in response to real or perceived rejection, criticism, or failure. It is not a formal diagnosis, but it is widely discussed in relation to ADHD, where emotional dysregulation is common. People describe RSD as feeling crushed by a small comment, a delayed reply, or a hint of disapproval, often far more than the situation seems to warrant. Understanding RSD can reduce shame and help people respond to these moments with more steadiness.

For a closer look at the everyday experience, see the page on feeling rejected intensely by small comments.

What Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria Looks Like

  • Sudden, overwhelming hurt, shame, or anger after criticism or perceived rejection.
  • Replaying a comment for hours or days.
  • Assuming others are upset with you based on small cues.
  • Avoiding feedback, new opportunities, or social situations to prevent rejection.
  • People-pleasing or perfectionism to avoid disapproval.
  • Reacting strongly and then feeling embarrassed about the reaction.
  • In relationships, fear of abandonment or defensiveness when given feedback.

At work, RSD may make performance reviews feel unbearable. In friendships, a short text can trigger worry about the whole relationship. Many people describe the feeling as physical, like a punch.

Why It Happens

RSD is thought to reflect differences in emotional regulation that are common in ADHD. Emotions may arrive faster and more intensely, and it can be harder to step back and put them in perspective. Many people with ADHD have also experienced more criticism and correction growing up, for forgetfulness, disorganization, or impulsivity, which can heighten sensitivity to disapproval. Low self-esteem and past rejection add to the effect. Research on RSD as a distinct concept is still limited, but rejection sensitivity and emotional dysregulation in ADHD are well documented.

RSD reactions often follow a recognizable sequence. Something small happens, such as an unanswered message or a neutral comment. The mind quickly interprets it as rejection, and a wave of hurt or anger follows before there is time to consider other explanations. Afterward, people may withdraw, over-apologize, or lash out. Learning to recognize the first step in this sequence is often the most practical way to soften it.

What It Is Not

  • Not a formal diagnosis. RSD is a descriptive term, not a condition listed in diagnostic manuals.
  • Not exclusive to ADHD. Rejection sensitivity also appears in depression, social anxiety, borderline personality disorder, and after relational trauma.
  • Not simply being thin-skinned. The intensity and speed of the reaction often feel beyond the person's control.
  • Not the same as social anxiety. Social anxiety involves anticipatory fear of judgment. RSD describes the intense pain after perceived rejection.
  • Not borderline personality disorder. BPD involves broader patterns of identity, relationships, and impulsivity, though rejection sensitivity is part of it.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Because RSD is not a diagnosis, a clinician will usually look at the broader picture, including whether ADHD, depression, anxiety, or another condition is present. An adult ADHD assessment can clarify whether emotional dysregulation is part of ADHD. Treatment of ADHD, including medication prescribed by a physician for some people, may reduce emotional intensity. Therapy can help people recognize RSD reactions, pause before responding, check assumptions, build self-worth, and practise self-compassion. Dialectical behaviour therapy skills for emotion regulation can also be useful.

Common Questions About Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

Is rejection sensitive dysphoria a real diagnosis?

No. It is a descriptive term rather than a formal diagnosis, but it describes a real and common experience, especially among people with ADHD.

How is rejection sensitive dysphoria linked to ADHD?

ADHD often involves emotional dysregulation, with emotions that arrive quickly and intensely. Many people with ADHD also have histories of frequent criticism, which heightens sensitivity.

Why does one critical comment feel like a punch to the gut?

People describe sudden, overwhelming hurt, shame, or anger after perceived rejection, sometimes felt physically, followed by rumination or embarrassment.

Can people without ADHD have rejection sensitivity?

Yes. Rejection sensitivity also appears in depression, social anxiety, borderline personality disorder, and after relational trauma.

Why do I read rejection into my partner's quiet moods?

It can lead to misreading a partner's mood, fear of abandonment, and strong reactions to feedback, which can create conflict. Awareness and communication help.

Why do I avoid feedback because of rejection sensitivity?

Avoiding feedback can protect against the intense pain of perceived criticism, but it can limit growth and opportunities over time.

Can ADHD medication reduce rejection sensitivity?

Some people find that treating ADHD reduces emotional intensity, including rejection sensitivity. Medication decisions are made with a physician.

What helps in the moment when RSD hits?

Naming the reaction, pausing before responding, slowing your breathing, and checking your assumptions about the other person's intent can help the wave pass.

How is RSD different from social anxiety?

Social anxiety centres on fear of being judged before or during situations. RSD describes the intense emotional pain that follows perceived rejection or criticism.

Which therapy approaches help with rejection sensitivity?

Cognitive behavioural therapy, dialectical behaviour therapy skills, and self-compassion work can help people manage intense reactions and build self-worth.
