Treatment Options for PMDD and Premenstrual Mood Changes

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. In British Columbia, you can call 8-1-1 to speak with a nurse.

Premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) and other premenstrual mood changes are treated with a combination of medical options prescribed by a physician, psychotherapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy, and lifestyle supports. PMDD is a severe condition in which depression, irritability, anxiety, or mood swings appear in the week or two before a period and ease soon after it starts. Premenstrual exacerbation, where existing conditions such as depression or anxiety worsen before a period, is also treatable. Many people find significant relief once the pattern is recognized and a plan is in place. Treatment often involves trying and adjusting options over a few cycles.

The Main Treatment Options

Symptom tracking. Tracking mood and physical symptoms daily for at least two cycles helps confirm the pattern and guide treatment. It is also useful for measuring progress. More about the condition is on the page about PMDD.

Medication. A physician may recommend certain antidepressants, which can be taken daily or only during the premenstrual phase, since they often work quickly for PMDD. Some hormonal options, such as certain hormonal contraceptives, may help by stabilizing hormonal changes. For severe PMDD that does not respond to other treatments, a specialist may discuss further options. All medical treatments should be discussed with a physician, who considers your health history and preferences.

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT can help with the thoughts and reactions that intensify during the premenstrual phase, such as self-criticism, hopelessness, and conflict. It also helps people plan coping strategies for difficult days. Research suggests CBT can reduce premenstrual distress for many people.

Other therapy approaches. Mindfulness-based approaches, acceptance and commitment therapy, and dialectical behaviour therapy skills can help with emotional intensity and irritability.

Lifestyle supports. Regular physical activity, consistent sleep, regular meals, and reducing alcohol and caffeine may ease symptoms for some people. Some people find certain nutritional supplements helpful, though evidence varies, and supplements should be discussed with a doctor or pharmacist.

Relationship and work supports. Talking with partners, family, or employers about the pattern, and planning around high-symptom days, can reduce stress.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

Choosing a plan starts with confirming the pattern through tracking and ruling out other conditions, such as depression, bipolar disorder, thyroid problems, or perimenopause. Severity matters. Mild premenstrual symptoms may respond well to lifestyle changes and therapy, while PMDD often benefits from medication combined with therapy. Preferences, such as whether you want to avoid hormonal treatments, plans for pregnancy, other health conditions, and past experiences with treatment, all shape choices. When premenstrual symptoms worsen an existing condition, treating that condition is also important. A doctor, psychiatrist, or gynecologist may be involved.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress is often measured across cycles. Some medications for PMDD begin working within the first cycle, while therapy and lifestyle changes may take a few cycles to show full effects. Early signs of progress might include milder irritability, fewer conflicts, better sleep, or feeling less overwhelmed during the premenstrual phase. Tracking symptoms makes these changes easier to see. It is common to adjust treatment several times before finding what works best. Some cycles may be harder than others, especially during stress or illness.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Keep tracking your symptoms, which helps you and your care team see what is working. Plan ahead for high-symptom days by reducing demands, scheduling rest, and letting trusted people know what helps. Keep regular sleep, movement, and meals throughout the month, as described on the page about routines for mood stability. Practise self-compassion. Premenstrual mood changes are biological, not a personal failing. If you notice thoughts of suicide during the premenstrual phase, include this in a safety plan, as described on the page about safety plans, and reach out for support.

Common Questions About PMDD Treatment

My doctor confirmed PMDD. What mix of care might help me most?

Treatment often includes medication prescribed by a physician, therapy such as CBT, and lifestyle supports.

Can antidepressants be taken only before my period for PMDD?

Some can. A physician may recommend taking certain antidepressants only during the premenstrual phase.

Do hormonal contraceptives help with PMDD?

Some hormonal options may help by stabilizing hormonal changes. Discuss options with your physician.

Why would thought-focused work help when my mood shift is hormonal?

Hormones set off the change, but CBT helps with the self-criticism, hopelessness, and conflict that intensify during the premenstrual phase, and helps plan coping strategies.

Why do I need to track symptoms for PMDD treatment?

Tracking over at least two cycles confirms the pattern, guides treatment, and shows whether it is working.

Can exercise and diet changes help with PMDD?

Regular activity, consistent sleep, regular meals, and reducing alcohol and caffeine may ease symptoms for some people.

Will I feel a difference in my very next cycle once I start treatment?

Some medications help within the first cycle, while therapy and lifestyle changes may take a few cycles.

What is premenstrual exacerbation and how is it treated?

It is when existing conditions like depression worsen before a period. Treating the underlying condition is important.

How can I plan for my hardest premenstrual days?

Reduce demands, schedule rest, and let trusted people know what helps on those days.

Should a safety plan for PMDD account for the days in my cycle when I feel worst?

If suicidal thoughts appear during the premenstrual phase, noting this in your safety plan can help you prepare and reach out.
