Understanding Sibling Rivalry

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Sibling rivalry refers to the jealousy, competition, and conflict that commonly arise between brothers and sisters. It is a normal part of family life and can even help children learn negotiation, empathy, and conflict resolution. Rivalry tends to peak when children are close in age or going through developmental changes, and it often flares when parental attention feels scarce. It becomes a concern when conflict is constant, one child is consistently targeted, or physical or emotional harm occurs. Understanding what drives rivalry helps parents respond in ways that reduce conflict and strengthen sibling bonds.

What Sibling Rivalry Looks Like

  • Frequent arguments, teasing, and competition over toys, space, or attention.
  • Complaints about fairness, such as "you always let them."
  • Tattling or trying to get a sibling in trouble.
  • Regression in an older child after a new baby arrives.
  • Comparing achievements, looks, or privileges.
  • Physical fights, especially among younger children.

At home, rivalry can make daily routines tense and leave parents feeling like referees. It can also increase stress on parents, contributing to moments when they snap at their kids more than they would like.

Why It Happens

Siblings compete for a limited resource: their parents' time, attention, and approval. Differences in temperament, age, and needs can make things feel unfair even when parents try to be even-handed. Developmental stages matter, since toddlers are learning to share and teenagers are forming identities. Family changes, such as a new baby, a move, or separation, can increase tension. Children with ADHD, autism, anxiety, or health needs may require more parental attention, which siblings may resent. Parental stress, comparisons, and labels such as "the smart one" or "the difficult one" can intensify rivalry.

Rivalry is also shaped by how conflict is handled at home. When children see adults resolve disagreements respectfully, they tend to copy those skills. When conflict is handled with yelling or withdrawal, siblings may use the same patterns with each other.

What It Is Not

  • Not a sign of bad parenting. Rivalry happens in loving, well-functioning families.
  • Not the same as sibling bullying. Bullying involves repeated, one-sided harm with a power imbalance, such as an older child consistently hurting or humiliating a younger one, and needs firm intervention.
  • Not always solved by treating children identically. Fairness often means meeting each child's needs, not giving everyone the same thing.
  • Not always just rivalry. Persistent aggression, withdrawal, or distress in one child may point to anxiety, ADHD, or other needs. Your family doctor or a child mental health professional can help you look into this.

How It Is Addressed

Most sibling rivalry improves with consistent, calm parenting strategies rather than clinical treatment. Helpful approaches include giving each child regular one-on-one time, avoiding comparisons and labels, setting clear family rules about physical and verbal harm, coaching children to solve problems rather than always stepping in, acknowledging feelings while holding limits, and praising cooperation. When a new sibling arrives, involving older children and keeping routines steady helps. When conflict is severe, involves ongoing harm, or is linked with a child's mental health needs, parent coaching or family therapy can help families find new patterns. Parents' own stress and wellbeing are an important part of the picture too, as explored on the page about feeling like you are failing as a parent.

Common Questions About Sibling Rivalry

Is sibling rivalry normal?

Yes. Competition and conflict between siblings are common and can help children learn to negotiate and resolve disagreements.

What causes sibling rivalry?

Competition for parental attention, differences in age and temperament, developmental stages, family changes, and comparisons between children all contribute.

How do I know if sibling conflict has become bullying?

Bullying involves repeated, one-sided harm with a power imbalance, such as one child consistently hurting, threatening, or humiliating another. It needs firm adult intervention.

Should parents step in during every sibling fight?

Not always. For minor disagreements, coaching children to solve problems helps them build skills. Step in right away when there is physical harm or cruelty.

How can I reduce jealousy after a new baby arrives?

Involve older children in small ways, keep routines steady, protect one-on-one time with them, and acknowledge their feelings about the change.

Is it fair to treat each child differently?

Fairness often means meeting each child's individual needs rather than treating everyone identically. Explaining this can help children feel understood.

How do labels like 'the responsible one' affect siblings?

Labels can lock children into roles and increase comparison and resentment. Describing specific behaviours rather than fixed traits is usually more helpful.

Can a child's ADHD or autism increase sibling tension?

It can, especially if one child needs more attention. Protecting time for each child and explaining differences in age-appropriate ways can help.

Do siblings who fight as children stay close as adults?

Many do. Childhood conflict does not predict adult distance, and warmth between siblings often grows as they mature.

When is family therapy helpful for sibling rivalry?

Family therapy can help when conflict is constant, involves ongoing harm, or is linked with a child's mental health needs, or when parents feel stuck.
