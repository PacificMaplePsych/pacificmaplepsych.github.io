Why Do I Feel Lonely All the Time

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You might live alone or with a family. You might have hundreds of contacts on your phone or very few. Either way, a feeling of loneliness follows you through the day. Evenings and weekends can be the hardest, when the quiet makes the ache more noticeable. You might wonder whether something is wrong with you, or why connection seems to come easily to others but not to you.

Loneliness is extremely common, and it is not a personal failing. It is a signal, much like hunger or thirst, that a basic human need for connection is not being met. Understanding why it has become constant is the first step toward addressing it.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Life changes. Moving, starting a new job, retirement, a breakup, becoming a parent, or friends drifting away can leave gaps in your social world that take time to fill. This often overlaps with feeling lost after a big life change.

Relationships without depth. You can be surrounded by people and still feel lonely if you rarely talk about what really matters. People who avoid talking about how they really feel often experience this kind of loneliness.

Social anxiety. Fear of judgment can make it hard to initiate plans, join groups, or open up, even when you want connection.

Depression. Low mood makes people withdraw and makes connection feel less rewarding, which deepens loneliness. This is explored in Why Do I Feel Disconnected From Everyone Around Me.

Feeling different. Neurodivergent adults, newcomers to Canada, and people whose identity or experiences are not reflected around them can feel lonely even in busy social settings.

How we live now. Remote work, long commutes, high housing costs, and fewer community spaces make casual, regular contact harder to come by.

When It Looks More Like Chronic Loneliness

Occasional loneliness is part of life. Chronic loneliness is different: a persistent sense of disconnection that lasts months or years. Research links long-term loneliness to poorer sleep, higher stress, depression, and even physical health problems, which is why it deserves to be taken seriously.

Chronic loneliness can also change how people think. Feeling lonely for a long time tends to make the brain more alert to social threat, so people may expect rejection, notice slights more, and hold back from reaching out. This protective stance makes sense, but it can keep loneliness going.

Signs of chronic loneliness include:

  • Feeling lonely most days for months, even when you have people around.
  • Believing others do not really understand or care about you.
  • Hesitating to reach out because you expect to be let down.
  • Dreading evenings, weekends, or holidays.
  • Feeling uneasy or anxious when you are alone.

What Can Help

  • Look for regular contact. Repeated, low-pressure contact with the same people, such as a weekly class, club, or volunteer shift, builds connection more reliably than one-off events.
  • Deepen one relationship. Sharing a little more with one person you already know can be more meaningful than meeting many new people.
  • Reach out first, even briefly. A short message or invitation often lands better than you expect.
  • Notice rejection expectations. When you predict people will not want to see you, treat it as a thought to test rather than a fact.
  • Be patient. New friendships take time and repeated contact to grow.

Common Questions About Chronic Loneliness

Can someone with a partner and family still feel lonely all the time?

Loneliness is about the quality of connection rather than how many people are around. If relationships lack depth or you do not feel understood, you can feel lonely in a crowd or even in a relationship.

Is chronic loneliness bad for my health?

Research links long-term loneliness to poorer sleep, higher stress, depression, and increased risk of some physical health problems. Addressing loneliness can benefit both mental and physical wellbeing.

How is loneliness different from being alone?

Being alone is a situation, while loneliness is a feeling of disconnection. Many people enjoy time alone without feeling lonely, and others feel lonely despite being surrounded by people.

Can depression make me feel lonely?

Yes. Depression often leads to withdrawal and makes connection feel less rewarding, which increases loneliness. Loneliness can also contribute to depression, so the two often reinforce each other.

Why is it so hard to make friends as an adult?

Adults have fewer natural settings for repeated, casual contact, such as school. Busy schedules, moves, and family demands also make it harder. Joining regular activities helps recreate those conditions.

Does long-term loneliness change how I see other people?

It can. Chronic loneliness tends to make people more alert to possible rejection, so they may interpret neutral behaviour as unfriendly and hold back. Recognizing this pattern makes it easier to test those expectations.

Can social anxiety cause loneliness?

Yes. Fear of judgment can stop people from initiating plans, joining groups, or opening up, even when they want connection. Treating social anxiety often makes it easier to build relationships.

What are good ways to meet people in a new city?

Classes, sports leagues, volunteering, faith or cultural communities, and interest-based groups offer repeated contact with the same people, which is how most friendships form.

Is loneliness common among newcomers to Canada?

Yes. Leaving behind family, friends, and familiar culture can lead to significant loneliness, especially in the first years. Cultural community groups and settlement services can help build new connections.

What does therapy for chronic loneliness focus on?

Yes. Therapy can address anxiety, depression, and past experiences that make connection difficult, and help you build skills and confidence for reaching out. Group therapy can also provide direct connection with others.
