Understanding Self-Harm

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you have hurt yourself and the injury is serious, or you are thinking about suicide, call 911 or go to your nearest emergency department. You can call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Young people can reach Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting 686868.

Self-harm, sometimes called non-suicidal self-injury, means deliberately hurting yourself. For most people, it is a way of coping with intense emotional pain, numbness, or distress, rather than an attempt to end their life. Self-harm is more common than many people realize, especially among teens and young adults, though it occurs at every age. People who self-harm often feel shame and hide it. Understanding self-harm with compassion, rather than judgment, is an important step toward finding safer ways to cope. Support helps, and recovery is possible.

What Self-Harm Can Look Like

  • Hurting yourself on purpose when emotions feel too big to handle.
  • Wearing long sleeves or covering skin even in warm weather.
  • Unexplained injuries, or explanations that do not quite fit.
  • Withdrawing, spending long periods alone, or keeping sharp objects hidden.
  • Feeling relief, calm, or a sense of control after self-harm, followed by shame or guilt.
  • Strong urges that build during stress, loneliness, or conflict.

For a parent or friend, noticing signs can be frightening. The page on signs your child may need mental health support offers guidance for families.

Why It Happens

People self-harm for many reasons, and these often change over time. Common reasons include getting relief from overwhelming emotions, feeling something when numb, expressing pain that is hard to put into words, punishing oneself because of shame or self-hatred, or regaining a sense of control. Self-harm can bring short-term relief because it shifts attention and changes the body's stress response. That relief is real but brief, and it tends to reinforce the urge, so the pattern can become hard to stop.

Risk factors include difficult or traumatic experiences, bullying, emotional neglect, depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and difficulty managing intense emotions. Some people who self-harm also experience thoughts of suicide or wishing they could disappear. Although self-harm is usually not a suicide attempt, it is associated with a higher risk of suicide over time, which is why it deserves careful attention and support.

What It Is Not

  • Not attention-seeking. Most people hide self-harm and feel deep shame about it.
  • Not the same as a suicide attempt, though the two can overlap and both need support.
  • Not a choice made lightly. It often reflects pain that feels unmanageable.
  • Not something to stop through punishment or ultimatums. Pressure often increases shame and secrecy.
  • Not a life sentence. Many people learn other ways to cope and stop self-harming.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

A doctor can treat injuries and check physical health. A mental health professional will ask, with care, about the reasons behind self-harm, how often it happens, other emotional difficulties, and any thoughts of suicide, and will work with the person on a safety plan. Dialectical behaviour therapy is well supported for self-harm and teaches skills for emotion regulation, distress tolerance, and relationships. Cognitive behavioural therapy and mentalization-based approaches can also help. Therapy addresses the underlying pain, not just the behaviour. For young people, involving family in a supportive way often helps. If someone tells you they self-harm, staying calm, listening without judgment, and helping them connect with support can make a real difference.

Common Questions About Self-Harm

Why do people self-harm?

Most people self-harm to cope with overwhelming emotions, numbness, shame, or distress. It often brings brief relief but reinforces the urge.

Is self-harm a suicide attempt?

Usually not. Most self-harm is a way of coping rather than trying to end one's life, but it is linked with higher suicide risk over time.

Is self-harm attention-seeking?

No. Most people who self-harm hide it and feel deep shame. It usually reflects real pain that deserves support.

How common is self-harm among teens?

Self-harm is relatively common among adolescents and young adults, though it occurs at all ages.

What should I do if a friend tells me they self-harm?

Stay calm, listen without judgment, thank them for telling you, and encourage them to connect with a trusted adult or professional.

How should parents respond if their child is self-harming?

Respond calmly and with care, avoid punishment, make sure injuries are treated, and seek professional support promptly.

Why does self-harm bring a sense of relief?

It can shift attention and change the body's stress response, bringing short-term calm, which is why the pattern can become hard to stop.

What therapy is recommended for self-harm?

Dialectical behaviour therapy has strong support, and cognitive behavioural and mentalization-based therapies can also help.

What is a safety plan for self-harm?

A safety plan lists warning signs, coping strategies, people to contact, and crisis numbers to use when urges become strong.

Can people stop self-harming?

Yes. With support and new coping skills, many people reduce or stop self-harm and address the pain behind it.
