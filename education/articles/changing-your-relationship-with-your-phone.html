Changing Your Relationship With Your Phone

Changing your relationship with your phone usually works best by changing your environment rather than relying on willpower: turning off most notifications, moving tempting apps off the home screen, keeping the phone out of the bedroom, setting phone-free times, and replacing some scrolling with activities you value. Phones are useful tools, but many apps are designed to capture attention and keep people engaged as long as possible. Many people notice they pick up their phone automatically, lose time scrolling, or feel worse after using it. Small, deliberate changes can help you use your phone more intentionally, with more time and attention left for the rest of your life.

Why These Strategies Help

Phone habits form through repeated cues and rewards. A notification, a moment of boredom, or an uncomfortable feeling acts as a cue. Picking up the phone brings a small, unpredictable reward, such as a message, a like, or an interesting post. Unpredictable rewards are especially powerful at building habits. Over time, reaching for the phone becomes automatic, often happening before people realize it. The broader pattern is described on the page about problematic technology and social media use.

Phones can also become a way to avoid difficult feelings, such as anxiety, loneliness, or stress. Scrolling offers quick relief but can leave people feeling more drained, especially late at night. Changing the environment interrupts the cue and reward cycle, reduces automatic use, and creates space for choice.

Strategies to Try

1. Notice Your Patterns

Check your screen time report or simply notice for a few days when and why you pick up your phone. Common triggers include boredom, waiting, stress, and waking up.

2. Turn Off Non-Essential Notifications

Keep notifications only for people and apps that truly need your immediate attention.

3. Rearrange Your Home Screen

Move tempting apps into folders or off the home screen, and keep useful tools, such as maps or a calendar, in easy reach. Some people switch the display to greyscale to make it less appealing.

4. Create Phone-Free Zones and Times

Keep your phone out of the bedroom and off the dinner table. Use a regular alarm clock. Try the first and last thirty minutes of the day without it.

5. Add Friction

Log out of social media apps, use app timers, or delete apps you use compulsively and access them only on a computer.

6. Replace, Do Not Just Remove

Plan what you will do instead, such as reading, going for a walk, calling a friend, or a hobby. Keep a book or puzzle where you usually scroll.

7. Check In With Yourself

Before picking up your phone, pause and ask what you are looking for. Connection, rest, and information can often be found in other ways.

8. Curate Your Feed

Unfollow accounts that leave you anxious, envious, or low, and follow ones that feel supportive or useful.

Common Obstacles

Phones are often necessary for work, school, family, and safety, which makes cutting back harder. Focus on reducing automatic, unhelpful use rather than eliminating use entirely. Many people feel restless or anxious when they first cut back. This usually eases within days or weeks. Social pressure to respond quickly can also make limits hard. Letting people know you check messages at certain times can help. People with ADHD may find phones especially hard to put down, and structured strategies, like those on the page about non-medication strategies for ADHD, can help. Late-night scrolling often worsens sleep, as described on the page about winding down before bed.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If phone use feels out of control, interferes with work, sleep, or relationships, continues despite harm, or is linked with anxiety, depression, or loneliness, therapy can help. Cognitive behavioural approaches can address the habit and what drives it. If phone use involves gambling or spending you cannot control, specialized support may be needed. Social media can also affect how people feel about themselves, as explored on the page about body image distress.

Common Questions About Changing Phone Habits

Why is it so hard to put my phone down?

Phone apps use unpredictable rewards, such as messages and likes, which make checking your phone a powerful, automatic habit.

What is the easiest first step to reduce phone use?

Turning off non-essential notifications is often the easiest first step, since it removes many cues to pick up your phone.

Does keeping my phone out of the bedroom help?

Yes. Keeping your phone out of the bedroom can improve sleep and reduce scrolling first thing in the morning and last thing at night.

Does greyscale mode really make my phone less addictive?

Some people find greyscale mode makes their phone less appealing, which can reduce automatic scrolling.

Why do I feel anxious when I try to use my phone less?

Restlessness or anxiety is common when cutting back on phone use and usually eases within days or weeks.

What should I do instead of scrolling on my phone?

Plan replacements, such as reading, walking, calling a friend, or a hobby, and keep them where you usually reach for your phone.

Is it realistic to cut back on my phone when I need it for work?

Yes. Focus on reducing automatic phone use, such as social media scrolling, rather than cutting out necessary use.

Why does scrolling on my phone leave me feeling worse?

Scrolling can bring comparison, overstimulation, and lost time, which often leave people more drained or low after using their phone.

Can phone use be harder to control with ADHD?

Yes. ADHD brains respond strongly to novelty and quick rewards, so phones can be especially hard to put down.

When should I get help for how much I use my phone?

If phone use feels out of control or affects sleep, work, relationships, or mood, therapy can help.
