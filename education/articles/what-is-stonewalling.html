What Is Stonewalling

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Stonewalling is when a person withdraws from a conversation or conflict by shutting down, going silent, looking away, or physically leaving without explanation. Relationship researcher John Gottman identified stonewalling as one of four patterns, along with criticism, contempt, and defensiveness, that predict relationship distress. People often stonewall because they feel emotionally flooded, meaning their heart rate and stress hormones have risen so much that they cannot think clearly or respond. Although it can feel protective, stonewalling leaves the other partner feeling ignored, rejected, or abandoned. The good news is that couples can replace stonewalling with agreed, time-limited breaks and repair.

What Stonewalling Looks Like

  • Silence or one-word answers during conflict
  • Looking away, at a phone, or appearing blank
  • Leaving the room without saying why or when you will return
  • Changing the subject or refusing to engage
  • Shutting down emotionally, as described on the page about shutting down during arguments

Why People Stonewall

Flooding

When conflict triggers a strong stress response, the body shifts into fight, flight, or freeze. Thinking and listening become very difficult, and shutting down can feel like the only option.

Avoiding Escalation

Some people stonewall to avoid saying something hurtful or to stop an argument from getting worse.

Learned Patterns

Growing up in homes where conflict was frightening or emotions were not discussed can make withdrawal a default, as described on the page about avoidant attachment.

Feeling Overwhelmed or Criticized

Harsh criticism can trigger withdrawal, which in turn leads to more criticism, creating a cycle, as described on the page about the pursue-withdraw pattern.

How It Affects Relationships

For the partner on the receiving end, stonewalling can feel like being shut out or punished, which often increases anxiety, anger, and pursuit. Problems remain unresolved, and over time both partners may feel lonely and hopeless. Our blog post on why smart couples get stuck in negative communication loops explores how these cycles develop.

Stonewalling and Physical Health

Research on couples has found that the partner who stonewalls is often highly stressed internally, even when appearing calm on the outside. Heart rate and stress hormones may be elevated, which is why the person feels unable to engage. Over time, frequent conflict and flooding can take a toll on both partners' wellbeing. Learning to recognize flooding and calm the body before continuing a conversation benefits not only the relationship but also each partner's health.

Stonewalling vs. Taking a Break

Taking a break during conflict can be healthy. The difference lies in how it is done. A healthy break is communicated, such as "I am too overwhelmed to talk well right now. I need 30 minutes, and then I want to come back to this." It has a time frame, and both partners return to the conversation. Stonewalling is silent, open-ended, and leaves the other person guessing.

Breaking the Pattern

  1. Notice flooding early. Signs include a racing heart, tension, or blanking out.
  2. Agree on a break signal with your partner ahead of time.
  3. Take at least 20 minutes to calm down, doing something soothing rather than replaying the argument.
  4. Come back. Return to the conversation at the agreed time.
  5. Use a gentle start-up. Raising issues without blame reduces flooding.
  6. Repair. Acknowledge the shutdown and reconnect, as described on the page about how to repair after an argument.

When to Get Support

If stonewalling has become a regular pattern, couples therapy can help both partners understand their responses and build safer ways to handle conflict. The page about the Gottman Method describes one well-researched approach.

Common Questions About Stonewalling

What does stonewalling look like during an argument?

Stonewalling during an argument looks like going silent, looking away, giving one-word answers, or leaving without saying why or when you will return.

Why do people stonewall?

People often stonewall because they feel emotionally flooded during conflict, or because they learned to withdraw to avoid escalation.

Is stonewalling a form of abuse?

Stonewalling is usually a stress response rather than abuse, but deliberate, prolonged silent treatment used to punish or control can be emotionally abusive.

Is taking a break during conflict a form of stonewalling?

Not when it is communicated. A healthy break is time-limited with a plan to return, while stonewalling is silent and open-ended.

What is flooding in a relationship?

Flooding is a strong stress response during conflict, with a racing heart and overwhelm, that makes listening and thinking difficult.

Why does Gottman suggest at least 20 minutes to recover from flooding?

Gottman research suggests the body needs at least 20 minutes to calm down after flooding, which helps partners stop stonewalling and return to the conversation.

How do I respond when my partner stonewalls me?

Stay calm, avoid pursuing harder, suggest a break with a set time to return, and discuss the pattern when you are both calm.

Is stonewalling one of the four horsemen?

Yes. Stonewalling is one of John Gottman's four horsemen, along with criticism, contempt, and defensiveness.

Can stonewalling end a relationship?

Ongoing stonewalling can erode connection and predict relationship distress, but couples can change the pattern with effort and support.

Can couples therapy help with stonewalling?

Yes. Couples therapy can help partners understand flooding, agree on healthy breaks, and repair after conflict.
