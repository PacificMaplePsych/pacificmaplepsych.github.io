Depression and Chronic Illness

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If living with illness and low mood has led to thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911. Speak with your medical team about any new or worsening physical symptoms.

Depression and chronic illness often go together. People living with long-term conditions such as diabetes, heart disease, arthritis, multiple sclerosis, cancer, chronic pain, inflammatory bowel disease, and autoimmune conditions have higher rates of depression than the general population. Depression is not an inevitable part of illness, and it is not simply an understandable reaction to be endured. It is a treatable condition that can affect how people feel, how they manage their illness, and even physical health outcomes. Recognizing and treating depression is an important part of caring for the whole person.

What Depression Alongside Illness Can Look Like

  • Persistent low mood, hopelessness, or emptiness lasting two weeks or more.
  • Loss of interest in things that are still possible to enjoy.
  • Feelings of worthlessness, guilt, or being a burden.
  • Withdrawing from friends and family.
  • Difficulty following treatment plans, taking medications, or attending appointments.
  • Sleep and appetite changes beyond what the illness explains.
  • Thoughts of death or suicide.

Because fatigue, pain, and sleep problems can come from both illness and depression, depression is sometimes missed. Emotional symptoms, such as hopelessness and loss of pleasure, are often the clearest signs. The page on physical symptoms of depression explores this overlap.

Why the Two Are Linked

The connection runs in both directions. Living with a chronic illness brings losses, such as reduced mobility, independence, work, or plans for the future, which can trigger grief and depression. Pain and fatigue affect mood. Some illnesses directly affect the brain or involve inflammation, which research links with depression. Certain medications can also contribute to low mood. The emotional and practical demands of managing illness, as described on the page about coping with chronic illness, add further stress.

Depression, in turn, can make illness harder to manage. It can reduce energy for self-care, make it harder to follow treatment, and lower motivation to stay active. It can also intensify the experience of pain, as explained on the page about the mind-body connection in chronic pain. Research has linked depression with poorer outcomes in some conditions, which is one reason treating it matters.

Social factors play a role too. Illness can limit work, finances, and social activities, leading to isolation. Invisible illnesses may bring disbelief from others. Support from family, friends, and community can protect against depression, while isolation increases risk.

Caregivers and family members can also be affected. Supporting someone with a long-term condition can be exhausting and emotionally demanding, and caregivers themselves face higher rates of depression. Including loved ones in conversations about mood and support can benefit the whole household.

What It Is Not

  • Not a normal part of being ill. Depression is common but treatable.
  • Not a sign that illness is all in your head. Treating depression complements medical care.
  • Not something to push through alone. Support improves both mood and illness management.
  • Not a personal failing. Anyone facing chronic illness can develop depression.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Health care providers can screen for depression as part of routine care. A physician can review medications and check for medical contributors. Psychotherapy is effective. Cognitive behavioural therapy helps address hopeless thinking and build coping skills. Behavioural activation encourages meaningful activity within physical limits. Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people live according to their values alongside illness. Antidepressants may be helpful, with attention to interactions with other medications. Support groups for specific illnesses reduce isolation. Coordinated care between mental health and medical providers often works best. More information about the condition itself is on the page about major depressive disorder.

Common Questions About Depression and Chronic Illness

Is depression more common in people with chronic illness?

Yes. People with long-term conditions have higher rates of depression than the general population.

Which signs suggest low mood is more than a reaction to my condition?

Emotional signs such as hopelessness, worthlessness, and loss of pleasure point toward depression, beyond fatigue or pain alone.

Can a chronic illness directly cause depression?

Some illnesses affect the brain or involve inflammation, which research links with depression, alongside the stress of illness.

Does depression make chronic illness worse?

It can make self-care and treatment harder and intensify pain, and it is linked with poorer outcomes in some conditions.

Can my medications cause low mood?

Some medications can affect mood. Ask your doctor or pharmacist to review them if you notice changes.

Is it safe to take antidepressants with other medications?

Often yes, but interactions need to be checked. Your doctor or pharmacist can help choose a safe option.

How does therapy help depression with a chronic illness?

Therapy helps address hopeless thinking, build coping skills, and find meaningful activity within physical limits.

Why do I feel like a burden to my family?

Illness can change roles and increase reliance on others. Feeling like a burden is common and worth discussing in therapy.

Can caregivers of people with chronic illness become depressed?

Yes. Caregivers face higher rates of depression and benefit from support for themselves.

Are there support groups for people with chronic illness?

Yes. Many condition-specific organizations and hospitals offer peer support groups.
