Why Do I Feel Sick to My Stomach Before Big Events

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

The job interview is at ten, and by eight your stomach is in knots. Maybe you feel queasy, maybe you cannot face breakfast, or maybe you spend the morning making repeated trips to the bathroom. The same thing happens before presentations, first dates, flights, weddings, and exams. It can feel like your body is betraying you at exactly the moment you most need it to cooperate.

The link between the stomach and the emotions is real and well documented. Understanding it can take some of the embarrassment and fear out of the experience and point toward what actually helps.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Ordinary nerves. Some stomach flutter before a meaningful event is completely normal. The body gears up for something important, and the gut is one of the first places that shift is felt. If the symptoms are mild, pass quickly, and do not stop you from doing things, they may simply be part of caring about the outcome. See also Why Do I Get Sweaty Palms Before Talking to People.

Excitement. Physically, excitement and nervousness are very similar. Butterflies before a vacation or a concert come from the same system as butterflies before a performance review.

Eating patterns and stimulants. Skipping meals when you are busy, drinking extra coffee to push through, or eating unfamiliar food while travelling can all upset the stomach on their own. Related reading: Why Does My Heart Race for No Reason.

Gut conditions. Irritable bowel syndrome, reflux, food intolerances, and other digestive conditions can all flare with stress. If you have stomach symptoms often, not just before events, or notice blood, weight loss, or pain that wakes you at night, it is important to see a doctor.

Medications. Some medications cause nausea or bowel changes, which may be more noticeable when you are already tense.

When It Looks More Like Anxiety

The gut and brain are in constant communication through nerves, hormones, and the immune system, a connection often called the gut-brain axis. When your brain senses a threat, including a social or performance threat, it activates the stress response. Blood flow shifts toward the muscles, digestion slows or speeds up unpredictably, and stomach acid and gut sensitivity can increase. The result is nausea, cramping, loss of appetite, or sudden urgency.

Anxiety is likely playing a bigger role when:

  • Symptoms begin hours or even days ahead of the event, as your mind rehearses what might go wrong. This is known as anticipatory anxiety.
  • The discomfort is strong enough that you have cancelled plans, left early, or turned down opportunities because of it.
  • You worry about the symptoms themselves, such as feeling sick in public or not being able to reach a bathroom, and that worry makes them worse.
  • The pattern shows up across many kinds of events, not just one specific situation.

That third point matters a lot. When people become afraid of their stomach symptoms, they start watching for any sign of queasiness, which keeps the stress response running and makes the symptoms more likely. Some people develop a specific fear of vomiting, which can strongly shape their daily choices. Avoiding events brings short term relief, but it teaches the brain that the situation really was dangerous, so the next one feels harder.

What Can Help

  • Eat something gentle. Going into a stressful event on an empty stomach often makes nausea worse. Small, plain snacks and water tend to sit better than a big meal or extra coffee.
  • Slow your breathing. Slow breaths into the belly, with a longer exhale, help shift the body out of fight or flight and toward a rest and digest state.
  • Reframe the sensation. Telling yourself "this is my body getting ready" rather than "something is wrong with me" can reduce the second wave of worry that amplifies symptoms.
  • Plan, but do not over plan. Knowing where washrooms are can ease worry. Constantly checking and rechecking escape routes, though, can keep anxiety high.
  • Keep showing up. Gradually facing events rather than avoiding them is one of the most reliable ways to reduce anticipatory anxiety over time.

Common Questions About Nervous Stomach Symptoms

Why does anxiety affect the stomach so strongly?

The gut is packed with nerves that are closely connected to the brain's stress system. When the brain senses a threat, it changes how quickly the gut moves, how sensitive it is, and how much acid is produced. That is why emotional stress so often shows up as nausea, cramping, or bowel changes.

Is it normal to feel nauseous before a wedding, exam, or job interview?

Yes, mild nausea before a high stakes event is very common and usually passes once things get underway. It becomes more of a concern when it is intense, starts days in advance, or leads you to avoid important events.

How can I tell if it's IBS or anxiety?

The two often overlap, since stress can trigger IBS flares. IBS usually involves recurring abdominal pain linked to bowel changes that happen outside stressful moments too. If your symptoms are frequent or come with warning signs like weight loss or blood, a doctor should assess them.

Why do I sometimes need the bathroom urgently before something important?

The stress response can speed up movement in the lower gut, which leads to sudden urgency or loose stools. This is a common physical sign of anxiety, and it often eases as you practise calming techniques and face the situations that trigger it.

Should I skip eating before a big event if my stomach is upset?

Skipping food entirely can make nausea and shakiness worse, partly because low blood sugar adds to the physical stress response. Light, plain foods and small sips of water are usually easier on the stomach than either a large meal or nothing at all.

Can fear of vomiting make the nausea worse?

Yes. Fear of being sick, sometimes called emetophobia when it becomes intense, keeps attention fixed on stomach sensations and keeps the stress response active. That combination can increase nausea and create a cycle that exposure-based therapy is well suited to address.

Why do the stomach symptoms start days before the event?

This is usually anticipatory anxiety. Each time your mind imagines the event going badly, the body reacts as though it is happening, so the stress response can switch on long before the actual day.

Can excitement cause the same stomach feelings as anxiety?

It can. Excitement and nervousness use much of the same physical machinery, including adrenaline and changes in gut activity. The difference is largely in how the feeling is interpreted, which is why reframing sensations as readiness can sometimes help.

Are there breathing techniques that help settle nausea?

Slow, steady breathing into the belly, with the exhale a little longer than the inhale, helps activate the calming branch of the nervous system. Practising it daily, not just in the moment, tends to make it more effective when you need it.

When should stomach symptoms before events be checked by a doctor?

See a doctor if symptoms happen often outside stressful situations, are severe, or come with blood in the stool, unexplained weight loss, fever, vomiting that will not stop, or pain that wakes you up. These signs point toward causes that need medical attention.
