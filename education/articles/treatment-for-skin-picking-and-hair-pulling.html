Treatment for Skin Picking and Hair Pulling

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Skin picking (excoriation disorder) and hair pulling (trichotillomania) are treated most effectively with behavioural therapies designed for body-focused repetitive behaviours (BFRBs), especially habit reversal training and the comprehensive behavioural model known as ComB. These approaches help people understand what triggers picking or pulling, notice the urges earlier, and use competing responses and environmental changes to reduce the behaviour. Acceptance and commitment therapy and other supports can help with the shame and emotions that often accompany BFRBs. Many people experience significant improvement with treatment, though urges may not disappear entirely. BFRBs are common, and they are not a sign of weakness or a lack of willpower.

The Main Treatment Options

Habit reversal training. This is the core behavioural treatment for BFRBs. It includes awareness training, where you learn to notice when and how picking or pulling happens, and competing response training, where you practise a physical action that makes the behaviour hard to do, such as clenching your fists or holding an object for a minute when urges arise. It often includes social support from a trusted person.

The comprehensive behavioural model (ComB). ComB looks at the sensory, cognitive, emotional, motor, and environmental factors that drive each person's picking or pulling and builds a personalized plan. For example, someone who pulls while reading in bed might use gloves, change lighting, or keep a fidget item nearby.

Acceptance and commitment therapy. ACT helps people notice urges without acting on them and focus on what matters to them, and is often combined with habit reversal.

Cognitive behavioural approaches. CBT can address perfectionism, shame, and beliefs that fuel the behaviour, such as needing skin to be perfectly smooth.

Emotion regulation skills. Since many people pick or pull to manage stress, boredom, or anxiety, learning other ways to regulate helps.

Medication. Evidence for medication is limited and mixed, though a physician may treat co-occurring anxiety, depression, or OCD.

The conditions themselves are described on the page about body-focused repetitive behaviours.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A clinician will explore when and where picking or pulling happens, what it feels like before, during, and after, how much time it takes, and how it affects your skin, hair, and daily life. Some people pick or pull automatically without noticing, while others do it deliberately in response to urges or tension. Many do both. These patterns shape which strategies are chosen. The clinician will also screen for anxiety, depression, OCD, ADHD, and body image concerns. BFRBs are grouped with OCD-related conditions, though treatment differs from standard ERP, so a therapist with BFRB experience makes a difference. The page about finding an OCD-informed therapist offers questions that apply here too.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Early progress often involves noticing picking or pulling sooner, including the situations and feelings that trigger it. Over weeks to months, many people reduce how often and how long they pick or pull, and skin or hair begins to heal. Urges often continue for a while but feel more manageable. Setbacks during stress are common and are part of recovery. Tracking progress helps you see change that may feel slow day to day.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Keep tools that block or replace the behaviour within reach, such as fidget items, gloves, bandages, or hats. Reduce triggers, such as magnifying mirrors or bright bathroom lighting. Keep a simple log of when urges happen. Practise competing responses daily. Treat yourself with compassion rather than shame, as described on the page about self-compassion, since shame often fuels the cycle. If picking leads to open wounds that look infected, see a doctor.

Common Questions About Treating Skin Picking and Hair Pulling

What is the most effective treatment for skin picking and hair pulling?

Behavioural therapies for BFRBs, especially habit reversal training and the ComB model, are the most effective treatments for skin picking and hair pulling.

How does habit reversal training interrupt hair pulling before it starts?

Habit reversal training teaches you to notice hair pulling urges and use a competing physical action that makes pulling hard to do.

What is the ComB model for skin picking?

The ComB model looks at sensory, emotional, cognitive, motor, and environmental factors behind skin picking and builds a personalized plan.

What is a competing response for BFRBs?

A competing response is a physical action, such as clenching your fists or holding an object, that blocks picking or pulling during an urge.

Is there medication for trichotillomania or skin picking?

Evidence for medication for trichotillomania and skin picking is limited and mixed, though co-occurring conditions may be treated.

How can I catch skin picking that happens on autopilot?

Awareness training helps. Many people pick automatically during focused or relaxed activities, and tracking when it happens helps you notice skin picking earlier.

How is treatment for BFRBs different from OCD treatment?

BFRBs are grouped with OCD-related conditions, but skin picking and hair pulling are treated mainly with habit reversal rather than standard ERP.

Is ACT used alongside habit reversal for hair pulling?

Yes. Acceptance and commitment therapy is often combined with habit reversal training for hair pulling, helping people make room for urges without acting on them.

What can I keep nearby to reduce skin picking?

Fidget items, gloves, bandages, and removing magnifying mirrors can reduce skin picking opportunities.

How long does treatment for skin picking or hair pulling take?

Many people see improvement in skin picking or hair pulling over weeks to months, with urges becoming more manageable over time.
