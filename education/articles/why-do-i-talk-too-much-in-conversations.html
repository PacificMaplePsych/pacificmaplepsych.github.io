Why Do I Talk Too Much in Conversations

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You notice it happening in real time and still cannot quite stop it. A simple story turns into five tangents. A quick update becomes a ten minute monologue. Afterward comes the familiar replay, wondering whether you talked too much again, whether people are tired of it, whether you should have just said less. If this is a recurring pattern for you, it is worth understanding what might actually be behind it.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Excitement and enthusiasm. Genuine excitement about a topic naturally produces more talking, more detail, and more tangents. This is a normal part of enthusiasm, though it can land differently depending on the listener and situation.

Anxiety. For some people, filling space with talking is a way of managing social discomfort or the anxiety of a pause. Nervous over-explaining or rambling can be a response to feeling uneasy rather than genuine excitement about the content itself.

Processing out loud. Some people think most effectively by talking through their ideas rather than silently, which can look like talking too much when really it is how their thinking process works.

Loneliness or limited social contact. When opportunities for conversation are infrequent, there can be a natural tendency to talk more once the chance finally arrives, simply because there is more built up to say.

Cultural and personal communication style. Some families and cultures have a naturally more expansive, storytelling-oriented conversational style, which can be misread as talking too much by people from a more clipped communication background.

When It Looks More Like ADHD

Excessive talking is a well recognized feature of ADHD, particularly the hyperactive-impulsive presentation, and it connects to the same impulse control differences that show up in other areas. Thoughts tend to arrive quickly and somewhat unfiltered, and the impulse to share them, a related idea, a tangent, a connected story, often outweighs the more effortful process of deciding whether now is the right moment or how much detail is actually needed.

A distinguishing feature is the difficulty stopping once started, even when the person is aware, in the moment, that they are talking more than the situation calls for. Many people describe watching themselves go off on a tangent, recognizing it as it happens, and still struggling to redirect back to the original point efficiently. This is different from simply enjoying talking, since it often comes with real, ongoing self-consciousness and frustration about the pattern rather than any lack of awareness.

This pattern also tends to be consistent across many different relationships and contexts, rather than being specific to one particular topic or one particular kind of excitement, and it is often present alongside other impulse-related patterns, like interrupting or blurting things out, which is a useful detail to notice.

What Can Help

Building in a brief pause before responding, deliberately asking yourself "does this need the full story, or just the short version" before launching in, and giving trusted people around you a light, agreed-upon signal to flag when you have drifted can all help without requiring you to suppress your personality or enthusiasm. Practicing summarizing a thought in one sentence before deciding whether to expand on it can also help build a more deliberate pause into the process.

Common Questions About Talking Too Much

Is talking a lot always a problem that needs addressing?

No. Talking a lot is often simply a personality trait or a reflection of genuine enthusiasm, and it is not inherently a problem. It becomes more worth exploring when it feels difficult to control, causes real distress, or affects relationships in ways the person does not want.

Why do I go off on so many tangents when telling a story?

This is a common ADHD-related pattern connected to how thoughts arrive quickly and somewhat unfiltered, with the impulse to share a connected idea often outweighing the more effortful process of staying on the original track.

Could this be about anxiety rather than excitement?

Yes, this is a genuine and different possibility. Some people talk more as a way of managing the discomfort of silence or social anxiety, which can look similar to enthusiastic talking from the outside but has a different underlying driver.

Is it normal to think out loud by talking?

Yes, for some people this is simply how their thinking process works best. It is not inherently a sign of anything clinical, though it can be worth being aware of in situations where more concise communication is expected.

Why do I notice myself talking too much but still struggle to stop?

This reflects an impulse control difficulty rather than a lack of self-awareness. Many people with ADHD are fully aware in the moment that they are talking more than needed and still find it genuinely difficult to redirect or stop efficiently.

Does talking too much always come with other impulsive behaviours?

Often, though not always. When excessive talking is connected to ADHD specifically, it frequently occurs alongside other impulse-related patterns, such as interrupting or blurting things out, rather than existing as an isolated trait.

Could a cultural or family communication style explain this?

Yes. Some families and cultures have a naturally more expansive, storytelling-oriented communication style, and this should not automatically be interpreted as excessive or a symptom of anything, particularly if it does not feel distressing or hard to control.

Can this pattern improve with therapy?

Yes, particularly when the underlying driver, whether anxiety, ADHD-related impulsivity, or something else, is identified and addressed directly rather than trying to simply talk less through willpower alone.

Is talking too much a sign of not listening well?

Not necessarily. Many people who talk a lot are still attentive, caring listeners; the pattern is more about difficulty regulating their own output than about a lack of interest in what others have to say.

What should I mention to a clinician if this is a concern for me?

It helps to describe how long the pattern has been present, whether it shows up across many different relationships and settings, whether it is accompanied by other impulsive behaviours, and how much self-awareness and difficulty stopping accompany it.
