What Happens After You Receive an Autism Diagnosis

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

After an autism diagnosis, most people receive a written report and a feedback session that explains the findings, describes strengths and challenges, and gives recommendations. What happens next depends on age and needs. For adults, it often means a period of reflection, learning about autism, adjusting expectations of oneself, and choosing whether to seek accommodations or therapy. For children, it may open access to funding, school supports, and therapies. A diagnosis does not change who you are. It offers a framework for understanding how you experience the world and what supports may help.

What to Expect

The feedback session. The clinician who completed the assessment usually meets with you to explain the diagnosis, how they reached it, and what it means for daily life. This is a good time to ask questions.

The written report. The report summarizes the assessment, results, diagnosis, and recommendations. It can be shared, with your consent, with a family doctor, school, employer, or other providers.

An emotional adjustment. Many adults describe relief, validation, and a clearer sense of self, sometimes mixed with grief for past struggles or frustration that the diagnosis came late. These feelings are common and often change over time, as discussed on the page about late diagnosis of autism.

Learning and connection. Many people find it helpful to read writing by autistic people and connect with autistic community groups, online or in person.

Practical Details

For children and youth in BC. A diagnosis from a qualified professional may allow families to apply for provincial autism funding through the Ministry of Children and Family Development, and to request supports at school. Funding programs and eligibility can change, so check current details with the ministry.

For adults. There is less dedicated funding, but a diagnosis can support requests for workplace or post-secondary accommodations, as explained on the page about accommodations for autistic adults at work. It may also support applications for some disability benefits, depending on the program.

Disclosure is your choice. You decide who to tell, if anyone. Some people share with family and close friends first, then decide about work later.

Co-occurring conditions. Autistic people often also experience anxiety, ADHD, depression, or sleep difficulties, as described on the page about autism and co-occurring anxiety. The report may recommend further assessment or treatment for these.

What Happens Next

  • Review the recommendations. Pick one or two that feel most useful to start with rather than trying to do everything at once.
  • Consider therapy if it would help. Neurodiversity-affirming therapy can support identity, self-acceptance, burnout recovery, and coping with anxiety. The page about finding an autism-informed therapist explains what to look for.
  • Adjust daily life. Small changes to sensory environments, routines, and energy use can make a big difference, as covered on the page about autism-friendly daily routines.
  • Build self-advocacy skills. Learning to explain your needs helps at work, in health care, and in relationships.
  • Share the report with your doctor. This can help with coordinated care.

For parents, it helps to focus on understanding your child's strengths and needs, connecting with other families, and choosing supports that respect your child's autistic identity.

Common Questions About Life After an Autism Diagnosis

What are the first steps once an autism assessment is complete?

You usually receive a written report and a feedback session explaining the autism diagnosis, strengths, challenges, and recommendations for next steps.

Is it normal to feel mixed emotions after an autism diagnosis as an adult?

Yes. Many adults feel relief and validation, along with grief or frustration about past struggles. These feelings are common and often shift over time.

Does an autism diagnosis give access to funding in BC?

For children and youth, an autism diagnosis may allow families to apply for provincial funding through the Ministry of Children and Family Development. Check current eligibility.

Can an autism diagnosis help me get accommodations at work?

Yes. An autism diagnosis can support requests for workplace or post-secondary accommodations, such as changes to lighting, noise, or communication.

Is disclosing autism at work required?

No. Disclosing an autism diagnosis is your choice, and you can decide who to tell and when.

Who can I share my autism assessment report with?

With your consent, you can share your autism assessment report with your doctor, school, employer, or other providers involved in your care.

Should I start therapy after being diagnosed with autism?

Therapy is optional after an autism diagnosis, but neurodiversity-affirming therapy can help with identity, burnout, anxiety, and self-acceptance.

Can autistic people also have ADHD or anxiety?

Yes. Autistic people often also experience ADHD, anxiety, depression, or sleep difficulties, and the report may recommend further assessment.

How can parents support a child after an autism diagnosis?

Parents can focus on their child's strengths and needs, connect with other families, and choose supports that respect the child's autistic identity.

Does an autism diagnosis change who I am?

No. An autism diagnosis does not change who you are. It gives a framework for understanding how you experience the world.
