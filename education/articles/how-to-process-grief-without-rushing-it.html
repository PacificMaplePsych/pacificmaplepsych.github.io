How to Process Grief Without Rushing It

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Processing grief without rushing it means allowing yourself to feel the loss as it comes, making space for both grief and daily life, and letting go of timelines about when you should feel better. Grief is a natural response to loss, and it does not follow a set schedule. Many people feel pressure to move on, whether from well-meaning friends, workplaces with short bereavement leave, or their own belief that they should be coping better by now. Pushing grief away often makes it resurface later. Giving it time and attention, in manageable doses, tends to help people carry their loss and gradually re-engage with life.

Why Grief Needs Time

Grief involves emotional, physical, and practical adjustment to a changed world. It often comes in waves, sometimes triggered by reminders and sometimes out of nowhere. Researchers describe a healthy pattern of moving back and forth between focusing on the loss, such as crying, remembering, and longing, and focusing on rebuilding, such as new routines and relationships. Both are needed. Grief does not move through neat stages, as explained on the page about grief and its stages.

When grief is rushed or avoided, it can show up in other ways, such as irritability, numbness, physical symptoms, or delayed waves of intense sadness. Allowing grief space helps the mind and body absorb the reality of the loss over time. Grief can also begin before a death, as described on the page about anticipatory grief.

Strategies to Try

1. Let Go of the Timeline

There is no correct amount of time to grieve. Intense grief often softens over months, but waves can return for years, especially around anniversaries.

2. Make Time for Grief

Some people find it helpful to set aside time for grief, such as looking at photos, writing, or visiting a meaningful place. This can make it easier to function the rest of the day.

3. Allow All Your Feelings

Sadness, anger, guilt, relief, numbness, and even moments of laughter are all part of grief. None of them mean you are grieving wrongly.

4. Express Grief in Your Own Way

Talking, writing, art, music, rituals, faith practices, or physical activity can all help. Some people grieve more through doing than through talking, and that is valid.

5. Take Breaks From Grief

Watching a show, spending time with friends, or focusing on work can offer relief. Taking breaks is part of healthy grieving, not avoidance.

6. Keep Basic Routines

Regular sleep, meals, and movement support the body through grief.

7. Tell People What You Need

Let others know if you want to talk about the person, prefer practical help, or need space. Many people want to help but are unsure how.

8. Delay Big Decisions

Where possible, wait before making major decisions, such as moving or selling belongings, until the early intensity of grief has eased.

9. Connect With Others Who Understand

Grief groups, described on the page about group therapy for grief, can reduce isolation.

Common Obstacles

Friends, family, or colleagues may expect you to be back to normal quickly, or avoid mentioning the loss. It can help to explain what you need, or to find people who can sit with your grief. You may also judge yourself for not coping better. Remind yourself that grief is a reflection of love and connection, not a problem to fix.

Some people find that they avoid grief entirely by staying busy, which can bring relief in the short term but lead to exhaustion or delayed grief later. Others feel stuck in intense grief for a long time. Balance, moving between grief and life, tends to help most. Once the early intensity has eased, some people find direction by reconnecting with what matters, as described on the page about building a values-based life after loss.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If grief feels as intense as it did early on for a long time, if you cannot function in daily life, if you feel hopeless, or if you are using alcohol or other substances to cope, grief counselling can help. Common Questions About Processing Grief at Your Own Pace

How long should grief take?

There is no correct length of time for grief. Intense grief often softens over months, but waves can return for years.

Why do people expect me to be over my grief already?

Others may not understand how long grief lasts, or may feel uncomfortable with it. Your grief does not need to follow their timeline.

Is it healthy to take breaks from grieving?

Yes. Moving between grieving and focusing on daily life is a healthy pattern, and breaks from grief are not avoidance.

What happens if I push my grief away?

Pushed-away grief often resurfaces later as irritability, numbness, physical symptoms, or delayed waves of sadness.

Is it normal to feel relief as well as grief after a death?

Yes. Relief is common, especially after a long illness, and it can exist alongside deep grief and love.

Should I set aside time each day to grieve?

Some people find planned time for grief, such as writing or looking at photos, makes it easier to function the rest of the day.

What if I grieve through doing things rather than talking?

Grieving through activity, such as projects, exercise, or rituals, is valid. There is no single right way to express grief.

How can I tell friends what I need while I am grieving?

Let friends know whether you want to talk about the person who died, prefer practical help, or need space. Most people want guidance.

Should I wait to make big decisions while grieving?

Where possible, waiting until the early intensity of grief eases before major decisions can help you choose more clearly.

When should I get help if my grief is not easing?

If grief stays intense for a long time, makes daily life very hard, or brings hopelessness, grief counselling can help.
