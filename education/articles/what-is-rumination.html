What Is Rumination

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Rumination is a pattern of repetitive, passive thinking about problems, mistakes, feelings, or their causes and consequences, without moving toward a solution. The word comes from the way some animals chew their food over and over. People who ruminate may replay a difficult conversation, dwell on why they feel bad, or keep asking questions such as why did this happen or what is wrong with me. Everyone ruminates sometimes, especially after upsetting events. When rumination becomes frequent or prolonged, it is closely linked with depression and anxiety, and it can keep low mood going. Rumination is a habit of mind that can be changed.

What Rumination Looks Like

  • Replaying conversations over and over.
  • Dwelling on past mistakes, regrets, or losses, or being unable to stop thinking about the past.
  • Repeatedly asking why you feel the way you do.
  • Comparing how things are with how they should be.
  • Spending long periods lost in thought, especially at night.
  • Feeling worse, not better, after thinking something through.
  • Difficulty concentrating on the present or enjoying activities.

Rumination is related to worry, but the two differ in focus. Rumination tends to look backward, at what has happened or what it means. Worry tends to look forward, at what might happen. Both are forms of repetitive negative thinking and can occur together, often alongside overthinking.

Why It Happens

Rumination often starts as an attempt to understand or solve a problem. It can feel productive, as if thinking harder will bring clarity or prevent future mistakes. In reality, repetitive dwelling rarely leads to solutions and tends to magnify negative feelings. Research by psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema found that people who respond to low mood by ruminating are more likely to experience longer and more severe depression.

Several factors increase rumination. These include perfectionism, self-criticism, a history of difficult experiences, stress, and beliefs that rumination is useful. Low mood itself narrows attention toward negative memories, which fuels further dwelling. Rumination also keeps people from engaging in activities that could lift mood, creating a cycle. It often pairs with catastrophizing, where thoughts jump to worst-case outcomes.

Rumination can affect sleep in particular. Lying in bed without distractions gives the mind space to replay the day, which can delay sleep and leave people tired and more vulnerable to low mood the next day. Winding down with a calming routine and writing thoughts down before bed can help interrupt this pattern.

What It Is Not

  • Not the same as healthy reflection. Reflection is curious, flexible, and leads to insight or action. Rumination is repetitive and stuck.
  • Not a character flaw. It is a common thinking habit that can be changed.
  • Not always a sign of depression, though it is a major risk factor and often part of it.
  • Not solved by trying not to think. Suppressing thoughts often makes them return more strongly.

How It Is Addressed and Treated

Several approaches help. Cognitive behavioural therapy, including rumination-focused CBT, helps people notice when they are ruminating, understand its triggers, and shift toward more concrete, problem-solving thinking. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy teaches people to observe thoughts without getting caught up in them and is effective in preventing depression relapse. Behavioural activation encourages engaging in meaningful activities rather than staying stuck in thought. Practical strategies include setting aside a brief thinking time, asking whether a thought leads to action, taking a short walk, or turning attention to the senses. Treating underlying depression or anxiety often reduces rumination.

Common Questions About Rumination

How can I tell helpful reflection from rumination?

Reflection is curious and leads to insight or action. Rumination is repetitive and passive, and tends to make you feel worse.

How is rumination different from worry?

Rumination usually focuses on the past and its meaning, while worry focuses on possible future threats. Both are repetitive negative thinking.

Why does my mind keep going over the same things?

Rumination can feel like problem-solving, and low mood draws attention toward negative memories, keeping the loop going.

Why does dwelling on problems make low mood last longer?

Research links rumination with longer and more severe depression, because dwelling magnifies negative feelings and crowds out mood-lifting activity.

Why do I ruminate more at night?

At night there are fewer distractions, so the mind has more room to replay the day and dwell on problems.

Does trying not to think about something stop rumination?

Usually not. Suppressing thoughts often makes them come back more strongly. Redirecting attention works better.

What is rumination-focused CBT?

It is a form of cognitive behavioural therapy that targets repetitive thinking, helping people shift toward concrete, helpful thinking and action.

How does mindfulness help with rumination?

Mindfulness helps people notice thoughts as passing mental events and step back from them rather than getting caught up.

What quick strategies can interrupt a rumination loop?

Try a short walk, focusing on your senses, asking whether the thought leads to action, or scheduling a limited time to think it through.

Does going over problems for hours affect sleep and energy?

Yes. Prolonged rumination can keep stress responses active, affecting sleep, energy, and overall wellbeing.
