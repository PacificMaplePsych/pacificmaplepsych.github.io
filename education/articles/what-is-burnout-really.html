What Is Burnout, Really

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Burnout has become a widely used word, often describing any kind of tiredness or stress. In its more precise sense, burnout is a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. The World Health Organization describes it as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical condition, with three core dimensions: exhaustion, increasing mental distance or cynicism toward work, and reduced professional effectiveness. Burnout develops gradually, often in people who care deeply about their work. Understanding what burnout really is can help people recognize it early and address both personal and workplace factors.

What Burnout Looks Like

  • Exhaustion. Feeling drained physically and emotionally, with little energy left for work or life.
  • Cynicism and detachment. Feeling cynical about your job, irritable with colleagues, or detached from work you once cared about.
  • Reduced effectiveness. Feeling less competent, making more mistakes, or struggling to accomplish tasks.
  • Dreading work, especially on Sunday evenings.
  • Sleep problems, headaches, or getting sick more often.
  • Withdrawing from friends, hobbies, and family.
  • Relying on caffeine, alcohol, or screens to get through the day.

Burnout often builds so slowly that people do not notice until they feel they have nothing left. People who are driven and conscientious may push harder rather than slowing down, as described on the page about burnout in high achievers.

Why It Happens

Research on burnout, much of it led by psychologist Christina Maslach, points to six key areas of work life. Burnout becomes more likely when there is a mismatch in any of them: workload that is too high, lack of control, insufficient reward or recognition, breakdown of community and support, unfairness, and conflict between personal values and the job. This means burnout is not only about individual resilience. It is strongly shaped by the conditions and culture of work. The page on workplace stress explores these factors further.

Certain roles carry higher risk, including health care, teaching, social services, and other helping professions, as explored on the page about burnout in helping professions. Personal factors, such as perfectionism, difficulty setting boundaries, and a strong sense of duty, can increase vulnerability, especially when combined with demanding environments.

The word burnout is also used outside of work, for example to describe parental burnout or caregiver burnout, which share features of exhaustion and detachment in response to long-term demands. Autistic burnout is a related but distinct experience involving loss of skills and sensory tolerance.

What It Is Not

  • Not ordinary tiredness. Burnout involves exhaustion plus cynicism and reduced effectiveness, and a weekend off does not fix it.
  • Not depression, though they overlap. Depression affects all areas of life, while burnout is tied mainly to work. Burnout can lead to depression.
  • Not a personal failing. It is often a reasonable response to unreasonable conditions.
  • Not solved by self-care alone. Changes to workload, control, or support are usually needed.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Recovery from burnout usually involves both reducing demands and restoring resources. This can include taking time off, adjusting workload or responsibilities, setting boundaries around hours and availability, and protecting sleep, movement, and time with people outside work. Talking with a manager or human resources about workload and support can help. Therapy can help people understand what led to burnout, address perfectionism or difficulty saying no, reconnect with values, and make decisions about their work. If depression or anxiety has developed, it can be treated alongside burnout. Sometimes recovery involves a larger change, such as a new role or career direction.

Common Questions About Burnout

Is burnout just another word for being tired?

No. The World Health Organization describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon from chronic, unmanaged workplace stress, involving exhaustion, cynicism, and reduced effectiveness.

Is burnout a medical diagnosis?

No. Burnout is described as an occupational phenomenon rather than a medical condition, though it can affect health.

What are the three dimensions of burnout?

Exhaustion, increasing mental distance or cynicism toward work, and reduced professional effectiveness.

What workplace factors lead to burnout?

Research highlights workload, control, reward, community, fairness, and values as key areas where mismatches lead to burnout.

How is burnout different from depression?

Burnout is tied mainly to work, while depression affects all areas of life. Burnout can lead to depression if it continues.

Why does a vacation not fix my burnout?

Burnout develops from ongoing conditions, so rest helps briefly but symptoms return if the underlying causes stay the same.

How long does burnout recovery take?

It varies from weeks to many months, depending on severity and how much the underlying conditions change.

Can you have burnout outside of work?

The term is also used for parental and caregiver burnout, which share features of exhaustion and detachment.

Should I quit my job if I am burned out?

Not necessarily. Adjusting workload, boundaries, and support may help, though some people decide a change is needed.

What does recovery from burnout look like in therapy?

It often involves understanding what led to burnout, learning to set boundaries, addressing perfectionism, and making decisions about work.
