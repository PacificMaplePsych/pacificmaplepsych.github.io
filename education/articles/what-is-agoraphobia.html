What Is Agoraphobia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Agoraphobia is an anxiety disorder involving intense fear of situations where escape might be difficult or help might not be available if something goes wrong, such as having a panic attack or feeling faint. People with agoraphobia often avoid public transit, crowds, open spaces, enclosed spaces like shops or theatres, and being away from home alone. It is not simply fear of open spaces or of going outside, though in severe cases people may become housebound. Agoraphobia is treatable, and many people regain their freedom with therapy.

What Agoraphobia Looks Like

Agoraphobia usually involves fear in two or more of these situations:

  • Using public transit, such as buses, SkyTrain, ferries, or planes.
  • Being in open spaces, such as parking lots or bridges.
  • Being in enclosed spaces, such as shops, cinemas, or elevators.
  • Standing in line or being in a crowd.
  • Being outside the home alone.

People may only go out with a trusted companion, stick to familiar routes, sit near exits, or carry items that make them feel safe. At work, agoraphobia can limit commuting or travel. At home, it can shrink life to a small radius. Relationships can be strained when a partner becomes the only safe companion.

Why It Happens

Agoraphobia often develops after panic attacks. After an unexpected attack in a particular place, people begin to fear having another where escape would be difficult or embarrassing, and start avoiding those places. Avoidance brings relief, which reinforces the fear, and the list of avoided places tends to grow. Contributing factors include genetics, an anxious temperament, stressful life events, and sensitivity to physical sensations such as a racing heart or dizziness. Agoraphobia can also develop without a history of panic attacks, centred on fears of fainting, losing bladder control, or other symptoms.

What It Is Not

  • Not just fear of open spaces. The core fear is being unable to escape or get help, which can apply to many kinds of places.
  • Not social anxiety. Social anxiety centres on fear of judgment. Agoraphobia centres on fear of panic or incapacitating symptoms in places hard to leave.
  • Not a specific phobia. Specific phobias focus on one object or situation. Agoraphobia spans several situations linked by the same fear.
  • Not PTSD-related avoidance. Avoiding reminders of trauma is a different pattern, though the two can coexist.
  • Not a choice. Avoidance feels necessary, not optional, for people with agoraphobia, which is why avoiding nervous situations becomes self-reinforcing.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

A psychologist or physician assesses agoraphobia by asking about feared situations, panic attacks, avoidance, and the effect on daily life, and by ruling out medical causes of symptoms. Cognitive behavioural therapy is the main treatment. It helps people understand panic, reduce safety behaviours, and gradually face avoided situations through exposure. Interoceptive exposure, deliberately bringing on harmless physical sensations, can reduce fear of the body's signals. Medication prescribed by a physician may also help some people. Virtual therapy can be a useful starting point for those who find leaving home very difficult.

Common Questions About Agoraphobia

What does agoraphobia actually mean?

Agoraphobia is fear of situations where escape might be difficult or help unavailable if panic or other symptoms occur. It leads people to avoid places like transit, crowds, or being away from home alone.

Is agoraphobia the same as being afraid to leave the house?

Not always. Being housebound is a severe form, but many people with agoraphobia leave home while avoiding specific places or only going out with a companion.

How are panic attacks connected to agoraphobia?

Many people develop agoraphobia after panic attacks, fearing another attack in places where escape would be hard or embarrassing, and avoiding those places.

Can agoraphobia develop without panic attacks?

Yes. Some people fear other symptoms, such as fainting or losing bladder control, in situations where help might not be available.

Why do I feel safer going out with a trusted person?

A companion can feel like a source of help if something goes wrong. Over time, relying on them can keep the fear going, so therapy works toward gradual independence.

What are safety behaviours in agoraphobia?

Safety behaviours include sitting near exits, carrying water or medication, or only going out with someone. They feel protective but prevent learning that you can cope.

What does CBT for agoraphobia involve week to week?

Cognitive behavioural therapy with gradual exposure is the main treatment. It may include interoceptive exposure and, for some people, medication prescribed by a physician.

Is online therapy a good first step if agoraphobia keeps me at home?

Yes. Virtual sessions can be a helpful way to start treatment, with in-person or real-world exposure added as confidence grows.

Is agoraphobia common?

Agoraphobia is less common than some other anxiety disorders but affects a meaningful number of people. It often begins in late adolescence or early adulthood.

Can people fully recover from agoraphobia?

Many people improve substantially with treatment and regain the ability to travel and go out independently, though progress is often gradual.
