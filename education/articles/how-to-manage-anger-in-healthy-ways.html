How to Manage Anger in Healthy Ways

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Managing anger in healthy ways means noticing anger early, calming the body before reacting, understanding what the anger is about, and expressing it clearly and respectfully rather than exploding or holding it in. Anger is a normal emotion. It often signals that a boundary has been crossed, a need is unmet, or something feels unfair. It can be useful, motivating people to stand up for themselves or address problems. Anger becomes a concern when it is frequent, intense, or expressed in ways that hurt others or yourself, such as yelling, threats, breaking things, or turning it inward. With practice, people can learn to respond to anger with more control and less harm.

Why Managing Anger Helps

When anger rises, the body activates the stress response. Heart rate and muscle tension increase, and the thinking parts of the brain can go offline, making it harder to consider consequences. Acting in this state often leads to words or actions people regret. Anger is also frequently a surface emotion covering other feelings, such as hurt, fear, shame, or sadness.

Anger can be shaped by many factors, including stress, sleep deprivation, pain, burnout, depression, ADHD, trauma, and substance use. For some people, anger is part of a trauma response, a protective reaction learned in unsafe environments. Learning to manage anger helps protect relationships, health, and self-respect. It also builds emotional regulation skills that help with many other feelings.

Strategies to Try

1. Learn Your Early Warning Signs

Notice physical signs, such as a hot face, clenched jaw, or tight fists, and thoughts such as "they always do this." Catching anger early makes it easier to manage.

2. Take a Time-Out

When anger rises quickly, step away for at least twenty minutes. Let the other person know you will come back, such as "I need a break so I do not say something I regret. Let us talk after dinner."

3. Calm Your Body

Slow breathing, walking, splashing cold water on your face, or tensing and releasing muscles can lower physical arousal. The page about grounding techniques offers more ideas.

4. Look Beneath the Anger

Ask what else you might be feeling, such as hurt, disrespected, scared, or exhausted. Naming these feelings often reduces the intensity of anger.

5. Challenge Hot Thoughts

Thoughts such as "they did it on purpose" or "this is completely unfair" can fuel anger. Consider other explanations and whether the situation will matter in a week.

6. Express Anger Assertively

Use calm, clear I statements, such as "I felt frustrated when the plans changed without telling me. I would like a heads-up next time." Focus on the behaviour, not attacking the person. The page about conflict styles offers more ideas for relationships.

7. Address Ongoing Stressors

If anger stems from recurring problems, such as workload or unfair treatment, problem-solving and setting boundaries can reduce triggers.

8. Look After the Basics

Sleep, regular meals, movement, and limiting alcohol reduce irritability.

Common Obstacles

Anger can feel powerful and even satisfying in the moment, which makes it hard to change. Noticing the costs, such as damaged relationships or regret, can motivate change. Some people grew up in homes where anger was expressed through yelling or violence, or where anger was not allowed at all. Both can make healthy expression hard to learn. Others hold anger in until it bursts out, or turn it inward, leading to self-criticism or depression.

Stress, pain, burnout, and conditions such as ADHD or depression can lower tolerance and make anger harder to manage. Treating these can reduce anger too. Alcohol and drugs can lower inhibitions and increase aggression.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If anger feels out of control, happens often, damages relationships or work, or involves threats, intimidation, or physical aggression, professional support is important. Therapy, including cognitive behavioural approaches and emotion regulation skills, can help people understand and manage anger. When anger is linked with trauma, trauma-focused therapy may help. If you are worried you might harm someone, step away and seek help right away. If someone is in immediate danger, call 911. If you are experiencing anger or violence from a partner, support is available through abuse resources.

Common Questions About Managing Anger

Should I be trying to get rid of my anger completely?

No. Anger is normal and can signal unmet needs or crossed boundaries. The aim is to manage how it is expressed, not to eliminate it.

How do I calm down quickly when I am angry?

Step away, breathe slowly, move your body, or splash cold water on your face to lower physical arousal.

What does my body do in the moments before I snap at someone?

Many people notice a hot face, clenched jaw, tight fists, or a racing heart, along with thoughts like they always do this.

How long should I take a break when I am angry?

At least twenty minutes often helps the body calm down, with a plan to return to the conversation.

Why is anger often covering other feelings?

Anger can protect against more vulnerable emotions such as hurt, fear, shame, or sadness.

How can I express anger without yelling?

Use calm I statements, focus on the behaviour, and say what you would like to happen next.

Can past trauma make me quick to anger?

Yes. Anger can be part of a trauma response, a protective reaction learned in unsafe situations.

Does alcohol make anger worse?

Alcohol can lower inhibitions and increase the risk of aggressive reactions.

Why do I hold anger in until I explode?

Suppressing anger can let it build until it bursts out. Learning to express it earlier and calmly helps.

My temper is scaring people I love. Is it time to see someone?

Yes. If anger feels out of control, happens often, damages relationships, or involves threats or aggression, professional support is important.
