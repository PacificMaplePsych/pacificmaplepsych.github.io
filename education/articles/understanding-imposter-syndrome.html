Understanding Imposter Syndrome

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Imposter syndrome describes a persistent belief that you are less capable than others think and that you will eventually be exposed as a fraud, despite clear evidence of your abilities. It is not a formal diagnosis, but it is a well-studied pattern first described by psychologists in the late 1970s. It often affects high-achieving people and can appear at work, in school, in parenting, in creative pursuits, and in new roles. It is closely linked with perfectionism and anxiety and can be eased with understanding and practice.

For a closer look at how this shows up on the job, see the page on feeling like an impostor at work.

What Imposter Syndrome Looks Like

Common features include:

  • Attributing success to luck, timing, or other people rather than your own ability.
  • Fearing that others will discover you are not as competent as they think.
  • Discounting praise and struggling to accept compliments.
  • Over-preparing, over-working, or procrastinating out of fear of being found out.
  • Feeling that achievements do not count because they came too easily or took too much effort.
  • Avoiding new opportunities in case they reveal your limitations.

At home, a new parent might feel like everyone else knows what they are doing. In school, a graduate student might feel admitted by mistake. In creative work, someone might feel their success is a fluke that will not repeat.

Why It Happens

Imposter feelings often follow a cycle. A new task triggers anxiety and self-doubt, which leads to over-preparation or procrastination followed by a last-minute rush. When the task goes well, success is attributed to effort or luck rather than ability, so confidence does not grow, and the cycle repeats. Contributing factors include perfectionism, family messages that emphasized achievement or comparison, being the first in your family to reach a certain level, and environments where you are underrepresented. Transitions, such as a promotion or new field, commonly trigger it. People who feel they have to be perfect are especially prone.

What It Is Not

  • Not realistic self-assessment. Sometimes people are genuinely new to a skill and still learning. Imposter syndrome persists even when evidence of competence is strong.
  • Not only individual. Workplace bias, exclusion, or lack of support can create real barriers and feelings of not belonging. These deserve attention beyond individual mindset.
  • Not the same as low self-esteem overall. Many people with imposter feelings feel confident in other areas. However, it can overlap with a broader sense of never being good enough.
  • Not a diagnosis. It can occur alongside anxiety, depression, or ADHD, which may need their own support.

How It Is Assessed and Addressed

Because imposter syndrome is not a diagnosis, there is no formal test, although questionnaires can help people recognize the pattern. A therapist can explore related anxiety, perfectionism, and self-esteem. Helpful approaches often include noticing and naming imposter thoughts, keeping a record of accomplishments and feedback, talking openly with trusted colleagues or mentors, reframing mistakes as part of learning, and challenging all-or-nothing standards. Cognitive behavioural approaches can help shift beliefs about competence, and self-compassion practices can reduce the fear of failure.

Common Questions About Imposter Syndrome

What is imposter syndrome in simple terms?

Imposter syndrome is feeling like a fraud who does not deserve their success, and fearing that others will find out, even when there is clear evidence of competence.

Is imposter syndrome a mental illness?

No. It is not a formal diagnosis, but it is a recognized pattern that can cause real distress and often occurs alongside anxiety, perfectionism, or depression.

What is the imposter cycle?

The imposter cycle begins with anxiety about a task, leads to over-preparation or procrastination, and ends with success attributed to effort or luck, so self-doubt returns next time.

Who is most likely to experience imposter syndrome?

It is common among high achievers, people in new roles, first-generation students or professionals, and people underrepresented in their field, though anyone can experience it.

Can imposter syndrome happen outside of work?

Yes. It can show up in school, parenting, creative work, relationships, and any area where people feel they should be more capable than they are.

Why don't achievements make imposter feelings go away?

People with imposter feelings tend to explain success away as luck or effort, so achievements do not update their belief about their ability.

Why do perfectionists so often feel like frauds?

Perfectionism sets impossibly high standards, so anything less feels like failure. This fuels the fear of being exposed and the discounting of success.

Can workplace bias contribute to imposter feelings?

Yes. Being underrepresented, overlooked, or stereotyped can create real doubts about belonging. These external factors deserve attention along with individual strategies.

How can I start overcoming imposter syndrome?

Notice and name imposter thoughts, record your accomplishments, share your feelings with trusted people, accept mistakes as part of learning, and practise self-compassion.

What does therapy for imposter feelings involve?

Therapy may explore where imposter beliefs began, address perfectionism and anxiety, and use cognitive and self-compassion approaches to build a more accurate view of your abilities.
