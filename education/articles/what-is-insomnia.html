What Is Insomnia

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Insomnia is a sleep disorder in which a person has persistent difficulty falling asleep, staying asleep, or waking too early and being unable to get back to sleep, despite having the opportunity to sleep. It affects daytime life, causing fatigue, low mood, irritability, or trouble concentrating. Short-term insomnia is very common during stress, illness, or change. When sleep problems occur at least three nights a week for three months or longer, it is called chronic insomnia. Insomnia is highly treatable, most effectively with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I).

What Insomnia Looks Like

  • Lying awake for a long time before falling asleep.
  • Waking during the night and struggling to return to sleep.
  • Waking much earlier than planned.
  • Feeling unrefreshed even after spending enough time in bed.
  • Being unable to sleep even when exhausted.
  • Worrying about sleep during the day and dreading bedtime.
  • Daytime tiredness, low mood, irritability, or poor focus.

Many people with insomnia describe feeling tired but wired: physically exhausted yet mentally alert, especially once their head hits the pillow.

Why It Happens

Sleep researchers often describe insomnia using three kinds of factors. Predisposing factors make someone more vulnerable, such as a tendency to worry, a sensitive stress response, or family history. Precipitating factors trigger the sleep problem, such as a stressful event, illness, pain, a new baby, shift work, or grief. Perpetuating factors keep insomnia going long after the trigger has passed. These include spending extra time in bed to catch up, napping, irregular schedules, using screens in bed, and worrying about not sleeping.

Over time, the bed can become linked with frustration and alertness rather than rest, a process called conditioned arousal. Efforts to force sleep tend to backfire, because sleep comes more easily when the mind and body are relaxed rather than striving. Insomnia also often occurs alongside anxiety, depression, chronic pain, and other conditions, and each can worsen the other. What It Is Not

  • Not simply poor sleep habits. Habits play a role, but insomnia involves the nervous system and learned patterns.
  • Not the same as short sleep by choice. Insomnia means sleep does not come even with the chance to sleep.
  • Not sleep apnea, though the two can co-occur. Loud snoring, gasping, or pauses in breathing need medical assessment.
  • Not a delayed body clock alone. Some people fall asleep late but sleep well once they do. That pattern is a circadian rhythm issue, which is treated differently.
  • Not best treated long term with sleeping pills. Medications may help briefly but do not address the underlying patterns.

Loud snoring, gasping or choking during sleep, or falling asleep while driving should be checked by a doctor promptly. Avoid driving when you are very drowsy.

How It Is Assessed and Treated

Assessment usually involves a sleep history and a sleep diary kept for one to two weeks, recording bedtime, wake time, time awake, and daytime effects. A physician may check for medical causes, medications, or other sleep disorders. CBT-I is the recommended first treatment for chronic insomnia. It combines stimulus control, which means using the bed only for sleep and getting up when unable to sleep, with sleep restriction, which temporarily limits time in bed to strengthen sleep drive. It also includes consistent wake times, relaxation, and changing unhelpful thoughts about sleep. Most people see improvement within six to eight sessions, and gains tend to last.

Common Questions About Insomnia

What counts as chronic insomnia?

Chronic insomnia means trouble sleeping at least three nights a week for three months or more, with effects on daytime functioning.

What is CBT-I?

CBT-I is a structured therapy for insomnia that uses techniques such as stimulus control, sleep restriction, and changing sleep-related thoughts.

Why do I feel tired but wired at bedtime?

Stress and conditioned arousal can keep the mind alert even when the body is exhausted, making it hard to switch off.

What does stimulus control involve for insomnia?

It means using the bed only for sleep, getting up after about twenty minutes awake, doing something calm elsewhere, and returning when sleepy.

Is it helpful to nap when I have insomnia?

Napping can reduce sleep drive at night. Many people with insomnia sleep better when they avoid or limit naps.

How long does CBT-I take to work?

Many people notice improvement within a few weeks, and a typical course runs six to eight sessions.

Can insomnia be caused by anxiety?

Yes. Anxiety and worry are common triggers, and insomnia can in turn increase anxiety, creating a cycle.

What role does a physician play in treating insomnia?

A physician can check for medical causes and other sleep disorders, review medications, and discuss short-term medication options alongside CBT-I.

Why does trying harder to sleep make insomnia worse?

Sleep is a relaxed process. Effort and frustration raise arousal, which pushes sleep further away.

What is sleep restriction therapy?

Sleep restriction temporarily limits time in bed to match actual sleep time, building sleep drive and consolidating sleep. Time in bed is then gradually increased.
