Types of Therapy: A Plain-Language Guide to Choosing

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Most types of therapy fall into a few broad families: cognitive and behavioural therapies, which focus on thoughts and actions; acceptance- and mindfulness-based therapies; psychodynamic and insight-oriented therapies, which explore patterns rooted in past experience; humanistic and emotion-focused therapies; trauma-focused therapies; and therapies for couples and families. Choosing well usually means matching the approach to your concern, your goals, and how you like to work. Research also shows that the relationship with your therapist matters a great deal, whichever approach you choose. Many therapists blend methods, so you do not need to pick the perfect label before starting.

How the Main Types Work

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). A structured, skills-based approach that helps you notice and change unhelpful thoughts and behaviours. It has strong evidence for anxiety, depression, OCD, insomnia, and more. See the page about CBT.

Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT). Teaches skills for managing intense emotions, distress, and relationships. It was developed for borderline personality disorder and is used more broadly. See the page about DBT.

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT). Helps you make room for difficult thoughts and feelings while acting on your values. See the page about ACT.

Exposure-based therapies. Including exposure and response prevention for OCD and exposure for phobias and panic, these involve gradually facing fears.

Psychodynamic therapy. Explores how past experiences, relationships, and unconscious patterns shape the present. See the page about psychodynamic therapy.

Humanistic and emotion-focused therapies. Person-centred and emotion-focused approaches emphasize empathy, emotional awareness, and personal growth.

Trauma-focused therapies. Cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, and EMDR are well researched for PTSD. See the page about trauma-informed therapy.

Other approaches. Schema therapy, internal family systems, compassion-focused therapy, solution-focused therapy, and couples and family approaches each suit particular needs.

Practical Details

Structured or open-ended. CBT, DBT, and exposure therapies tend to be structured, with homework and clear goals. Psychodynamic and humanistic therapies are often more exploratory.

Length. Structured therapies may last a few months for a specific concern. Work on long-standing patterns usually takes longer.

Format. Most approaches can be delivered individually, in groups, or virtually. Couples and family approaches involve more than one person.

Integrative practice. Many therapists draw on several approaches, tailoring them to the person.

Cost and coverage. Coverage depends on your benefits plan and the type of provider, not usually the type of therapy.

How to Choose

  • Start with your concern. For OCD, ERP is the leading treatment. For PTSD, trauma-focused therapies are recommended. For intense emotions and self-harm urges, DBT is often a good fit. For anxiety and depression, CBT and ACT are strong options.
  • Think about your style. Do you want practical tools and homework, or space to explore and understand patterns?
  • Consider your goals. Symptom relief, understanding yourself, improving relationships, or all three.
  • Ask potential therapists. How they work, what approach they would suggest for you, and why.
  • Pay attention to fit. Feeling understood and respected by your therapist is one of the strongest predictors of benefit.
  • Stay open to adjusting. If one approach is not helping after a fair trial, talk with your therapist about changing course.

Common Questions About Choosing a Type of Therapy

What are the main types of therapy?

The main types of therapy include cognitive behavioural, acceptance-based, psychodynamic, humanistic and emotion-focused, trauma-focused, and couples and family therapies.

How do I choose the right type of therapy for me?

Match the therapy to your concern, goals, and preferred style, and ask potential therapists what approach they would suggest and why.

Which type of therapy is best for anxiety and depression?

CBT has strong evidence for anxiety and depression, and ACT and other approaches also help many people. The best therapy depends on the person.

What is the difference between structured and exploratory therapy?

Structured therapy, such as CBT, uses clear goals and homework. Exploratory therapy, such as psychodynamic therapy, focuses on understanding patterns and their roots.

Does the type of therapy matter more than the therapist?

Both matter. The therapy approach should fit the concern, and research shows the relationship with the therapist is one of the strongest predictors of benefit.

What is integrative therapy?

Integrative therapy combines methods from several approaches, tailored to the person's needs, rather than following a single model.

Which therapy approaches are recommended for PTSD?

Trauma-focused therapies such as cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure, and EMDR are well-researched approaches for PTSD.

Who besides people with BPD can benefit from DBT?

DBT was developed for borderline personality disorder, but its skills are used more broadly for intense emotions, self-harm urges, and relationship difficulties.

Can I switch therapy approaches if one is not helping?

Yes. If a therapy approach is not helping after a fair trial, talk with your therapist about adjusting or trying a different method.

Should I pick a therapy approach before my first appointment?

No. A therapist can help you decide which therapy approach fits your concern during an initial consultation.
