Co-Parenting Strategies After Separation

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Co-parenting after separation means sharing the responsibilities of raising children with a former partner while living apart. The strategies that help most are keeping children out of adult conflict, communicating respectfully and briefly about child-related matters, keeping routines and rules reasonably consistent across homes, and supporting the child's relationship with both parents when it is safe to do so. Separation is a major change for children, and many adjust well over time, especially when conflict between parents is low. Co-parenting does not require friendship. It requires a shared focus on the children's wellbeing.

Why These Strategies Help

Research consistently shows that ongoing, intense conflict between parents is one of the biggest risks to children's wellbeing after separation, more than the separation itself. Children who are exposed to arguments, asked to carry messages, or made to feel they must choose sides often experience anxiety, sadness, guilt, and divided loyalties. Some children take on adult worries or try to care for a parent, a pattern described on the page about parentification.

When parents keep conflict away from children, communicate clearly, and provide stable routines, children feel more secure. They can love both parents without guilt and focus on being children. Consistency also reduces confusion and helps children adjust to living in two homes.

Strategies to Try

1. Keep Children Out of the Middle

Avoid arguing in front of children, asking them to pass messages, or questioning them about the other home. Do not speak negatively about the other parent in their hearing.

2. Communicate Like Business Partners

Keep communication brief, informative, friendly, and firm. Focus on logistics and the children's needs. Some parents use co-parenting apps, shared calendars, or email to reduce conflict and keep records.

3. Create a Clear Parenting Plan

A written plan covering schedules, holidays, decision-making, and how changes will be handled reduces uncertainty. Family mediators and family justice services can help create one.

4. Keep Routines Consistent Where Possible

Similar bedtimes, homework expectations, and rules across homes help children feel secure. Perfect consistency is not necessary, but major differences can be discussed.

5. Make Transitions Smooth

Prepare children for changeovers, keep handoffs calm and brief, and allow them to bring comfort items between homes.

6. Support the Child's Relationship With the Other Parent

Let children know it is okay to love and enjoy time with both parents, when this is safe.

7. Listen to Your Children

Give children space to share feelings about the separation without judgment. Reassure them the separation is not their fault. Older children and teens may want some say in schedules.

8. Look After Yourself

Separation brings grief, stress, and change for parents too. Support from friends, family, or a therapist helps you stay steady for your children.

Common Obstacles

Strong emotions such as anger, hurt, and grief can make calm communication hard, especially soon after separation. It can help to pause before replying to messages, write drafts, or ask a trusted person to review them. When the other parent does not cooperate, parallel parenting, where each parent manages their own home with minimal direct contact, may reduce conflict.

Differences in parenting styles are common. Focusing on major issues, such as safety and health, and letting go of minor differences can reduce tension. Children may react to separation with anger, sadness, clinginess, or behaviour changes. The pages about anxiety in children and teens and signs a child may need support can help you know when to seek help. Managing two homes, work, and emotions can also lead to parental burnout.

Co-parenting is not appropriate when there has been family violence, abuse, or coercive control. In these situations, safety comes first, and arrangements may need to be made through legal processes. Support is available through abuse resources and safety planning information. If anyone is in immediate danger, call 911.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If conflict remains high, communication breaks down, or children show ongoing distress, professional support can help. Family mediators can help parents reach agreements. Parenting coordinators may help in high-conflict situations. Therapy can support parents coping with separation and help children adjust. In BC, family justice services and legal aid can provide information about parenting arrangements and legal options.

Common Questions About Co-Parenting

What is the most important thing for children after their parents separate?

Keeping children out of ongoing conflict and helping them feel secure and loved by both parents, when safe, matters most.

How should I communicate with my co-parent when we do not get along?

Keep messages brief, informative, friendly, and focused on the children. Written communication can reduce conflict.

What is parallel parenting?

It is an approach where each parent manages their own home with minimal direct contact, often used in high-conflict situations.

When co-parenting after separation, should the rules be the same in both homes?

Similar routines help children feel secure, but perfect consistency is not necessary. Focus on major issues.

How can I make handoffs easier for my children?

Prepare them ahead of time, keep changeovers calm and brief, and let them bring comfort items between homes.

What should I tell my children about the separation?

Use honest, age-appropriate language, reassure them it is not their fault, and avoid blaming the other parent.

Is it okay to vent to my children about my ex?

No. Speaking negatively about the other parent can create guilt and divided loyalties. Share your feelings with other adults.

Where can I get help creating a parenting plan in BC?

Family mediators, family justice services, and legal aid can help parents create parenting plans.

When is co-parenting not safe?

When there has been family violence, abuse, or coercive control, safety comes first and arrangements may need legal processes.

How do I know if my child needs therapy after our separation?

Ongoing distress, behaviour changes, withdrawal, or difficulties at school may be signs a child could benefit from support.
