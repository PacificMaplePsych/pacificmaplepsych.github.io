Why Do I Feel So Anxious About Money

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Your stomach drops when you open your banking app, so you avoid opening it. Bills sit unopened on the counter. You lie awake calculating what you owe, or you feel guilty every time you spend money on yourself, even when you can afford it. Some people with money anxiety are genuinely struggling to make ends meet. Others are financially stable on paper but still feel a constant sense of scarcity.

Money is one of the most common sources of stress, particularly with the high cost of living in places like Vancouver. Understanding what sits behind your money anxiety can help you tell the difference between a problem that needs a practical plan and a worry that needs a different kind of support.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Real financial pressure. Rising rent, debt, job insecurity, and the cost of living are real stressors. Anxiety in these circumstances is a reasonable response, and practical help such as budgeting support or credit counselling may be just as important as emotional support.

Growing up with scarcity. If money was tight or unpredictable in childhood, or conflict often centred on money, you may carry a lasting sense that financial disaster is always around the corner, even when things are stable now.

General anxiety. For people prone to worry, money is often one of several topics the mind returns to. If you also feel on edge most of the time, money may be just one focus of a broader anxiety.

ADHD. Forgotten bills, late fees, and impulse purchases are common with ADHD, and the resulting stress can build into chronic money anxiety. This overlaps with making impulsive decisions you later regret.

Relationship tension. Different spending styles or unequal financial power in a relationship can make money a source of ongoing conflict and worry.

Low mood. Depression can make financial tasks feel overwhelming, leading to avoidance that makes problems grow.

When It Looks More Like Chronic Stress

When money worries are constant and long-lasting, they can keep the body's stress response switched on for months or years. That chronic stress affects sleep, concentration, mood, and physical health, and it can make it harder to take the very steps that would ease the financial situation.

Avoidance is one of the most common patterns. Not looking at accounts or opening bills brings short-term relief but tends to increase anxiety over time, because the unknown feels bigger than the reality. This is the same cycle described in Why Do I Avoid Situations That Make Me Nervous.

Signs that money stress has become chronic include:

  • Thinking about money most days, even when nothing new has happened.
  • Physical symptoms such as poor sleep, tension, or stomach upset linked to financial worry.
  • Avoiding financial tasks, then feeling guilty or panicked about the avoidance.
  • Feeling unable to enjoy spending, even on necessities or things you can afford.
  • Money arguments or secrecy in relationships.

What Can Help

  • Look, briefly. Set a short, regular time each week to check accounts and bills. Facing the numbers in small doses usually reduces anxiety.
  • Make a simple plan. Even a basic list of income, essential costs, and debts gives you something concrete to work with.
  • Automate what you can. Automatic bill payments and savings reduce the number of decisions and the risk of missed payments.
  • Get practical help. Non-profit credit counselling services and financial advisors can help with debt and budgeting.
  • Separate facts from fears. Writing down what is actually true about your finances, next to what you fear, can shrink catastrophic thinking.

Common Questions About Money Anxiety

Is it normal to feel anxious about money all the time?

Money is one of the most common sources of stress, especially with high living costs. Constant worry is worth addressing when it affects sleep, mood, relationships, or your ability to manage finances, whether or not your situation is objectively difficult.

Why do I feel anxious about money even though I'm financially stable?

Past experiences of scarcity, family messages about money, or a general tendency toward anxiety can keep money worries going even when your current situation is secure. The anxiety may reflect old beliefs more than present reality.

Why do I avoid opening bills and checking my bank account?

Avoidance offers quick relief from anxiety, but it usually makes things worse over time because problems grow and the unknown feels more frightening. Checking in small, regular doses tends to reduce anxiety.

Can ADHD cause money problems and financial anxiety?

Yes. Forgetting bills, impulse spending, and difficulty with budgeting are common with ADHD. The late fees and stress that follow can build into chronic money anxiety, which often eases with ADHD-friendly systems and support.

How does growing up poor affect money anxiety as an adult?

Childhood scarcity can leave a lasting sense that money could run out at any moment. Adults may overwork, struggle to spend on themselves, or feel constant financial dread even when they are secure.

Can financial stress affect my physical health?

Ongoing financial stress keeps the body's stress response active, which can contribute to poor sleep, headaches, stomach problems, and muscle tension. Addressing both the stress and the finances usually helps.

How can couples reduce arguments about money?

Regular, calm conversations about shared goals, clear agreements about spending, and understanding each other's money history can reduce conflict. Couples therapy can help when money arguments are frequent or intense.

Where can I get practical help with debt in Canada?

Non-profit credit counselling organizations offer budgeting help and debt management plans, and financial advisors can help with planning. Checking that an organization is non-profit and reputable before signing anything is a good idea.

Can therapy help with money anxiety?

Yes. Therapy can address the worry, avoidance, guilt, and underlying beliefs about money that keep anxiety going. It works well alongside practical financial planning.

What is financial avoidance?

Financial avoidance means putting off money tasks such as checking balances, opening bills, or budgeting because they cause anxiety. It is common and understandable, but it often allows problems to grow.
