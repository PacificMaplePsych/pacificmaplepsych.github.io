DBT skills for emotional regulation are practical techniques from dialectical behaviour therapy that help people understand their emotions, reduce vulnerability to overwhelming feelings, and change emotions that are not helping them. Key skills include naming emotions, checking the facts, opposite action, reducing vulnerability through self-care (the PLEASE skills), and building positive experiences (the ABC skills). DBT was originally developed for people with borderline personality disorder and chronic suicidal thoughts, but these skills help many people who feel their emotions are too intense, too fast, or too hard to control. They are learnable, and they improve with practice.

Why DBT Emotion Regulation Skills Help

Emotions carry useful information and motivate action, but when they are very intense or last longer than the situation calls for, they can lead to actions people regret, such as lashing out, shutting down, or self-harm. Some people are born with a more sensitive emotional system, reacting quickly and strongly and taking longer to return to baseline. Growing up in environments where feelings were dismissed or punished can make it harder to learn how to manage them. The broader picture is explained on the page about emotional regulation.

DBT emotion regulation skills work in three ways: understanding emotions so they feel less confusing, reducing the physical and life stress that makes emotions more intense, and acting in ways that shift unhelpful emotions. They are often combined with distress tolerance skills for crisis moments and mindfulness skills that build awareness. People with borderline personality disorder often find these skills especially helpful, though they benefit many others too.

Strategies to Try

1. Name the Emotion

Notice what you feel in your body, what prompted it, what thoughts came up, and what you feel like doing. Putting a precise name to the feeling, such as hurt rather than just upset, can reduce its intensity.

2. Check the Facts

Ask whether the emotion fits the facts of the situation. What actually happened? What am I assuming? Is there another explanation? If the emotion does not fit the facts, it may ease as you see the situation more clearly.

3. Use Opposite Action

When an emotion does not fit the facts or is not helpful, act opposite to its urge. If fear tells you to avoid something safe, gently approach it. If shame tells you to hide, stay connected. If anger tells you to attack, step back and be gentle. Doing this fully, including posture and tone, helps the emotion shift.

4. Reduce Vulnerability With PLEASE

Emotions are harder to manage when the body is depleted. PLEASE stands for treating physical illness, balanced eating, avoiding mood-altering substances, balanced sleep, and exercise. Small improvements here make a real difference.

5. Build Positive Experiences With ABC

Accumulate positive experiences daily, build mastery by doing something slightly challenging each day, and cope ahead by planning how to handle difficult situations before they happen.

6. Problem-Solve When the Emotion Fits

If the emotion fits the facts, such as sadness after a real loss, use problem-solving to address the situation, or allow the emotion without making it worse.

7. Pair With Distress Tolerance

In a crisis, regulation skills can be hard to use. Distress tolerance skills, such as cold water on the face, paced breathing, or grounding, help bring intensity down first.

Common Obstacles

These skills can feel awkward or mechanical at first, and they rarely work perfectly the first time. Practising when calm makes them easier to use under stress. Opposite action can feel very uncomfortable, since it goes against strong urges. Starting with small situations helps. Some people feel that checking the facts invalidates their feelings. The goal is not to dismiss emotions but to understand whether they fit the situation.

When emotions rise very quickly, people may move out of their window of tolerance before they can use a skill. Learning early warning signs helps. Self-care skills can feel hard to maintain during stressful periods. Focusing on one area, such as sleep, is a reasonable start.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

If emotions frequently feel out of control, if you use self-harm or substances to cope, if relationships are often in crisis, or if you have thoughts of suicide, professional support is important. Comprehensive DBT includes individual therapy, a skills group, phone coaching, and a therapist consultation team, and it has strong research support. DBT-informed individual therapy can also help. If you are in crisis, call or text 9-8-8 at any time.

Common Questions About DBT Emotion Regulation Skills

Which DBT skills help most with emotional regulation?

Core DBT emotion regulation skills include naming emotions, checking the facts, opposite action, the PLEASE skills for self-care, and the ABC skills for building positive experiences.

How does opposite action in DBT actually change an emotion?

Acting fully against an emotion's urge, such as approaching instead of avoiding when fear does not fit the facts, helps the emotion ease over time.

What does check the facts mean in DBT?

It means asking whether your emotion and its intensity fit what actually happened, separating facts from assumptions and interpretations.

What does the PLEASE skill stand for in DBT?

PLEASE covers treating physical illness, balanced eating, avoiding mood-altering substances, balanced sleep, and exercise, which reduce emotional vulnerability.

Are DBT emotion regulation skills only for borderline personality disorder?

No. DBT began with borderline personality disorder, but its emotion regulation skills help many people with intense or fast-changing emotions.

Why do DBT skills feel so awkward when I first try them?

New emotion regulation skills often feel mechanical at first. Practising in calm moments makes them more natural and easier to use under stress.

Can I learn DBT emotion regulation skills without joining a group?

Many people learn DBT skills through individual therapy or workbooks, though comprehensive DBT programs usually include a skills group.

What should I do when my emotions are too intense to use a DBT skill?

Use DBT distress tolerance skills first, such as cold water on the face or paced breathing, then return to regulation skills once intensity drops.

Does checking the facts in DBT mean my feelings are wrong?

No. Checking the facts helps you see whether an emotion fits the situation. When it does, the next step is problem-solving or allowing the feeling.

When should I look for a full DBT program instead of practising emotion skills alone?

If emotions often feel out of control, or you use self-harm or substances to cope, a comprehensive DBT program or DBT-informed therapy is recommended.
