How to Talk to Kids About Grief

If you or your child are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. Young people can contact Kids Help Phone at 1-800-668-6868 or text 686868. In an emergency, call 911.

Talking to kids about grief works best when adults use honest, simple, age-appropriate language, such as saying a person has died rather than "gone to sleep," answer questions as they come, and let children know that all feelings are okay. Children grieve too, but they often grieve in bursts, moving quickly between sadness and play. They may ask the same questions many times, act out, regress to younger behaviours, or seem unaffected and then react weeks later. These patterns are normal. With honest information, steady routines, and caring adults, most children cope well with loss over time.

Why Honest Conversations Help

Children notice when something is wrong. If adults avoid the topic, children often fill the gaps with their own explanations, which can be more frightening than the truth or lead them to blame themselves. Clear, honest information helps children feel safe and included. Euphemisms such as "lost," "went away," or "went to sleep" can cause confusion or fear, such as worry about going to sleep.

Children's understanding of death develops with age. Young children may not yet understand that death is permanent, while school-age children begin to grasp that it is final and may ask detailed questions. Teens understand death much as adults do but may hide grief or turn to friends. The broader grief experience is described on the page about grief and its stages.

Strategies to Try

1. Use Clear, Simple Words

Say "Grandpa died. His body stopped working, and he cannot come back." Avoid euphemisms.

2. Tell Them Soon

Share the news as soon as you can, in a quiet place, ideally from someone they trust.

3. Answer Questions Honestly

Give brief, truthful answers, and it is fine to say "I do not know." Expect repeated questions as children process.

4. Share Your Own Feelings

It is okay for children to see you sad. You might say, "I am crying because I miss her. It is okay to feel sad."

5. Name and Allow All Feelings

Children may feel sad, angry, scared, guilty, or confused. Help them name feelings and let them know none are wrong.

6. Reassure Them

Children may worry that they caused the death or that others will die soon. Reassure them that it was not their fault and explain who will take care of them.

7. Keep Routines

Predictable meals, bedtimes, and school help children feel secure.

8. Include Them in Rituals

Offer age-appropriate ways to take part in funerals or memorials, with preparation about what they will see. Let them choose, and have a trusted adult with them.

9. Remember Together

Look at photos, tell stories, draw pictures, or create a memory box. Special dates may bring grief back, as described on the page about grief during holidays and anniversaries.

10. Let Their School Know

Teachers can offer support and watch for changes.

Common Obstacles

Many adults want to protect children from pain, so they avoid the subject. Children usually cope better with honest information than with silence. Parents who are grieving themselves may struggle to support a child. Asking other trusted adults to help, and getting support for yourself, is okay. Some deaths, such as suicide, overdose, or violent deaths, are especially hard to explain. Age-appropriate honesty is still important, and professional guidance can help, as described on the page about grief after a sudden or traumatic death. Children's grief may also show up in behaviour rather than words, such as tantrums, clinginess, or trouble at school.

When Self-Help Is Not Enough

Consider professional support if a child's grief stays intense for a long time, if they withdraw, have ongoing sleep problems, struggle significantly at school, talk about wanting to die or join the person who died, or show signs of trauma such as nightmares or intense fears. The page about signs your child may need mental health support can help. Child grief counselling, family sessions, and grief groups for children can all help.

Common Questions About Talking to Children About Grief

How do I tell my child that someone has died?

Use clear, simple words, such as saying the person died and cannot come back, tell them soon in a quiet place, and let them ask questions about the death.

Why do child grief experts advise against saying a loved one went to sleep?

Euphemisms like went to sleep can confuse children about death and may make them afraid of going to sleep themselves.

Is it okay to cry in front of my child when we are grieving?

Yes. Seeing you sad shows children that grief is normal and that it is okay to express feelings about a loss.

How do young children understand death differently from teens?

Young children may not understand that death is permanent, while teens understand it like adults but may hide their grief.

Should my child go to the funeral?

Offer children a choice to attend a funeral, prepare them for what they will see, and have a trusted adult stay with them.

Why does my child seem fine one minute and upset the next after a death?

Children often grieve in bursts, moving between sadness and play, which is a normal way of processing grief.

What if my child thinks the death was their fault?

Reassure them clearly that nothing they thought, said, or did caused the death, since young children sometimes blame themselves.

How do I explain a death by suicide to a child?

Use honest, age-appropriate language about the death, answer questions simply, and consider professional guidance for explaining a suicide.

Should I tell my child's teacher about a death in the family?

Yes. Letting the school know about a death helps teachers offer support and notice changes in your child's grief.

When does a grieving child need professional support?

If a child's grief stays intense, they withdraw, struggle at school, talk about wanting to die, or show trauma signs, seek professional support.
