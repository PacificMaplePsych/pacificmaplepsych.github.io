What Is Caregiver Burnout

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If caregiving stress has led to thoughts of suicide, or you are worried you might harm yourself or the person you care for, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time, or call 911 in an emergency.

Caregiver burnout is a state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion that can develop when someone provides ongoing care for a person with illness, disability, dementia, mental health challenges, or aging needs. Many caregivers are family members who take on the role without training, pay, or adequate support, often while juggling work and their own families. Over time, the demands can outweigh the resources, leading to fatigue, resentment, guilt, and health problems. Caregiver burnout is common and not a sign of failure. Recognizing it early helps protect both the caregiver and the person receiving care.

What Caregiver Burnout Looks Like

  • Constant exhaustion, even after sleep, and feeling like you have nothing left to give.
  • Irritability, impatience, or resentment toward the person you care for, followed by guilt.
  • Withdrawing from friends, hobbies, and social activities.
  • Neglecting your own health, meals, or medical appointments.
  • Frequent illness, headaches, or sleep problems.
  • Feeling hopeless, trapped, or emotionally numb.
  • Anxiety about the future and the person's decline.

Caregivers often describe feeling invisible, as attention focuses on the person who is ill. Many feel they should be able to cope, and struggle to ask for help.

Why It Happens

Caregiving can involve long hours, physical tasks, medical coordination, financial strain, and emotional weight, often with little rest. When the person's needs are unpredictable or increasing, as with dementia or progressive illness, caregivers may feel constantly on call. Role changes, such as an adult child caring for a parent or a partner becoming a caregiver, can bring grief and strain relationships. Guilt about needing breaks, cultural or family expectations, and limited access to services all contribute. Caregivers also often experience anticipatory grief as they watch a loved one change, explored on the page about anticipatory grief.

Caregiver burnout tends to build gradually. Many caregivers only realize how depleted they are when their own health breaks down or they notice they no longer feel any warmth, just duty.

What It Is Not

  • Not a lack of love. Burnout reflects the weight of the role, not how much you care.
  • Not selfish to address. Caring for yourself makes it possible to keep caring for others.
  • Not the same as compassion fatigue, though they overlap. Compassion fatigue centres on the emotional cost of witnessing suffering.
  • Not depression, necessarily. However, caregivers are at higher risk of depression and anxiety, which may need treatment.
  • Not something to push through indefinitely. Without support, burnout can affect the quality of care and the caregiver's health.

How It Is Assessed and Supported

A doctor or psychologist can assess caregiver stress and screen for depression, anxiety, and health effects. Support usually involves both practical and emotional help: respite care, home care services, sharing tasks with family, caregiver support groups, and financial or workplace supports where available. Setting realistic expectations, protecting time for rest, and caring for your own health are essential. Therapy can help caregivers manage guilt, grief, resentment, and boundaries. Common Questions About Caregiver Burnout

What are the signs of caregiver burnout?

Signs include exhaustion, irritability, resentment followed by guilt, withdrawing from others, neglecting your own health, frequent illness, and feeling hopeless or trapped.

Why do caregivers feel guilty about needing a break?

Many caregivers believe they should be able to cope or that taking time for themselves is selfish. In reality, breaks help sustain the ability to care.

What is respite care?

Respite care provides temporary care for a loved one so the caregiver can rest, whether for a few hours, a day, or longer.

Are family caregivers at higher risk of depression?

Caregivers are at higher risk of depression and anxiety, especially with long hours, limited support, and a loved one's decline.

How is caregiver burnout different from compassion fatigue?

Caregiver burnout comes from the overall demands of caregiving. Compassion fatigue centres on the emotional cost of witnessing suffering. They often overlap.

Is it normal to feel resentful toward someone I care for?

Yes. Resentment is common when demands are heavy and support is limited. It does not mean you do not love the person.

How can I share caregiving with family members?

Make a list of tasks, have an open family discussion, assign specific responsibilities, and revisit the plan as needs change.

Where do BC caregivers find respite and peer support?

Yes. Many community organizations and health authorities offer caregiver support groups, education, and respite programs.

How can I protect my own health while caregiving?

Keep up with your own medical care, protect sleep, eat regularly, stay connected with others, and schedule regular breaks.

Can therapy help with caregiver stress and guilt?

Yes. Therapy can help caregivers manage guilt, grief, resentment, and boundaries, and find sustainable ways to continue caring.
