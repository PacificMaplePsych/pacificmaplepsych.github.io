Understanding Substance Use as a Coping Mechanism

Stopping alcohol, benzodiazepines, or opioids suddenly after regular heavy use can be dangerous. Speak with a doctor before cutting back. If you or someone else shows signs of overdose, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8.

Using substances as a coping mechanism means relying on alcohol, cannabis, or other drugs to manage difficult feelings or situations, such as stress, anxiety, sadness, loneliness, pain, sleep problems, or painful memories. It is a very common pattern. Substances can bring quick relief, which is why they are so appealing. Over time, though, they often make underlying problems worse and can lead to dependence. Understanding why people use substances to cope is an important step toward finding other ways to manage, without judgment.

What It Looks Like

  • Relying on alcohol to unwind after work.
  • Using cannabis to relax or sleep every night.
  • Drinking to feel comfortable in social situations.
  • Using substances to numb grief, trauma memories, or loneliness.
  • Needing more over time to get the same effect.
  • Feeling unable to cope without the substance.
  • Using when alone or hiding how much you use.

At first, the substance may seem helpful. Gradually, people may notice more anxiety or low mood the next day, poorer sleep, and difficulty facing problems without it.

Why It Happens

Substances act quickly on the brain, reducing tension, blunting painful emotions, or bringing pleasure. When relief follows use, the brain learns to associate the substance with feeling better, reinforcing the habit. Over time, the brain adapts, so more is needed for the same effect and people feel worse without it. People with anxiety, depression, ADHD, trauma, chronic pain, or high stress are more likely to use substances to cope, especially if they have few other strategies or supports. Social norms, such as drinking to relax, also make this pattern common.

The difficulty is that substances often worsen the problems they were meant to ease. Alcohol disrupts sleep and increases next-day anxiety. Cannabis can raise anxiety and lower motivation with regular use. Relying on substances can also prevent people from processing emotions or building other coping skills.

What It Is Not

  • Not a moral failing. Using substances to cope is an understandable response to pain.
  • Not always a substance use disorder. Coping use can progress to a disorder, described on the page about substance use disorder, but it does not always.
  • Not a long-term solution. Relief is temporary and often followed by worse symptoms.
  • Not separate from mental health. Addressing the underlying stress, anxiety, or trauma is usually key.

How It Is Assessed and Addressed

A clinician can explore what the substance is helping with, patterns of use, and any signs of dependence. Treatment often focuses on both the substance use and the underlying issue. Approaches include motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioural therapy, harm reduction, trauma-informed therapy, and building alternative coping skills such as exercise, relaxation, social support, and emotional regulation. Medical support may be needed for withdrawal. Many people find that as they develop other ways to cope, the pull of substances decreases.

Common Questions About Using Substances to Cope

Why do people use alcohol or drugs to cope?

Substances bring quick relief from stress, anxiety, sadness, or pain, which reinforces their use. Over time, they often make these problems worse.

Is drinking to relax after work a problem?

Occasional drinking may not be. It becomes a concern when you feel unable to relax without it, need more over time, or it affects sleep, mood, or health.

How do I know if coping with substances is becoming dependence?

Signs include needing more for the same effect, cravings, withdrawal symptoms, difficulty cutting back, and continuing despite harm.

Why does alcohol make anxiety worse the next day?

As alcohol wears off, the nervous system rebounds, increasing anxiety and disrupting sleep, which can leave people feeling worse than before.

Can trauma lead people to use substances?

Yes. Many people use substances to numb trauma memories or feelings. Trauma-informed care can address both the trauma and the substance use.

What healthier coping strategies can replace substance use?

Options include exercise, relaxation techniques, talking with supportive people, journalling, creative activities, and therapy to build emotional regulation skills.

What is harm reduction in the context of coping with substances?

Harm reduction focuses on reducing the risks of substance use, such as using less or more safely, without requiring immediate abstinence.

Should I stop using substances all at once?

For some substances, stopping suddenly after heavy use can be dangerous. Speak with a doctor about a safe plan before cutting back significantly.

Does treating anxiety or depression help reduce substance use?

Often, yes. When the underlying condition is treated, the need to use substances to cope may decrease.

What does therapy focus on when substances are used for coping?

Therapy explores what the substance is helping with, builds alternative coping skills, addresses underlying issues, and supports goals for reducing or stopping use.
