Understanding Minority Stress

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Minority stress describes the additional, chronic stress experienced by people who belong to stigmatized or marginalized groups. Beyond the everyday stresses everyone faces, members of minority groups may deal with prejudice, discrimination, stereotypes, exclusion, and the ongoing effort of anticipating or managing these experiences. The concept was developed by researcher Ilan Meyer to help explain higher rates of mental health difficulties in some communities. It has since been applied to many groups, including racialized people, religious minorities, people with disabilities, newcomers, and sexual minorities. Minority stress is not a personal weakness. It is a response to social conditions, and support can help.

What Minority Stress Looks Like

Researchers describe two broad kinds of stressors:

  • External stressors, such as experiences of discrimination, harassment, microaggressions, exclusion, or violence.
  • Internal stressors, such as expecting rejection, staying on guard, hiding parts of your identity, and absorbing negative beliefs about your group.

Common experiences include:

  • Feeling constantly watched or judged in certain settings.
  • Adjusting how you speak, dress, or act to fit in or stay safe.
  • Anxiety about coming out or disclosing aspects of identity.
  • Exhaustion from explaining or defending yourself.
  • Feeling isolated or caught between communities.
  • Anxiety, low mood, anger, or trouble sleeping.

Why It Happens

Minority stress arises from social environments rather than from the identity itself. When people are treated unfairly or face prejudice, the stress response is activated repeatedly. Even anticipating discrimination keeps the nervous system on alert. Over time, this chronic stress can contribute to anxiety, depression, substance use, and physical health problems. Concealing identity to avoid rejection can be protective in some situations but takes ongoing effort and can increase isolation. Internalized stigma, when negative messages from society are turned inward, can affect self-esteem.

Minority stress often intersects with other pressures, such as financial strain, migration, or family conflict. Newcomers, for example, may face acculturation stress alongside discrimination, as described on the page about acculturation stress. People who belong to more than one minority group may face compounded stress.

Protective factors matter too. Connection with community, pride in identity, supportive relationships, and access to affirming services can buffer the effects of minority stress. Many people find strength and resilience in shared experience with others who understand.

Minority stress can show up at work, at school, in health care, and even within families. A person may feel they must work harder to be taken seriously, avoid speaking up in meetings, or brace themselves before medical appointments. Young people may face bullying or feel they cannot be open at home. Recognizing these patterns as responses to real pressures, rather than personal shortcomings, can reduce self-blame and open the door to support.

What It Is Not

  • Not a flaw in the person. Minority stress reflects experiences of prejudice and exclusion.
  • Not something people should simply toughen up against. Chronic stress has real effects on health.
  • Not the same for everyone. Experiences differ across groups and individuals.
  • Not a diagnosis. It is a framework for understanding added stress and its effects.
  • Not all there is to identity. Many people also experience pride, belonging, and strength.

How It Is Addressed and Supported

Therapy that is culturally responsive and affirming can help people process experiences of discrimination, reduce internalized stigma, and build coping and resilience. It may involve validating experiences, exploring identity, strengthening connections with community, and addressing anxiety, depression, or trauma. Choosing a therapist who understands or shares aspects of your identity can make it easier to feel understood. Community groups, cultural organizations, and peer support can reduce isolation. Advocacy and collective action can also be empowering for some people. At a broader level, reducing discrimination and creating inclusive environments addresses the root causes.

Common Questions About Minority Stress

What is minority stress?

Minority stress is the extra, chronic stress experienced by members of stigmatized groups due to prejudice, discrimination, and exclusion.

Who developed the minority stress model?

Researcher Ilan Meyer developed the model, which has since been applied to many minority groups.

What are external and internal minority stressors?

External stressors include discrimination and harassment. Internal stressors include expecting rejection, concealment, and internalized stigma.

Why can repeated prejudice wear down wellbeing over time?

Chronic activation of the stress response can contribute to anxiety, depression, substance use, and sleep and physical health problems.

What are microaggressions?

Microaggressions are everyday comments or actions that communicate bias or stereotypes, often unintentionally.

Why is hiding part of my identity so exhausting?

Concealing identity requires constant monitoring and effort, which adds stress and can increase isolation.

What is internalized stigma?

Internalized stigma is when negative messages from society about your group are turned inward, affecting self-esteem.

What helps protect against minority stress?

Community connection, pride in identity, supportive relationships, and affirming services can buffer its effects.

How do I find a culturally responsive therapist?

Ask about experience with your community, approach to identity and discrimination, and whether they offer affirming care.

What does affirming therapy offer someone facing ongoing prejudice?

It offers a space to process experiences, reduce internalized stigma, and build resilience and belonging.
