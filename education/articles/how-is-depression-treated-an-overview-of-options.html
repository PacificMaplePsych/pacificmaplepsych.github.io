How Is Depression Treated: An Overview of Options

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 (Canada's Suicide Crisis Helpline) at any time. If you are in immediate danger, call 911.

Depression is treated with psychotherapy, medication prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist, or a combination of the two, along with lifestyle and practical supports such as physical activity, sleep, routine, and social connection. Most people with depression improve with treatment, though finding the right approach can take time. Mild depression often responds well to therapy and lifestyle changes. Moderate to severe depression often benefits from therapy combined with medication. For depression that does not respond to several treatments, other options are available. Treatment is tailored to each person's symptoms, history, and preferences.

The Main Treatment Options

Cognitive behavioural therapy. CBT helps people notice and change unhelpful thinking patterns and increase meaningful activities through behavioural activation. It has strong research support for depression.

Other evidence-based therapies. Interpersonal therapy focuses on relationships and life changes. Acceptance and commitment therapy helps people act on values despite difficult feelings. Emotion-focused therapy, compassion-focused therapy, psychodynamic therapy, and behavioural activation on its own also have evidence. Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy helps prevent relapse in recurrent depression.

Medication. Antidepressants are prescribed by a physician or psychiatrist and are often recommended for moderate to severe depression. It usually takes several weeks to notice benefits, and adjustments may be needed. Medication should not be stopped suddenly without medical guidance.

Lifestyle supports. Regular physical activity, consistent sleep, daily structure, and time with others can improve mood. The page about daily routines for mood stability offers ideas.

Other treatments. For depression that has not responded to other approaches, a psychiatrist may consider options such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation or electroconvulsive therapy. Light therapy can help seasonal depression. These are prescribed and monitored by physicians.

Group and peer support. Groups can reduce isolation and share coping ideas.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A plan begins with an assessment, as described on the page about how depression is diagnosed. Severity is a key factor. Mild depression may be treated with therapy and lifestyle changes alone, while moderate to severe depression often benefits from combined treatment. Co-occurring conditions, such as anxiety, substance use, ADHD, or bipolar disorder, shape choices. For example, a history of elevated mood changes how medication is approached.

Personal preferences matter. Some people prefer therapy, others medication, and many choose both. Past experiences with treatment, side effects, pregnancy or breastfeeding, other medical conditions, and practical factors such as cost and access are also considered. Plans are often adjusted based on how someone responds.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Recovery from depression is often gradual. Early signs of progress can include slightly better sleep, more energy, or small moments of interest or enjoyment, often before mood itself lifts. Therapy often shows benefits over several weeks to a few months. Medication usually takes a few weeks to begin working, with fuller effects over a couple of months.

Progress is rarely a straight line. Good days and hard days can alternate, and setbacks are common. They do not mean treatment has failed. If there is little improvement after a reasonable trial, it is worth talking with your care team about adjusting the plan. After recovery, continuing some treatment and creating a relapse prevention plan can help prevent depression from returning.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Small, steady actions help. Try to keep regular sleep and meal times, include some movement each day, and spend time with supportive people, even briefly. Break tasks into small steps and notice each one completed. Limit alcohol, which can worsen depression. Be honest with your therapist and prescriber about what is and is not working, including side effects. Treat yourself with patience. Depression affects energy and motivation, so doing less than usual is not failure.

Common Questions About Depression Treatment

If I tell my doctor I am depressed, what kinds of help might they offer?

The main options are psychotherapy, medication prescribed by a physician, a combination of both, and lifestyle supports.

Is therapy or medication better for depression?

Both can be effective. Mild depression often responds to therapy, while moderate to severe depression often benefits from both.

How long does it take for depression treatment to work?

Therapy often helps over weeks to months, and medication usually takes a few weeks to begin working.

Which types of therapy are used for depression?

CBT, interpersonal therapy, ACT, behavioural activation, emotion-focused therapy, and psychodynamic therapy all have evidence.

What if my depression has not improved with treatment?

Talk with your care team about adjusting the plan. Other options, such as different therapies or medications, may help.

Can exercise help treat depression?

Regular physical activity can improve mood and is often part of a treatment plan, alongside other approaches.

Should I stop antidepressants once I feel better?

Do not stop suddenly. Talk with your prescriber, who can advise on timing and tapering safely.

What are early signs that depression treatment is working?

Better sleep, more energy, or small moments of interest often appear before mood fully lifts.

How can I prevent depression from coming back?

Continuing some treatment, keeping healthy routines, and creating a relapse prevention plan can help.

What treatments exist for depression that does not respond to medication?

A psychiatrist may consider options such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation or electroconvulsive therapy.
