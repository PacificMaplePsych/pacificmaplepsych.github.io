Understanding Group Therapy for Grief

Group therapy for grief brings together a small number of people who are mourning a loss, guided by a trained facilitator such as a counsellor or therapist. Members share their experiences, listen to one another, and learn about grief and ways of coping. Many people find that being with others who understand reduces the isolation that often comes with loss. Friends and family may mean well but move on sooner, or avoid the topic, while a group offers a place where grief is expected and welcomed. Grief groups can complement individual counselling or stand on their own.

How Group Therapy for Grief Works

Grief groups rest on a simple idea: sharing loss with people who understand can ease its weight. Hearing others describe feelings like guilt, anger, numbness, or unexpected waves of sadness helps people realize their reactions are normal. Groups also offer practical ideas for getting through hard days and a sense of connection at a time when many people feel alone.

There are several kinds of grief groups:

  • Therapy groups led by a mental health professional, often with a set number of sessions and a structured focus on processing grief and building coping skills.
  • Support groups led by trained facilitators or peers, which may be ongoing and less structured.
  • Loss-specific groups for people grieving a particular kind of loss, such as the death of a partner, a child, a parent, a pregnancy, or a loss to suicide. The page about grief after a suicide loss explains why specialized support can matter.
  • Groups for specific people, such as young adults, parents, or caregivers.

Therapy groups often draw on ideas about how grief changes over time, including the understanding that grief does not follow neat stages, as described on the page about grief and its stages. They may include elements of cognitive behavioural, meaning-centred, or narrative approaches.

What Sessions Are Like

Most grief groups meet weekly or every other week for about sixty to ninety minutes. Therapy groups often run for a set number of sessions, such as six to twelve, with the same members throughout, which builds trust. Some support groups are drop-in. Groups may meet in person or online.

A first session usually includes introductions, sharing a little about the person who died, and agreeing on group guidelines such as confidentiality and respectful listening. Later sessions may focus on topics such as grief reactions, guilt and regret, anger, changes in identity, handling anniversaries and holidays, and remembering the person. Facilitators may use prompts, readings, writing, or bringing a photo or keepsake to share.

No one is required to speak before they are ready. Many people spend the first session or two mostly listening, and that is fine. Facilitators help make sure everyone has space and that conversations stay supportive. Some people find it emotionally tiring at first, and it can help to plan something gentle after sessions.

A good facilitator also watches for members who may need more than the group can offer, such as someone struggling with thoughts of suicide or grief that remains intense over a long time, and helps connect them with additional support.

Who It Can Help

Grief groups can help people who feel isolated in their grief, who have few people to talk to about the loss, or who want to connect with others who understand. They can be especially helpful after losses that others may not recognize or understand, as described on the page about disenfranchised grief. Research suggests group support can reduce distress and loneliness for many bereaved people, though experiences vary.

Group settings are not the best fit for everyone. People who feel very private, who have experienced a traumatic loss, or whose grief is severe and long-lasting may benefit from individual therapy first or alongside a group. Prolonged grief often responds well to specialized individual treatment. Some people try a group, find it is not right at the moment, and return later.

Common Questions About Grief Groups

What happens during a typical grief group meeting?

Members share experiences, listen to each other, and explore topics such as guilt, anger, and remembering, guided by a facilitator.

Do I have to talk in a grief group if I am not ready?

No. Many people listen for the first few sessions and share when they feel ready.

What is the difference between a grief therapy group and a support group?

Therapy groups are led by mental health professionals with a structured focus, while support groups may be peer-led and ongoing.

Are there grief groups for specific kinds of loss?

Yes. Some groups focus on losing a partner, child, parent, pregnancy, or a loss to suicide.

How long does a grief therapy group usually run?

Many run for six to twelve weekly sessions, while some support groups are ongoing or drop-in.

Will hearing other people's losses make my grief feel heavier?

It can feel intense at first, but many people find that shared understanding eases their sense of isolation.

Can I join a grief group online?

Yes. Many groups meet online, which can make them easier to attend.

When is individual grief counselling a better fit than a group?

After traumatic losses, or when grief is severe, very private, or long-lasting, individual support may help first.

How soon after a death can I join a grief group?

There is no right time. Some people join within weeks, while others wait months or years.

Is what I share in a grief group kept confidential?

Groups usually agree on confidentiality guidelines at the start so members feel safe sharing.
