What Is Dialectical Behaviour Therapy (DBT)

Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT) is an evidence-based form of cognitive behavioural therapy that helps people manage intense emotions, cope with crises, improve relationships, and reduce behaviours such as self-harm or impulsive actions. It was developed by psychologist Marsha Linehan, originally for people with chronic suicidal thoughts and borderline personality disorder. The word dialectical refers to balancing two ideas that seem opposite: accepting yourself as you are, and working to change. DBT teaches practical skills in four areas: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness. It has strong research support and is now used for many conditions involving emotional intensity.

How DBT Works

DBT is based on the idea that some people are born with more sensitive emotional systems, reacting quickly and intensely and taking longer to return to calm. When this sensitivity meets an environment where feelings are dismissed, criticized, or punished, people may not learn how to manage emotions and may turn to behaviours such as self-harm, substance use, or outbursts to cope. This is called the biosocial model.

DBT balances acceptance and change. Therapists validate a person's experiences and emotions, showing that they make sense, while also helping them build new skills and behaviours. The four skill areas are:

  • Mindfulness. Noticing thoughts, feelings, and sensations in the present without judgment. This is the foundation of the other skills.
  • Distress tolerance. Getting through crises without making things worse, using techniques such as changing body temperature, paced breathing, and distraction.
  • Emotion regulation. Understanding and changing emotions, as described on the page about DBT skills for emotional regulation.
  • Interpersonal effectiveness. Asking for what you need, saying no, and maintaining relationships and self-respect.

What Sessions Are Like

Comprehensive DBT usually includes four parts:

  1. Individual therapy, usually weekly, focusing on applying skills to personal challenges and targeting the most serious behaviours first.
  2. Skills group, usually weekly, which teaches the four skill areas in a class-like format with homework.
  3. Phone coaching between sessions, to help people use skills in real-life moments.
  4. A consultation team for therapists, which supports them in delivering effective treatment.

Comprehensive programs often last six months to a year. Many people also benefit from DBT-informed therapy, where an individual therapist teaches DBT skills without the full program structure. Diary cards are commonly used to track emotions, urges, behaviours, and skill use each day. Sessions are structured and collaborative, with a strong focus on commitment to goals.

Validation is a central part of every DBT session. Therapists look for what makes sense about a person's reactions, given their history and situation, before working on change. Many people say this is the first time they have felt truly understood, and that feeling makes it easier to try new skills.

Who It Can Help

DBT has strong evidence for borderline personality disorder, chronic suicidal thoughts, and self-harm. It is also used for eating disorders, particularly binge eating and bulimia, substance use, depression that has not responded to other treatments, PTSD alongside emotional dysregulation, and for teens with intense emotions, often with family involvement. It suits people who feel their emotions are overwhelming or who act in ways they later regret. The broader concept is explained on the page about emotional regulation.

DBT involves a significant time commitment, which may not fit everyone. People with milder difficulties may benefit from shorter skills-based work or other approaches such as CBT. Some people also need trauma-focused therapy once emotions and safety are more stable.

Common Questions About DBT

What does dialectical mean in dialectical behaviour therapy?

In DBT, dialectical means balancing ideas that seem opposite, especially accepting yourself as you are while working to change.

Who developed DBT?

DBT was developed by psychologist Marsha Linehan, originally for people with chronic suicidal thoughts and borderline personality disorder.

Which skills would I learn in a DBT skills group?

A DBT skills group teaches four areas: mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, and interpersonal effectiveness.

What does comprehensive DBT include?

Comprehensive DBT includes individual therapy, a skills group, phone coaching between sessions, and a consultation team for therapists.

Why do comprehensive DBT programs often run for six months or longer?

Comprehensive DBT often lasts six months to a year because it takes time to learn all four skill areas and practise them in daily life. DBT-informed therapy can be shorter.

What is a diary card in DBT?

A DBT diary card is a daily log of emotions, urges, behaviours, and skill use that guides individual DBT sessions.

Can DBT help if I do not have borderline personality disorder?

Yes. DBT is also used for self-harm, eating disorders, substance use, treatment-resistant depression, and teens with intense emotions.

How does DBT explain why some people feel emotions so intensely?

The DBT biosocial model explains emotional difficulties as a combination of a sensitive emotional system and an invalidating environment.

What is the difference between DBT and DBT-informed therapy?

Comprehensive DBT follows the full program structure, while DBT-informed therapy teaches DBT skills within individual sessions.

Can teenagers do DBT with their families?

Yes. DBT has been adapted for teens with intense emotions, often including family members in skills learning.
