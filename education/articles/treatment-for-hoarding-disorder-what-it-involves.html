Treatment for Hoarding Disorder: What It Involves

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Hoarding disorder is treated most effectively with cognitive behavioural therapy designed specifically for hoarding. This therapy helps people understand why possessions feel so important, build skills for sorting and decision-making, practise discarding and resisting new acquiring, and gradually make living spaces safer and more usable. Treatment is usually gradual and collaborative. Forced clean-outs, where others remove belongings without the person's involvement, often cause great distress and rarely lead to lasting change. With support, many people reduce clutter, improve safety, and feel less controlled by their possessions. Motivation can fluctuate, and treatment is designed to work with that.

The Main Treatment Options

CBT for hoarding disorder. This approach, described in more detail below, has the strongest evidence. It addresses the beliefs, emotions, and habits that keep hoarding going, and it is often delivered over around twenty-six sessions, sometimes longer. It builds on the principles of cognitive behavioural therapy.

Skills training. Organization, categorizing, and decision-making skills help people sort items and decide what to keep.

Exposure to discarding and not acquiring. People practise letting go of items and resisting the urge to acquire new ones, starting with easier items and building up. This gradually reduces the distress linked with discarding.

Motivational interviewing. Because many people feel ambivalent about change, this approach helps explore personal reasons for change without pressure.

Home visits. Many hoarding treatments include in-home sessions, since skills are practised where the clutter is.

Group treatment. Structured groups, including peer-led programs, can offer support and accountability.

Medication. There is limited evidence for medication specifically for hoarding, but a physician may treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, or ADHD.

Community and safety support. In cases of serious safety risk, such as fire hazards or blocked exits, collaboration with local services may be needed, ideally in a respectful, gradual way.

The condition itself is described on the page about hoarding disorder.

How a Treatment Plan Is Chosen

A clinician will explore how much clutter is present, how it affects safety and daily living, what kinds of items are saved, how acquiring happens, and how the person feels about change. They also screen for depression, anxiety, ADHD, trauma, and OCD, which often co-occur. Hoarding was once considered a type of OCD, and although it is now recognized as separate, some treatment elements overlap, as described on the page about how OCD is treated. Safety concerns shape priorities, since clearing exits and cooking areas may come first. Readiness for change, family involvement, and practical factors such as mobility or living situation also matter.

What Progress Usually Looks Like

Progress with hoarding treatment is usually gradual. Early changes may include clearing a pathway, making one room usable, or bringing fewer items home. Over time, people often find that discarding becomes less distressing and decisions come more easily. Research suggests many people experience meaningful improvement, though some clutter and urges may remain. Relapse is possible, especially during stressful times, so ongoing maintenance and support help. Progress is best measured by improved safety, function, and quality of life, rather than a perfectly clear home.

Supporting Your Own Progress

Practising sorting and discarding regularly, even for short periods, keeps progress moving. Setting rules for acquiring, such as waiting a week before buying, can reduce new clutter. Focusing on one area at a time avoids overwhelm. Noticing the thoughts and feelings that come up, and responding with compassion rather than criticism, helps. Involving a supportive person, who helps without taking over, can also make a difference. Ways families can help are explained on the page about helping a family member who struggles with hoarding.

Common Questions About Hoarding Disorder Treatment

Is there a type of CBT built specifically for people who hoard?

Yes. CBT designed specifically for hoarding disorder, including skills training and practice with discarding, has the strongest evidence.

Why do forced clean-outs not work for hoarding?

Forced clean-outs cause great distress for people who hoard and rarely lead to lasting change, since the underlying beliefs and habits remain.

Why does CBT for hoarding usually need around six months of sessions?

Sorting, discarding, and changing acquiring habits take repeated practice, so CBT for hoarding disorder often involves around twenty-six sessions, with gradual progress.

Why does hoarding treatment include home visits?

Home visits let people practise sorting and discarding where the clutter is, which makes hoarding skills easier to apply.

Is there medication for hoarding disorder?

Evidence for medication specifically for hoarding disorder is limited, but a physician may treat co-occurring depression, anxiety, or ADHD.

How does motivational interviewing help with hoarding?

Motivational interviewing helps people who hoard explore their own reasons for change without pressure, which is important when feelings are mixed.

What does progress in hoarding treatment usually look like?

Progress in hoarding treatment often starts with clearing a pathway or one room and bringing fewer items home.

Is hoarding disorder the same as OCD?

No. Hoarding disorder is recognized as separate from OCD, though they can co-occur and some treatment elements overlap.

How can I reduce acquiring new items while in hoarding treatment?

Setting rules such as waiting a week before buying, and noticing the feelings behind acquiring, can reduce new clutter.

What happens when hoarding creates a fire or safety risk?

When hoarding creates serious safety risks, clearing exits and cooking areas may come first, sometimes with help from local services.
