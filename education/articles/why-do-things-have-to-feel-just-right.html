Why Do Things Have to Feel Just Right

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

You straighten the cushions, then straighten them again. You rewrite an email because one sentence does not feel quite right, or you retie your shoes until the laces feel even. Sometimes you have to repeat an action, touch something, or rearrange items until you get a certain feeling of completeness. You know it does not really matter, yet walking away before it feels right leaves you uneasy, sometimes for hours.

Many people like things a certain way. When the need for things to feel just right takes up a lot of time, causes distress, or is hard to resist, it can point to something that deserves attention.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

Personal preference. Enjoying order, symmetry, or neatness is common and not a problem when it is flexible and does not cause distress.

Perfectionism. High standards can make it hard to finish tasks until they seem flawless, often driven by fear of criticism or of being never good enough.

Sensory preferences and autism. Some autistic people need things to feel a certain way because of sensory sensitivity or a strong preference for predictability, as described in Why Do I Need Everything to Be the Same Every Day. This usually feels calming rather than driven by fear.

General anxiety. When life feels uncertain, controlling small things can provide temporary relief.

Tic-related urges. Some people with tics feel a physical urge to do an action until it feels complete, which can overlap with just-right feelings.

When It Looks More Like OCD

In obsessive-compulsive disorder, some people experience what are called not just right experiences. There may be no specific feared disaster, just an intense sense of incompleteness or wrongness that feels unbearable until an action is repeated or adjusted. Others do have a vague fear that something bad will happen if things are not right.

Signs that OCD may be involved:

  • Repeating actions, sometimes a certain number of times, until they feel right.
  • Arranging, evening up, or touching things in specific ways.
  • Rereading, rewriting, or redoing tasks repeatedly.
  • Strong distress or discomfort if you are interrupted or cannot complete the ritual.
  • Rituals taking more than an hour a day or interfering with work, school, or relationships.
  • Recognizing the urge does not make sense, but feeling unable to resist it.

OCD can also involve repeated checking and unwanted intrusive thoughts. Just-right OCD is less talked about but very treatable.

What Can Help

  • Notice the pattern. Keep track of when the urge appears and what you do in response.
  • Delay the ritual. Waiting a few minutes before adjusting or repeating helps you learn the discomfort fades on its own.
  • Practise leaving things slightly wrong. Small, deliberate steps, like leaving a picture slightly crooked, build tolerance for incompleteness.
  • Avoid asking others to fix things. Having family arrange things for you can keep the pattern going.
  • Be kind to yourself. These urges are not a character flaw, and change takes practice.

Common Questions About Needing Things to Feel Just Right

Why do I need things to feel just right?

A strong need for things to feel just right can come from personal preference, perfectionism, sensory needs, autism, anxiety, tics, or OCD. It is worth looking at more closely when it causes distress or takes up a lot of time.

What is just right OCD?

Just right OCD is a form of OCD where compulsions are driven by a feeling of incompleteness or wrongness rather than a specific fear. People repeat, arrange, or redo actions until they feel right.

What are not just right experiences?

Not just right experiences are uncomfortable feelings that something is incomplete, uneven, or wrong. They are common in OCD and can drive repeating, arranging, and redoing behaviours.

Is needing symmetry a sign of OCD?

Liking symmetry is common and not a problem on its own. It may be OCD if the need is intense, causes distress when not met, leads to time-consuming rituals, and feels hard to resist.

How is OCD different from autistic need for sameness?

In OCD, rituals usually relieve anxiety or discomfort and often feel unwanted. Autistic routines and preferences tend to feel calming and meaningful. The two can co-occur, and an assessment can help clarify.

Why do I reread or rewrite things until they feel right?

Rereading and rewriting can come from perfectionism or anxiety, or be a compulsion in OCD driven by a feeling of incompleteness. If it takes a lot of time or causes distress, support can help.

Can just right feelings be related to tics?

Yes. Some people with tic disorders feel urges to repeat actions until they feel complete. Tics and OCD often occur together, and a clinician can help tell them apart.

How does exposure and response prevention work for just right urges?

Exposure and response prevention, a form of CBT for OCD, involves gradually facing situations that trigger just right urges while resisting the urge to fix, repeat, or adjust, so the brain learns the discomfort fades on its own.

How can family members help with just right rituals?

Family can help by gently not taking part in rituals, such as rearranging items or offering reassurance, while staying supportive. A therapist can guide families on how to do this.

Can just right OCD be treated?

Yes. Exposure and response prevention is effective for just right OCD, and many people see significant improvement. Medication can also help some people.
