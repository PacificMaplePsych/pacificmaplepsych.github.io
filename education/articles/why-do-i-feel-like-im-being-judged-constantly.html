Why Do I Feel Like I'm Being Judged Constantly

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Feeling like you are being judged all the time usually means your mind is scanning for signs of disapproval and assuming others are evaluating you more than they are. Most people are focused mainly on themselves, but anxiety and self-doubt make it feel as though you are always under a spotlight. This can come from past criticism, low self-esteem, or experiences of being treated unfairly. When the fear is intense and leads to avoiding situations, it may be social anxiety disorder, one of the most common and treatable anxiety conditions.

People describe feeling watched while walking into a room, worrying that coworkers think they are incompetent, assuming a friend's quiet mood is about them, or avoiding eating, speaking, or even making phone calls in front of others.

It Could Be a Lot of Things

The spotlight effect. Research shows people tend to overestimate how much others notice their appearance and behaviour. Everyone experiences this to some degree.

Low self-esteem. When you are critical of yourself, it is easy to assume others see you the same way. People who are hard on themselves often project that criticism outward.

Past criticism or bullying. Being judged harshly in the past can make you expect it everywhere.

Actual judgment. Sometimes people do face real judgment, such as discrimination or a critical workplace. That experience is valid and not a distortion.

Autism or neurodivergence. Autistic adults and adults with ADHD may have been corrected or misunderstood often, which can lead to feeling constantly watched. The page on feeling like you are performing in social situations explores this.

Depression. Low mood can make people interpret neutral reactions as negative.

When It Looks More Like Anxiety

Social anxiety disorder involves a persistent, intense fear of being judged, embarrassed, or rejected in social or performance situations. Signs include:

  • Strong anxiety in situations where others might observe you, such as meetings, parties, or eating in public.
  • Physical symptoms, like blushing, trembling, or sweaty palms before talking to people.
  • Avoiding situations or enduring them with intense discomfort.
  • Using safety behaviours, such as staying quiet, rehearsing, or avoiding eye contact.
  • Replaying conversations afterward and focusing on what went wrong.
  • Fear that has lasted months and affects work, school, or relationships.

Social anxiety keeps attention turned inward, on how you appear, which makes it harder to notice how others are actually responding. Safety behaviours and avoidance then prevent you from learning that feared outcomes are unlikely or manageable. Cognitive behavioural therapy targets exactly this cycle and has strong evidence for social anxiety.

What Can Help

  • Shift attention outward. In conversations, focus on what the other person is saying rather than monitoring yourself.
  • Test your predictions. Before a situation, write down what you expect others to think. Afterward, look at the evidence.
  • Consider other explanations. A frown may reflect someone's own day, not a judgment of you.
  • Drop safety behaviours gradually. Speaking up a little more or making eye contact briefly helps build confidence.
  • Be kind to yourself afterward. Avoid picking apart every interaction.
  • Remember the spotlight effect. Others are usually thinking about themselves far more than about you.

Common Questions About Feeling Judged All the Time

Why do I always think people are judging me?

Anxiety, low self-esteem, and past criticism can make the mind scan for disapproval. People also naturally overestimate how much others notice them, known as the spotlight effect.

What is the spotlight effect?

The spotlight effect is the tendency to overestimate how much others notice our appearance or behaviour. Research shows people pay far less attention to us than we imagine.

Is feeling judged all the time a sign of social anxiety?

It can be. Social anxiety disorder involves persistent fear of being judged or embarrassed, often leading to avoidance or intense discomfort in social situations.

Why do I feel like people are staring at me in public?

Anxiety turns attention inward and heightens self-consciousness, making it feel like others are watching. In reality, most people are focused on their own concerns.

Why does being teased at school still shape how watched I feel today?

Yes. Experiences of bullying or harsh criticism can leave lasting expectations of judgment, even in safe environments.

How do I stop worrying about what people think of me?

Focus outward on others, test your predictions, consider other explanations for reactions, and gradually face situations you fear. Therapy can also help.

What are safety behaviours in social anxiety?

Safety behaviours are actions people use to avoid feared outcomes, such as staying quiet or avoiding eye contact. They feel protective but keep anxiety going.

Is it possible that people really are judging me?

Sometimes, yes. Discrimination and critical environments are real. Therapy can help you tell the difference between real judgment and anxious assumptions, and respond to both.

Can feeling judged affect my work performance?

It can. Fear of judgment may lead to avoiding speaking up, over-preparing, or passing on opportunities, which can limit career growth.

What happens in cognitive behavioural therapy for fear of judgment?

Cognitive behavioural therapy helps people identify anxious predictions, reduce self-focus and safety behaviours, and gradually face social situations, building confidence over time.
