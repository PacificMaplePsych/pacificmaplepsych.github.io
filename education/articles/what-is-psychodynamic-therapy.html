What Is Psychodynamic Therapy

This page reflects our understanding of mental health topics as of 2026. If you are reading this at a later date, some details may no longer be current, so please verify anything important against up to date sources. If you notice anything that needs updating, we would welcome you letting us know.

Psychodynamic therapy is a form of talk therapy that helps people understand how feelings, memories, and patterns outside of everyday awareness shape their emotions, relationships, and choices today. It draws on ideas that began with psychoanalysis but has evolved into a more flexible, face-to-face approach. Sessions focus on open conversation, noticing recurring themes, and exploring how past relationships influence the present, including the relationship with the therapist. Rather than teaching specific skills, psychodynamic therapy aims for deeper self-understanding, which can lead to lasting change in mood, relationships, and sense of self. Research supports it for depression, anxiety, and several other concerns.

How Psychodynamic Therapy Works

Psychodynamic therapy is based on several core ideas:

  • Not everything is conscious. Feelings, wishes, and fears that are hard to acknowledge can still influence behaviour. Bringing them into awareness makes new choices possible.
  • The past shapes the present. Early relationships with caregivers often create templates for how people see themselves and others, as described on the page about attachment styles.
  • Patterns repeat. People often find themselves in similar relationship dynamics or emotional situations. Understanding why can loosen their grip, which is also explored on the page about individual therapy for relationship patterns.
  • Defences protect us. Everyone uses ways of avoiding painful feelings, such as keeping busy, intellectualizing, or pushing people away. Therapy helps people notice these and decide whether they still help.
  • The therapy relationship is part of the work. Feelings that come up toward the therapist, such as fear of judgment or a wish for approval, can reveal patterns that show up elsewhere, and can be explored safely.

Change comes through insight combined with emotional experience. Understanding a pattern intellectually is a start, but feeling and working through it in a supportive relationship helps it shift.

What Sessions Are Like

Psychodynamic sessions are usually less structured than skills-based therapies. You are invited to talk about whatever feels important, including current problems, feelings, dreams, memories, and reactions to the therapist. The therapist listens closely for themes, gently points out patterns, and asks questions that deepen exploration. There is usually no homework, though insights often continue to unfold between sessions.

Therapy can be short-term, often around sixteen to thirty sessions focused on a specific issue, or longer-term for deeper, long-standing patterns. Sessions are typically weekly, and some people attend more often. It can feel slower than structured approaches at first, but many people find the understanding they gain lasts well beyond therapy.

Who It Can Help

Research suggests psychodynamic therapy can help with depression, anxiety, some personality difficulties, somatic symptoms, and relationship problems, and that benefits often continue or grow after therapy ends. It suits people who want to understand why they feel and act as they do, who notice repeating patterns in relationships, or who have tried skills-based therapy and want to go deeper. It can also help with low self-esteem and chronic self-criticism rooted in early experiences.

Psychodynamic therapy may be less suitable as a first treatment for conditions where structured approaches have stronger evidence, such as OCD, panic disorder, specific phobias, or PTSD, though it can be combined with them. People who want quick, practical tools may prefer cognitive behavioural therapy or a blend of approaches. People in crisis usually need stabilization first.

Common Questions About Psychodynamic Therapy

How is psychodynamic therapy different from psychoanalysis?

Psychodynamic therapy grew from psychoanalysis but is usually face to face, less frequent, and more flexible and focused on current difficulties.

What does unconscious mean in psychodynamic therapy?

In psychodynamic therapy, unconscious refers to feelings, wishes, and fears outside everyday awareness that still influence how you feel and act.

Why does a psychodynamic therapist ask about my childhood?

Psychodynamic therapy explores how early relationships created templates that still shape how you see yourself and others today.

Is psychodynamic therapy supported by evidence?

Research supports psychodynamic therapy for depression, anxiety, and several other concerns, and benefits often continue after therapy ends.

Will a psychodynamic therapist give me exercises to do between sessions?

Usually not. Psychodynamic therapy relies on open conversation in sessions, though insights often continue between sessions.

How long does psychodynamic therapy usually last?

Short-term psychodynamic therapy often runs around sixteen to thirty sessions, while longer-term work may continue for a year or more.

Why would my feelings about my therapist matter in psychodynamic therapy?

In psychodynamic therapy, reactions to the therapist can reveal relationship patterns that show up elsewhere, and exploring them safely helps them change.

What are defences in psychodynamic therapy?

Defences are ways of avoiding painful feelings, such as keeping busy or intellectualizing, that psychodynamic therapy helps you notice.

Who is psychodynamic therapy a good fit for?

Psychodynamic therapy suits people who want to understand repeating patterns, feelings, and relationships at a deeper level.

Should I choose CBT or psychodynamic therapy?

CBT offers structured skills, while psychodynamic therapy focuses on deeper understanding. Your goals and concerns shape which fits better.
