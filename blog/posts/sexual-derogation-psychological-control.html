When Insecurity Becomes a Strategy: The Psychology of Sexual Derogation

Contributed by Patricia on August 23, 2026

Sex is one of the easiest places to make another person insecure. Tell someone they are selfish and they may argue with you. Suggest they are undesirable, inadequate in bed, or somehow responsible for a partner's body not responding, and the criticism tends to land somewhere much deeper. Sexual rejection reaches into desirability, attachment, body image, and the quiet fear that we could be replaced.

Most sexual feedback between partners is not manipulation. People are allowed to have preferences, lose attraction, ask for better communication, and raise genuine concerns. Sexual response itself is influenced by dozens of interacting factors, a subject covered in more depth in our article on psychogenic erectile dysfunction. Naming a real concern is not, on its own, a problem.

But there is another pattern worth recognizing: a sexually insecure person manages his own vulnerability by relocating it into his partner. Instead of I feel insecure, the message becomes you are undesirable. Instead of I am anxious about my sexual performance, it becomes you are causing my body not to respond. Instead of I need reassurance, it becomes you need to behave differently if you want me to desire you. When that happens repeatedly, sexual insecurity can become a form of psychological control.

Exporting the Insecurity

One useful way to understand this pattern is externalization. Something uncomfortable is happening inside one person, whether that is performance anxiety, fear of rejection, jealousy, or uncertainty about being desirable enough, and owning that feeling requires vulnerability. A psychologically healthier response sounds something like "I lost my erection and now I'm embarrassed," or "I'm having trouble staying present because I'm worried you're not enjoying yourself." The more defensive response searches for evidence that the discomfort actually belongs to the other person: "You don't turn me on enough," "I wouldn't have this problem with another woman," "If you acted differently, this wouldn't happen." The internal problem has now become an external accusation.

This mechanism is not unique to sex. Blaming, minimizing, and justifying have all been studied in the context of intimate partner abuse, and a 2024 systematic review found that these strategies can serve several functions, including protecting a person's own self-image and, in some cases, achieving interpersonal goals instrumentally. That does not mean every defensive comment is deliberate abuse. It does mean that repeatedly protecting one's own ego by transferring shame onto a partner can become genuinely damaging over time.

When Desire Becomes Conditional

A useful descriptive term for a related pattern is conditionalizing desire. It is not a formal diagnosis. It describes a relational process in which a partner increasingly communicates that his attraction, affection, or sexual functioning will remain available only if the other person behaves correctly. The conditions can expand quietly over time: be more enthusiastic, do not criticize me, reassure me more, do not raise anything difficult before sex, show me that you want me in exactly the way I need to see it.

Some of this sounds reasonable in isolation. Emotional connection genuinely matters, and enthusiasm during sex is a fair thing to want. The problem is not having preferences. The problem emerges when one person's physiological or emotional response becomes evidence of the other person's wrongdoing. At that point, a partner is no longer simply participating in sex. She is managing the conditions under which his desire is willing to exist.

The Cost of Performing Desire

This can create what might be called reassurance labour. There is an enormous difference between "I want to make sure you're enjoying this, can you tell me what feels good?" and a dynamic in which a partner comes to believe her visible enthusiasm is what keeps him aroused. In the second version, she may begin producing sexual signals partly to regulate his nervous system rather than to express her own. She wonders whether she is responding enough, whether her expression looks convincing, whether an earlier disagreement has ruined the encounter. She is no longer simply experiencing sex. She is monitoring herself having sex, which is close to the opposite of erotic absorption, and it can make authentic pleasure harder for both people.

Blaming a Partner for Erectile Difficulties Has Actually Been Studied

There is remarkably direct research on this specific pattern. A 2021 study published in Sexual Medicine surveyed over 13,000 women about their experiences with a male partner's erectile difficulties. Seventy-nine percent reported having experienced a partner losing an erection during sex. More strikingly, 14.7 percent, roughly one woman in seven, said a male partner had blamed her for it. Women who experienced that blame reported lower sexual satisfaction and were more likely to end the encounter, and in some cases the relationship, because of it. The authors linked male blame to worse outcomes for women's mental health, sexual satisfaction, and relationships overall. The erection itself was rarely what damaged the relationship. What the man did with the experience was what mattered.

"But I Don't Have This Problem With Other Women"

This particular statement can feel destabilizing precisely because it seems to settle the question. If his body works effortlessly with everyone else and only falters here, surely that proves something about her.

Not necessarily. Situational erectile difficulty exists precisely because sexual functioning can vary across partners, contexts, and emotional states. Perhaps there is a genuine incompatibility. Perhaps conflict affects arousal. Perhaps a particular relationship activates old insecurity. None of those possibilities require assigning fault, and none of them are settled by a single confident claim. There is a real difference between "something about our dynamic seems to affect me, and I want to understand it together" and "my body works perfectly, you're doing something that makes it stop." The first treats sexuality as relational information. The second turns physiology into a prosecution.

When Every Explanation Ends With "You Need to Change"

One reliable way to recognize this pattern is to stop examining each explanation in isolation and look instead at where they all travel. One week the stated problem is a lack of enthusiasm. Another week it is something she said. Another time it is a concern she raised, a request she made, or a way she reacted to something that hurt her. Each explanation may sound plausible on its own. But if every road eventually arrives at the same destination, that she needs to change so that his functioning will be fine, the cumulative structure matters more than any single complaint. A partner can begin to learn, without anyone stating it outright, that disagreement is followed by withdrawal and compliance is followed by relief. That is a form of conditioning, and it is where ordinary insecurity can shade into control.

This pattern also has a documented name in the research on how people try to keep a partner from leaving. Researchers studying mate retention behaviour have catalogued derogation, meaning criticism of a partner's appearance, desirability, or worth, as a recognized tactic in the Mate Retention Inventory, and have found it is used more often by people who perceive themselves as less desirable than their partner or who fear losing the relationship. Undermining a partner's confidence that she could do better elsewhere can function to reduce that risk, whether or not the behaviour is consciously strategic.

Sexual Shame as a Tool of Control

Sexual desirability is difficult to objectively verify, which is exactly what gives this kind of criticism its power. A person can confirm whether they are punctual. Nobody can confirm, with any real objectivity, whether they are attractive or exciting enough. Comments that exploit this ambiguity, suggesting a partner is unsatisfying compared with someone else, or that no one else would tolerate some trait of hers, are difficult to disprove precisely because they cannot be measured. That is often the point. The recipient may begin to wonder whether everyone else would notice the same flaw, and whether the person criticizing her is, in fact, the only one willing to stay.

This is one reason humiliation and belittling are taken seriously in the intimate partner violence literature. The World Health Organization includes insults, belittling, and psychological abuse within its description of intimate partner violence, and researchers have separately linked derogation and related manipulation tactics to a heightened risk of later aggression toward a partner. Coercive control is a broader term than any single hurtful comment, and it should not be applied casually to every difficult relationship moment, but the underlying mechanism, deliberately reducing a partner's confidence in order to make leaving feel riskier, is well documented.

Is This Gaslighting?

Sometimes, though the word is dramatically overused. A partner saying something unkind, or genuinely believing a sexual problem stems from incompatibility, is not automatically gaslighting. Gaslighting specifically involves someone knowingly distorting another person's perception of reality over time and encouraging her to distrust her own judgment. Deliberately inventing a sexual defect and repeatedly insisting that everyone else would perceive it too could function that way. Often, though, more precise words describe what actually happened: sexual degradation, devaluation, or negging, a term for backhanded criticism intended to lower someone's confidence and increase their desire for approval. Clinical language is only useful when it clarifies what happened, rather than when it is reached for as the most dramatic available option.

Not Every Difficult Sexual Conversation Is Manipulation

This distinction matters. A partner is allowed to say:

"I have some concerns about your hygiene, could you shower?"

"I'd enjoy more verbal feedback during sex."

"I'm having trouble getting aroused because we've been fighting."

"I think alcohol affects our sexual connection."

"I don't feel desired lately."

"I'm worried you're not enjoying this."

None of those statements is inherently unkind, and healthy sexual relationships depend on exactly this kind of honesty. The difference tends to show up in how responsibility is handled afterward. A healthy conversation gives both partners freedom and remains open to more than one explanation. A controlling one sounds less like a preference and more like a condition for continued desirability, and it is usually the same person doing the explaining every time.

What Healthy Handling Looks Like

An erection disappearing during sex does not need to become a crisis. A psychologically healthy response might be as simple as, "Well, apparently my body changed its mind for a minute. Come here, let's cuddle." Sex continues in some other form, or it does not, and nobody has failed. If the difficulty recurs, the couple can get curious together: performance anxiety, stress, alcohol, medication, insufficient stimulation, or something worth discussing with a physician. Sexual medicine increasingly favours exactly this kind of biopsychosocial approach, addressing physical, psychological, and relational factors together rather than searching for a single guilty party. What should not be necessary is a courtroom.

Freedom From Evaluation

Sexual safety is usually discussed in terms of consent, and consent is foundational. But psychological safety belongs in that conversation too. Good sex requires enough freedom to be unpolished: to laugh, to stop, to say something does not feel good, to have a body that occasionally does something inconvenient, without that becoming evidence against anyone. A partner's sexual response is information about a complex nervous system. It is not a grade assigned to someone's worth, and this dynamic can sit within a larger pattern of undermining a partner's confidence and independence, the kind we describe in our article on covert narcissism and emotional abuse. At the same time, people make mistakes, and someone who did not mean to cause harm can notice the pattern, offer a genuine apology, and learn to do things differently going forward.

Common Questions About Sexual Blame and Conditional Desire

What does it mean when a man blames a woman for losing his erection?

Sometimes he may genuinely perceive a relationship or sexual factor affecting his arousal, since psychological and interpersonal factors can influence erectile functioning. But repeatedly assigning responsibility to a female partner is different from collaboratively identifying contributing factors. Research involving more than 13,000 women found that approximately one in seven had experienced a male partner blaming her for his erection loss, and that blame was associated with poorer female sexual satisfaction and relationship outcomes.

Can erectile dysfunction be caused by relationship problems?

Relationship distress can contribute to erectile difficulties, particularly through anxiety, conflict, reduced intimacy, distraction, or performance pressure. Erectile dysfunction is generally best understood through a biopsychosocial model that considers physical, psychological, and interpersonal contributors together, rather than searching for one guilty person.

What is conditional sexual desire?

Conditionalizing desire is a descriptive relationship concept rather than a formal diagnosis. It refers to repeatedly communicating that sexual attraction, affection, or functioning depends on a partner complying with particular behavioural or emotional expectations. Preferences are normal. The concern is a recurring pattern in which sexual connection becomes leverage for behavioural control.

Is this the same thing as negging?

There can be overlap. Negging generally refers to insults or backhanded criticism intended to lower someone's confidence and increase their desire for approval. Sexual derogation can perform a similar function, but terms such as sexual shaming, devaluation, or coercive control may better describe more serious and sustained relationship patterns.

How can I tell whether sexual feedback is healthy or manipulative?

Consider what happens to your freedom after receiving it. Healthy feedback gives you information you can consider and remains open to more than one explanation. Manipulation makes you increasingly afraid to disagree, criticize, relax, or behave naturally, because doing so risks rejection, humiliation, or withdrawal.

A Final Thought

Sexual insecurity is human. Almost everyone wants to feel desirable to someone they love, and almost everyone will occasionally feel rejection, embarrassment, or the fear of not being enough. The question is what we do with that vulnerability. We can own it, or we can hand it to someone else and tell them it belongs to them. A man who loses an erection has not failed, and a partner whose response he blames has not failed either. If someone consistently leaves sex feeling that she needs to become more reassuring, more compliant, or more careful in order to preserve someone else's desire, the more useful question is rarely how to get better at managing him. It is why his insecurity became her job to manage in the first place.
