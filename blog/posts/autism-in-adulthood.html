Autism in Adulthood: What Late Diagnosis Means and What to Do Next

Contributed by Patricia on November 3, 2026

Can adults get diagnosed with autism in BC? Yes. There is no age cutoff on an autism assessment in British Columbia. A registered psychologist or psychiatrist with training in autism spectrum disorder can assess and diagnose an adult at any age, whether or not they were ever evaluated as a child. Adults are increasingly being diagnosed in their 30s, 40s, 50s, and later, often after being told for years that what they were experiencing was anxiety, burnout, or simply a difficult personality.

If you have spent years feeling like you were quietly translating a language everyone else seemed to speak fluently, or you recently watched a video or read a description of autism and felt an unsettling sense of recognition, this article is for you. A Quiet Shift in Who Gets Diagnosed, and When

For a long time, autism was thought of primarily as something identified in early childhood, usually in boys, often alongside significant developmental or intellectual differences. Recent data is telling a more complicated story.

A large 2025 analysis by Epic Research, which reviewed more than 338,000 patient records collected between 2015 and 2024, found that the median age of autism diagnosis in children has dropped, but unevenly. For boys, the median age fell from 7 to 5 over that period. For girls, it stayed flat at around 8. That gap widens further into adulthood: in 2024, 25 percent of newly diagnosed female patients were adults aged 19 or older, more than double the 12 percent seen among newly diagnosed male patients.

In other words, an entire generation of women, and a meaningful number of men, moved through childhood, adolescence, and early adulthood without a diagnosis that, in hindsight, would have explained a great deal. Many are now recognizing themselves in descriptions of autism only after years of unexplained social exhaustion, sensory overwhelm, or a persistent sense of being fundamentally out of step with everyone around them.

What Autism Can Look Like in Adults, Especially Without Intellectual Disability

Autism spectrum disorder is exactly that: a spectrum. Many autistic adults have average or above-average intelligence, hold jobs, maintain relationships, and would never be identified by the outdated stereotype of autism as an obviously visible disability. This is precisely why so many capable, high-functioning adults go undiagnosed for decades.

Some of the patterns adults commonly describe, once they start learning about autism in adulthood, include the following.

  • Chronic social exhaustion. A dinner, a meeting, or even a casual conversation can leave you needing hours or a full day to recover, in a way that seems out of proportion to what actually happened.
  • A lifetime of conscious performance. Many undiagnosed autistic adults describe having built, often unconsciously starting in childhood, an entire internal rulebook for how to make eye contact, when to laugh, how long to hold a conversation, and how to appear at ease. This ongoing effort, often called masking or camouflaging, is exhausting precisely because it never fully switches off.
  • Sensory sensitivity. Certain sounds, lighting, fabrics, or smells can feel disproportionately overwhelming or distressing, while other sensory input may be actively sought out or barely registered at all.
  • A strong need for routine and predictability. Unexpected changes to plans, schedules, or environments can produce a level of distress that feels out of scale with the actual change.
  • Deep, focused interests. A tendency to become intensely absorbed in specific topics or activities, sometimes to the point of losing track of time, that goes beyond an ordinary hobby.
  • Difficulty with unspoken social rules. Unstructured social situations, small talk, or reading between the lines of what someone actually means can require real, deliberate effort, even when the person is genuinely socially motivated and cares about their relationships.

None of these traits on their own indicate autism. What tends to distinguish an autistic adult is the combination, the lifelong pattern, and the degree of effort required to function in ways that come more automatically to non-autistic people. It is also common for autistic adults to have a co-occurring history of anxiety, depression, or burnout, which is often what actually brings someone into a therapist's office in the first place.

Why So Many Adults Were Missed the First Time Around

If you are wondering how something this significant could have gone unnoticed for thirty or forty years, you are not alone in asking that question, and there are real, well-documented reasons behind it.

Diagnostic criteria were built around a narrow picture

Early autism research and diagnostic criteria were developed almost entirely by observing young boys with more visible, higher support needs. For decades, this shaped clinicians' mental image of what autism was supposed to look like, which meant anyone who did not match that image, including most girls and many adults with strong verbal skills, was simply not on the radar.

Masking hides the very traits clinicians were trained to look for

Camouflaging, the learned strategy of consciously suppressing autistic traits and imitating neurotypical social behaviour, is more common among women and among people diagnosed later in life. It is an effective survival strategy in the short term, and it is also precisely what makes autism harder for others, including trained clinicians, to recognize from the outside.

Symptoms got relabelled as something else

A child who is overwhelmed, anxious, avoidant, or intensely focused on narrow interests is very often diagnosed with generalized anxiety, social anxiety, or simply described as shy, sensitive, or strong willed. These labels are not necessarily wrong, anxiety and autism frequently co-occur, but they can become the whole story when the underlying autism is never assessed for.

"You don't seem autistic" was often taken as reassurance, not as a clue

Adults who function well in school or at work, who make eye contact when it matters, or who have one or two close relationships were frequently told, by well-meaning teachers, doctors, and family members, that they could not possibly be autistic. In reality, functioning well in some domains, often at significant personal cost, is entirely consistent with autism and does not rule it out.

What an Adult Autism Assessment Actually Involves

In BC, an autism diagnosis for an adult must be made by a registered psychologist or a psychiatrist with relevant training and experience in autism spectrum disorder. A general practitioner can refer you, but typically cannot make the diagnosis themselves. A comprehensive adult assessment generally includes the following components.

  • Intake and developmental history. A detailed conversation about your early development, family history, education, relationships, work history, and the specific patterns that led you to seek assessment. Old report cards, childhood photos or videos, or input from a parent or sibling can be useful here, though they are not required if unavailable.
  • Standardized self-report and clinical measures. Questionnaires designed to capture autistic traits and experiences across the lifespan, alongside measures that screen for commonly co-occurring conditions such as anxiety, depression, or ADHD.
  • Direct observation. Many assessments include a structured observational tool, such as the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule (ADOS-2), which involves semi-structured activities and conversation designed to elicit and observe social communication patterns in real time.
  • Clinical interview and integration. The clinician weighs everything gathered, developmental history, self-report data, observation, and clinical judgment, against the diagnostic criteria in the DSM-5-TR to determine whether the full picture is consistent with autism spectrum disorder, another explanation, or both.
  • Feedback session and written report. You receive a written report summarizing the findings and a dedicated session to go over what the results mean, ask questions, and talk through next steps.

Wait times and cost vary considerably. Public, publicly funded adult autism assessment options in BC are limited and often carry long waitlists, which means most adults access assessment through a private registered psychologist or psychiatrist. It is worth asking directly about a clinician's specific experience assessing adults, and adult women in particular, since presentation can differ meaningfully from the childhood-focused training many clinicians received.

What the Final Report Can Actually Be Used For

A diagnosis is not just a label. In practical terms, a formal diagnostic report is a document you can use to request specific, tangible forms of support.

Workplace accommodations

Under the BC Human Rights Code, employers have a legal duty to accommodate employees with disabilities, including autism, up to the point of undue hardship. A diagnostic report gives you documentation to formally request accommodations such as written rather than verbal instructions, a quieter workspace or noise-cancelling headphones, flexible start times, permission to work from home on certain days, or advance notice of changes to schedules or duties. You are not required to disclose a diagnosis to every colleague, only to the extent needed to support your accommodation request, typically through HR.

CLBC and the Personalized Supports Initiative, with an important caveat

Community Living BC (CLBC) funds services for adults with developmental disabilities in this province, and through its Personalized Supports Initiative (PSI), it also supports adults with a confirmed diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder who need significant, ongoing help with daily living. This is a genuinely useful pathway for some late-diagnosed adults, but it is important to understand its limits clearly: an autism diagnosis on its own does not automatically qualify an adult for CLBC support.

To be eligible under PSI, an adult generally needs both a confirmed ASD diagnosis from a registered psychologist and a documented, significant adaptive support need, meaning substantial and ongoing difficulty managing tasks of daily living independently. This is assessed separately, often through an adaptive behaviour evaluation, and confirmed by a CLBC facilitator. Many adults diagnosed later in life, particularly those without a co-occurring intellectual disability, live independently, work, and manage most daily tasks without significant support, and will not meet this bar. That does not make the diagnosis less real or less useful; it simply means CLBC is one specific support stream among several, aimed at adults with substantial support needs, rather than a universal outcome of an adult autism diagnosis.

If CLBC eligibility feels relevant to your situation, the first practical step is contacting a CLBC facilitator directly, since they can walk you through both the Developmental Disability and Personalized Supports Initiative streams and what documentation each requires.

Other places a diagnosis can open doors

  • Disability Tax Credit (DTC) and Registered Disability Savings Plan (RDSP). A diagnosis can support an application for the federal Disability Tax Credit, which in turn can open eligibility for an RDSP.
  • Academic accommodations. Adults returning to school or upgrading credentials can use a diagnosis to access accommodations through a post-secondary institution's disability or accessibility office.
  • Access to autism-specific and peer community. Many adult autism support groups, both in-person in the Vancouver area and online, are open specifically to formally diagnosed or self-identified autistic adults.
  • Self-understanding. For many adults, the most immediate and significant use of a diagnosis is simply the internal shift it creates: a lifetime of experiences finally organized under a coherent, explainable framework, rather than filed away as personal failure.

A Final Thought

If you recognized yourself in parts of this article, that recognition is worth taking seriously. You did not fail to notice something obvious for thirty years. The systems built to identify autism were, for most of your life, not built to notice you.

A diagnosis will not rewrite your past. What it can offer is a clearer map going forward, language for the parts of yourself you have spent a lifetime managing quietly, and, in some cases, concrete access to accommodations and support that were simply never on the table before.

Common Questions About Adult Autism Diagnosis

Can adults get diagnosed with autism in BC?

Yes. There is no upper age limit on an autism assessment in British Columbia. A registered psychologist or psychiatrist with relevant training can assess and diagnose an adult at any age, regardless of whether they were ever assessed in childhood.

Why weren't so many autistic adults diagnosed as children?

Diagnostic criteria for decades were shaped around how autism tends to present in young boys with more visible support needs, which meant quieter presentations, especially in girls and in people who learned to mask or camouflage their traits, were routinely missed. Many adults now being diagnosed were instead labelled anxious, sensitive, or simply difficult as children, while the underlying autism went unrecognized.

What does autism look like in adults without an intellectual disability?

It often looks like chronic exhaustion after ordinary social interaction, a lifetime of consciously studying and rehearsing how to act in socially acceptable ways, sensitivity to sensory input such as noise or light, a strong need for routine, deeply focused interests, and difficulty with unspoken social rules, all while functioning well enough day to day that no one around the person suspected autism.

What does an adult autism assessment involve?

A typical adult assessment includes a detailed developmental and social history, standardized self-report measures, a clinical interview, and often direct observation using a structured tool such as the ADOS-2. The clinician integrates all of this against diagnostic criteria and provides a written report along with a feedback session to discuss what the results mean.

Does an autism diagnosis automatically qualify an adult for CLBC support in BC?

Not automatically. CLBC's Personalized Supports Initiative is available to adults with a confirmed ASD diagnosis, but eligibility also requires a documented, significant adaptive support need, meaning substantial difficulty managing daily living independently. Many late-diagnosed adults without a co-occurring intellectual or adaptive impairment will not meet this bar, though the diagnosis can still support workplace accommodations, tax benefits, and other forms of support.

Can an adult autism diagnosis help with workplace accommodations?

Yes. Under the BC Human Rights Code, employers have a duty to accommodate employees with disabilities, including autism, up to the point of undue hardship. A formal diagnostic report gives an employee documentation to request specific accommodations, such as written instructions, a quieter workspace, flexible scheduling, or reduced sensory demands.
