Laugh Your Way to a Happy Relationship

Contributed by Patricia on August 26, 2026

Every couple remembers the honeymoon period. The inside jokes that formed within weeks. The way an ordinary Tuesday felt worth mentioning. The sense that your partner's flaws were somehow endearing rather than exhausting.

Then life happens. Bills, schedules, in-laws, kids, and the accumulated small irritations of sharing a life with another person. Couples often assume this shift is inevitable, that lightness is simply a phase that biology and time wear down.

The research tells a different story. Decades of work from the Gottman Institute and related researchers have identified a set of habits, many of them surprisingly small, that predict which couples stay happy for the long haul and which drift apart. Humour and playfulness show up again and again in that research, not as a personality trait some couples happen to have, but as a set of practices any couple can build.

Here is what the research actually recommends, and how these habits work together to keep a relationship feeling less like maintenance and more like the honeymoon it once was.

Laugh At Yourself First

Couples who stay light together tend to share a specific skill: the ability to gently poke fun at themselves before pointing at their partner.

This is not self-deprecation that hides real hurt. It is a way of naming a flaw or a mood with enough affection that it stops being a weapon. A partner who feels criticism coming might say, only half joking, that they are about to go full spiky hedgehog for a minute. A partner who knows they are digging in on something small might admit they are being a stubborn goat about it.

Giving a recurring pattern a silly, shared nickname does two things at once. It signals self-awareness, which research on emotional intelligence links to healthier conflict, and it invites a partner to respond with amusement instead of defensiveness. Humour used this way is not avoidance. It is a pressure valve that keeps small frustrations from calcifying into resentment.

Couples do not need to be naturally funny for this to work. They need a habit of taking themselves a little less seriously than they take the relationship.

For couples who want a more structured way to build this habit, laughter yoga, a practice that combines simulated laughter exercises with playful breathing, has been studied for its ability to boost mood and connection even when the laughter starts out deliberately forced. Trying a class together, or simply setting a timer for a minute of exaggerated, silly laughing at home, can loosen up a couple who has fallen out of the habit of being lighthearted with each other.

The Magic Ratio: More Praise Than Criticism

Gottman's research on married couples found something specific and measurable: emotionally stable relationships maintain roughly five positive interactions for every negative one, even during disagreements. Couples heading toward separation, by contrast, often show a ratio close to one to one.

A feedback sandwich, softening a critique with something kind before and after, is a reasonable starting point. But the 5:1 ratio is really about the overall climate of the relationship, not just how conflict is packaged. It means noticing out loud when a partner made the coffee, made you laugh, or handled something stressful well. It means treating fondness and admiration as something to practice daily rather than something to save for anniversaries.

Couples who keep this ratio healthy are not avoiding criticism. They are making sure criticism lands inside a relationship that already feels safe and appreciated, rather than one running on empty.

Build and Update Your Love Maps

Gottman uses the term love map to describe the detailed, current knowledge each partner has of the other's inner world: their stresses this week, their evolving dreams, their favourite small comforts, what is worrying them that they have not said out loud yet.

Love maps fade quietly. A couple can go months assuming they know what is happening in each other's heads while actually running on an outdated picture. Rebuilding a love map does not require a formal ritual, though some couples enjoy Gottman's card decks designed for this. It can be as simple as asking genuinely curious questions on a regular basis and actually remembering the answers.

A detailed, current love map is part of what makes playful references and inside jokes keep working. It is hard to tease someone affectionately about something you have not noticed in months.

Turn Toward Bids for Connection

Gottman's research identified something called a bid: a small attempt to connect, a comment about the weather, a sigh, a joke, a request to look at something on a phone screen. Couples who stayed together over the following years turned toward these bids, responding with attention, roughly 86 percent of the time. Couples who later divorced turned toward bids only about a third of the time.

Turning toward a bid does not require a long conversation. It can be a glance, a laugh, a one-word acknowledgment. What matters is that the small bid does not land in silence. Over thousands of repetitions, these tiny moments of attention are what build the emotional bank account that makes a relationship feel warm rather than transactional.

Give Space, With a Clear Return Time

Stepping back during a tense moment is not the opposite of intimacy. Gottman's research on physiological flooding shows that once heart rate spikes past a certain point during conflict, the ability to listen and reason clearly drops sharply. Taking a break is often the more respectful choice.

Recovering from flooding takes at least twenty minutes. That is roughly how long it takes for stress chemicals like adrenaline and cortisol to clear from the body once they have surged. Crucially, it needs to be twenty minutes spent not thinking about the argument itself. Ruminating on the disagreement during the break keeps the same stress response active and resets the clock, which is why a real break usually means doing something else entirely, going for a walk, listening to music, or focusing on a separate task, rather than replaying the conversation in your head.

The difference between healthy space and stonewalling is communication. Asking for twenty minutes, or saying that you will pick the conversation back up after dinner, keeps the door open. Space without a stated return time can read as withdrawal or punishment, even when that is not the intention.

A simple script covers most situations: naming that you need space, and naming when you will be back to talk. That combination protects both people, the one who needs to self-soothe and the one who needs reassurance that the conversation has not been abandoned.

Show Humility and Grace

One of the more difficult habits to build is also one of the most protective: the humility to recognize that almost no conflict has a villain and an innocent party. Nearly every recurring dynamic in a relationship is co-created, even when the contributions are not equal.

Being the first to apologize is often framed as losing an argument. In practice, one sincere apology tends to soften a partner's defensiveness and make their own apology more likely to follow. Waiting to see who apologizes first usually just extends the standoff. Not every apology lands the same way, either, and small wording choices can be the difference between an apology that repairs and one that quietly reopens the wound, a distinction covered in more depth in our article on the anatomy of effective apologies.

Grace, in this context, means defaulting to the most generous believable interpretation of a partner's behaviour rather than the most damning one. A partner who forgot something is more often overwhelmed than uncaring. Choosing the kinder interpretation, when it is genuinely plausible, tends to be self-fulfilling: partners tend to rise or sink toward the way they are seen.

Let Small Things Go, On Purpose

The old advice never to go to sleep angry can backfire, especially late at night when both people are tired and less capable of reasoning clearly.

A more sustainable rule many couples adopt is a time limit: if something has not been resolved within a set window, a few days is common, it gets consciously let go rather than revisited indefinitely. Bringing up old, supposedly closed issues repeatedly, is one of the more reliable ways to make conflict feel like it is eroding the relationship instead of bringing two people closer together. Although some people may have a harder time with this, particularly if they struggle with rejection sensitive dysphoria or perseveration, learning when to stop bringing something up is a learnable skill for anyone.

Other common, destructive communication habits to avoid include:

  • Kitchen-sinking: dragging several unrelated grievances into a single argument so that everything and the kitchen sink gets brought up at once, and the original concern gets lost.
  • Cross-complaining: answering a partner's complaint with a complaint of your own instead of addressing what they actually said.
  • Yes-butting: appearing to agree at first, only to follow it with a "but" that quietly reverses the agreement and dismisses the point.
  • Mindreading: assuming you already know what your partner thinks or feels, and responding to that assumption instead of asking them directly.
  • Drifting off-beam: letting the conversation wander from topic to topic so that no single issue ever gets discussed long enough to resolve.
  • Gunnysacking: quietly stockpiling grievances over time instead of raising them as they happen, then unloading the entire backlog at once during an unrelated conflict.
  • Stonewalling: shutting down and withdrawing from the conversation entirely, which can feel neutral to the person doing it but reads as icy disapproval to a partner.

Letting something go does not mean pretending it did not matter. It means deciding, out loud if needed, that continuing to relitigate it costs more than it is worth, and then actually not bringing it back up later as ammunition.

Accept That Some Conflicts Are Not Meant to Be Solved

Gottman's research found that a large majority of ongoing relationship conflict, often cited around 69 percent, is perpetual rather than solvable. These are disagreements rooted in fundamental differences in personality, values, or needs: one partner is naturally tidier, one is more social, one needs more alone time to recharge.

Trying to permanently resolve a perpetual problem usually just produces the same argument on a loop. The more workable goal is learning to discuss it with humour and respect rather than escalating contempt, and finding enough shared meaning underneath the difference that it stops feeling like a referendum on the relationship.

Build Rituals of Shared Meaning

Gottman's research also points to the importance of shared rituals, small recurring habits that create a sense of "us." This can be a Sunday morning coffee routine, a specific way of saying goodbye each morning, a running joke that only makes sense to the two of you, or a weekly check-in about the relationship itself.

Shared humour is often one of the most durable of these rituals. Private jokes, recurring bits, and playful nicknames function as a kind of relationship shorthand. They are also one of the easiest habits to lose track of during a busy season and one of the easiest to deliberately rebuild.

Watch for Negative Sentiment Override

Sometimes accumulated, unresolved friction builds up over weeks, months, or years until a couple slips into negative sentiment override, a state where even neutral or well-meaning gestures from a partner start getting read through a negative lens.

Escaping negative sentiment override is not something a single conversation or a break can accomplish on its own. Truly restoring the positive sentiment buffer takes deliberate rebuilding of positive interactions. Gottman calls this the Magic 6 Hours, a framework of brief daily rituals of affection, appreciation, and connection. Restoring positivity also requires accepting influence from a partner and consistently using repair attempts and softened startups.

Making the Honeymoon Period Last

None of these habits are secret. Most of them sound almost too simple to matter: notice the good things, apologize first, ask better questions, keep a few inside jokes alive, know when to step back and when to come back.

What the research actually shows is that couples who keep the honeymoon feeling alive are not couples who avoided hard seasons. They are couples who kept practicing these small habits through the hard seasons, so that warmth, curiosity, and humour never had the chance to fully disappear.

Some seasons make this harder than others. A low mood, anxiety, or depression can make playfulness feel genuinely out of reach, not from lack of love but from lack of capacity, and individual therapy can help address what is underneath that. Other times, it is the relationship dynamic itself that has worn down the ability to extend humility and grace, and couples therapy can help rebuild the fondness, admiration, and repair skills these habits depend on.

The honeymoon period was never really about novelty. It was about attention, appreciation, and not taking each other for granted. Those are habits, not a phase, and they can be practiced again at any point in a relationship.

Common Questions About Humour and Long-Term Relationship Happiness

Does the honeymoon period always have to end?

The early flood of infatuation chemistry naturally settles, but the feelings underneath it, delight, admiration, and playfulness, are habits as much as they are chemistry. Couples who deliberately keep practicing fondness, humour, and repair tend to report a version of that early spark lasting for decades.

What is the Gottman 5:1 ratio?

Research from the Gottman Institute found that stable, happy couples maintain roughly five positive interactions for every negative one during conflict. The ratio is not about avoiding hard conversations, it is about surrounding those conversations with enough warmth, appreciation, and humour that the relationship can absorb friction without eroding.

Is it healthy to give a partner space during conflict?

Yes, as long as the space has a clear, communicated end point. Stepping away to self-soothe during flooding is a recognized part of healthy conflict management. Space without a stated return time can feel like abandonment or stonewalling, which is why naming when you will reconnect matters.

Should couples try to resolve every disagreement?

No. Gottman's research suggests that most ongoing conflict in relationships is perpetual and tied to personality or lifestyle differences rather than solvable problems. The goal is usually to manage the conflict with humour and respect rather than to permanently resolve it.

What if it is hard to laugh or see the best in a partner right now?

A low mood, anxiety, or depression can make playfulness feel out of reach, and individual therapy can help address what is underneath that. When couples are stuck in a cycle where it is hard to extend grace or humility to each other, couples therapy can help rebuild the fondness and admiration these habits depend on.
