Autism Funding in BC: What Families Need to Know After a Diagnosis

Contributed by Patricia on November 17, 2026

The short answer: after an autism diagnosis in British Columbia, most families are navigating three separate systems, not one. MCFD provides direct funding for children and youth up to 18 (currently up to $22,000 a year under age 6, and up to $6,000 a year from 6 to 18), school districts provide a separate track of support through a Ministry designation and an Individual Education Plan (IEP), and at 19 that funding stops and families need to have already started the process with Community Living BC (CLBC) if ongoing adult support is needed. None of these systems talk to each other automatically. Each one has its own application, its own documentation requirements, and its own timeline, and BC's funding system is also in the middle of a multi-year overhaul that families should know about. This article walks through each piece.

A note on timing: This article reflects the funding rules and amounts in place as of September 2026. BC is partway through a significant, multi-year change to how children's disability funding works (more on this below), so figures and processes may shift over the next two years. Where a detail could change, we've noted it, and we'd always recommend confirming current amounts and deadlines directly at gov.bc.ca before making a financial decision.

MCFD Autism Funding: The Basics

The Ministry of Children and Family Development (MCFD) runs the main funding program available to families of children and youth with an Autism Spectrum Disorder diagnosis in BC. There are two funding streams, split by age:

  • Under Age 6: up to $22,000 per year per child.
  • Ages 6 to 18: up to $6,000 per year per child, available until the month the young person turns 19.

This funding is meant to help pay for services that build communication, social-emotional, academic, and life skills, delivered by professionals listed on the Registry of Autism Service Providers (RASP), along with a smaller number of other eligible categories, such as family counselling. It is intended to complement, not replace, the support your child receives through their school. Families can also use up to 20% of their allocation for travel, training, and equipment (TTE) related to their child's programming.

How the application actually works

To apply, a parent or guardian needs a Basic BCeID, which is used to complete the online application through the Autism Funding portal. As of April 2026, the online submission process changed, so it's worth double-checking the current steps on the government site rather than relying on older instructions you may find elsewhere. The application itself asks for your child's diagnostic documentation, meaning the assessment report confirming the ASD diagnosis, along with the completed funding forms.

This is the piece that tends to catch families off guard: the diagnostic report and the funding application forms are two different documents, and the forms often need to be completed or co-signed by the diagnosing professional. At Pacific Maple Psych Centre, completion of the relevant MCFD funding forms is included as part of our psychological and autism assessment fee, so families leave the assessment process with both the diagnostic report and the paperwork MCFD needs, rather than having to track down a separate appointment or additional fee afterward.

A few practical notes that trip families up:

  • It's risky to start paying for services before funding is formally approved. If something is missing from the application, you may end up responsible for costs you expected to be covered.
  • Funding in the first year is often prorated based on your child's birthday, and in some cases the first funding period can be extended to as long as 19 months.
  • Families in the Kelowna, Prince Rupert, Terrace, and Smithers areas may go through a Family Connection Centre pilot process instead of the standard individualized funding stream. If you live in one of these communities, ask MCFD directly which pathway applies to you.

Important: BC's funding system is changing. In February 2026, the provincial government announced that the current Autism Funding program, along with the School Aged Extended Therapy (SAET) program for children with other disabilities, will be phased out and replaced by a new BC Children and Youth Disability Benefit and BC Children and Youth Disability Supplement. The current Autism Funding program continues unchanged and is accepting new applications until March 2027. Government messaging so far indicates the new Disability Benefit is intended to cover a broader range of diagnoses, not just autism, with amounts described as up to $6,500 or $17,000 per year depending on assessed need, and a separate income-tested Disability Supplement beginning in July 2027. MCFD has stated it will contact families directly about how the transition affects them. Because the details of eligibility, amounts, and timing are still being finalized, we'd encourage families not to make major financial plans around the new system until the specifics are confirmed, and to keep an eye on official updates from MCFD over the next 18 months.

Setting Up Your Child's Care Team

Funding is only the first step. Families then have to decide which services to start with, find providers, and coordinate everyone involved, often while still absorbing the diagnosis. A care team might include a speech-language pathologist for communication, an occupational therapist for sensory and daily living skills, a behaviour consultant and behaviour interventionist, and a psychologist or counsellor for anxiety, emotional regulation, or family support. (If anxiety is part of the picture, our education article on autism and co-occurring anxiety explains how it tends to show up.) It usually helps to choose one or two priorities first, based on what would make the biggest difference to your child's daily life, rather than trying to start everything at once. For a shorter, step-by-step overview, see our education article on what happens after your child is diagnosed with autism.

If you are still getting your bearings, What Is Autism Spectrum Disorder is a good plain-language starting point, and Signs Your Child May Need Mental Health Support can help you decide when to add emotional or behavioural support alongside developmental services.

Choosing providers who fit your child

Ask providers what goals they will work on, how they will measure progress, and how they involve parents. Look for approaches that build on your child's interests and respect how your child communicates. Your child should seem comfortable and engaged in sessions. Families can choose from behavioural, developmental, and relationship-based approaches, and many combine them. Our guide to understanding autism support options for children compares ABA, DIRFloortime, RDI, speech and occupational therapy, and counselling in more detail.

How funding rules differ by age

For children under 6, many services must be delivered by professionals on the Registry of Autism Service Providers (RASP), although family counselling from registered psychologists and other counsellors is also an eligible use. For ages 6 to 18, families have more flexibility, including social skills programs, tutoring, and family counselling. If you are weighing a group, see social skills groups for children and teens for what to look for.

School-Based Supports and IEPs

A diagnosis does not automatically translate into school funding or classroom supports. In BC, that requires a separate process through your school district.

Designation: the step that unlocks school funding

School districts receive additional supplemental funding for students in specific Ministry of Education "designation" categories, one of which is Autism Spectrum Disorder. To be designated, a student generally needs a documented ASD diagnosis from a qualified specialist (a registered psychologist, pediatrician, psychiatrist, or neurologist), and the school team needs to determine that the disability significantly affects the student's educational program. A diagnosis alone, without evidence of educational impact, does not automatically produce a designation.

The IEP itself

Once a student is designated, the school is required to develop an Individual Education Plan (IEP): a working document that sets out individualized goals, any adaptations or modifications to the curriculum, the specific support services being provided, and how progress will be measured. Parents are entitled to be consulted in developing it, and most districts hold IEP review meetings once or twice a year. It's worth understanding that in BC, the IEP is explicitly described in Ministry policy as a planning and pedagogical tool rather than a binding legal contract, which is a meaningful difference from special education law in some other jurisdictions. If a service outlined in the IEP isn't being delivered consistently, that's a conversation worth raising directly with the school team, and in some cases with the district.

For a full psychoeducational assessment (sometimes required for a designation, and generally the strongest evidence a school team can work from), public system wait times can run well over a year in many BC districts. This is one of the more common reasons families choose a private assessment: not to bypass the school system, but to get a usable report into the school's hands sooner.

Turning 19: CLBC and the Adult System

MCFD funding, along with most child-specific supports, ends the month a young person turns 19. From that point, the relevant system is Community Living BC (CLBC), a provincial crown agency that funds services for adults with developmental disabilities and, through a distinct pathway, adults with autism or Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder who need significant support with daily living.

The two eligibility pathways

CLBC has two separate eligibility streams:

  • Developmental Disability stream: generally requires evidence of a significant intellectual disability alongside significant adaptive limitations, documented before age 18.
  • Personalized Supports Initiative (PSI): the more relevant pathway for many autistic adults who do not also have an intellectual disability. This requires a confirmed ASD diagnosis from a registered practitioner, plus documented evidence of a significant adaptive support need, most often shown through an adaptive functioning or psychoeducational assessment.

A diagnosis by itself is not sufficient for the PSI pathway. CLBC specifically requires evidence that the person needs significant help with day-to-day tasks, which is a different and additional threshold from the diagnostic criteria used in childhood.

When to start

Because assessments, intake, and eligibility review all take time, and because CLBC facilitators tend to prioritize files closer to a young person's 19th birthday, most transition guidance in BC recommends starting the conversation between ages 16 and 17, sometimes as early as 16 for the initial eligibility intake. Waiting until 19 to begin often means a gap in support right at the point MCFD funding ends. If your family is already receiving MCFD support, that's a natural point to also start asking your MCFD worker or school transition planner about the CLBC process, rather than treating it as a separate task for a few years down the road.

Once approved, families are typically invited to a CLBC Welcome Workshop series, after which a facilitator works with the individual to assess support needs and build an individual plan. This is also frequently the point at which families explore the Persons with Disabilities (PWD) benefit through the Ministry of Social Development and Poverty Reduction, since CLBC eligibility can simplify that separate application.

Workplace Accommodation for Autistic Adults

For autistic adults who are working or entering the workforce, funding programs aren't the relevant tool: workplace accommodation is a human rights question, not a benefits application. If you are an adult exploring a possible diagnosis, our education articles on signs of autism in adults and what a late diagnosis means may be useful background.

Under the BC Human Rights Code, employers have a legal duty to accommodate employees with disabilities up to the point of undue hardship, a standard that is intentionally high. A request for accommodation does not have to be made in writing, and in many cases an employee does not need to disclose a specific diagnosis to receive accommodation, only evidence of a functional need. Where an employer does request documentation, they are generally entitled to information about functional limitations, restrictions, and recommended accommodations, but not to a full diagnostic workup or unrelated medical history.

In practice, the most useful accommodation letters focus less on the diagnostic label itself and more on:

  • Specific functional impacts relevant to the workplace (for example, sensory sensitivities, difficulty with unstructured verbal instructions, or the need for predictable routines).
  • Concrete, practical accommodation recommendations tied to those impacts (written instructions in addition to verbal ones, noise-reducing headphones, advance notice of schedule changes, a quieter workspace).
  • Whether the need is ongoing or time-limited, and whether any follow-up review is recommended.

Employers cannot simply refuse an accommodation request because it's inconvenient; they're expected to genuinely assess the request and explore reasonable options in good faith. If a request is refused, or documentation is repeatedly requested beyond what's reasonably needed, that may be worth raising with the BC Human Rights Tribunal, generally within one year of the incident. A well-documented functional letter from an assessing psychologist is often the piece that moves an accommodation conversation from vague to actionable for both employee and employer.

A Final Thought

Every part of this system, MCFD, the school district, CLBC, and the workplace, was designed separately, at different points, by different parts of government, and it shows. None of them automatically talk to each other, and none of them will proactively tell you about the others' deadlines. The single most useful thing most families can do is start early: apply for MCFD funding as soon as a diagnosis is confirmed, ask about school designation the same year, and open the CLBC conversation well before 19, even if it feels early. None of these systems reward waiting.

Common Questions About Autism Funding in BC

What is MCFD Autism Funding and how much can my child receive?

MCFD provides two funding streams for children and youth with a diagnosis of Autism Spectrum Disorder: up to $22,000 per year for children under 6, and up to $6,000 per year for youth aged 6 to 18. The funding helps pay for eligible intervention services and therapies, generally delivered by professionals on the Registry of Autism Service Providers, and is separate from the supports your child receives through school.

Is autism funding changing in British Columbia?

Yes. In February 2026, the province announced that the current Autism Funding program will be replaced by a new BC Children and Youth Disability Benefit and Disability Supplement, phased in through 2027 and 2028. The existing program continues unchanged and is accepting new applications until March 2027. Because details are still being finalized, it's worth confirming the latest information directly on the Government of BC website.

Does my child need a new assessment to apply for MCFD Autism Funding?

No new assessment is required if you already have a diagnostic report from a qualified professional following BC's autism assessment standards. What often confuses families is that the funding application forms are a separate step from the diagnosis, sometimes requiring the diagnosing professional's completion or signature.

How do I choose providers for my child after an autism diagnosis?

Start with one or two priorities rather than everything at once. Ask each provider what goals they will work on, how they will measure progress, and how they involve parents, and look for approaches that build on your child's interests and respect how your child communicates. For children under 6, most services must come from professionals on the Registry of Autism Service Providers, while family counselling from registered psychologists and other counsellors is also an eligible use. For ages 6 to 18, families have more flexibility, including social skills programs and tutoring.

What is an IEP and how is it different from an autism diagnosis?

A diagnosis alone doesn't automatically create school supports. A student generally needs a Ministry of Education designation in the Autism category, based on a qualifying assessment, before the district receives supplemental funding. Once designated, the school develops an Individual Education Plan (IEP) outlining goals, adaptations, and services, reviewed with parents at least annually. In BC, the IEP is a working planning document rather than a legally binding contract.

When should we start planning for CLBC, and what is the Personalized Supports Initiative?

CLBC supports adults aged 19 and older. For autistic adults without a co-occurring intellectual disability, the relevant pathway is usually the Personalized Supports Initiative (PSI), which requires a confirmed ASD diagnosis and documented evidence of a significant adaptive support need. Because intake and assessment take time, families are encouraged to begin planning around age 16 to 17, well before MCFD support ends at 19.

What documentation does an autistic adult need for workplace accommodation?

Under the BC Human Rights Code, employers must accommodate employees with disabilities up to the point of undue hardship, and a formal diagnosis isn't always required to request accommodation. Where documentation is requested, employers are generally entitled to information about functional limitations and recommendations, not a full diagnosis or unrelated medical history. A letter focused on specific functional impacts and practical recommendations tends to be the most useful document for everyone involved.
