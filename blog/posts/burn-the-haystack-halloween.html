Something Wicker This Way Comes: Burn the Haystack Before It Haunts You

Contributed by Patricia on October 25, 2026

Halloween is the one night a year people pay good money to be frightened. Dating offers the same experience year-round, free of charge. The real scare usually arrives slowly: you look up and realize you have spent six months, three group chats, and more than one 900-word text message trying to make a mediocre match work.

The Burned Haystack Dating Method (BHDM), developed by rhetoric professor Jennie Young and expanded in her book Burn the Haystack, offers a way out. Instead of combing through every straw looking for the needle, you set the haystack of dating options on fire. Filter early and firmly for incompatibility and poor communication, and whatever survives the flames is worth your time. The method was written for women dating men on apps, but its logic works for anyone tired of auditioning.

Why We Wait Too Long

Most relationships that end badly gave plenty of notice. The dynamic that finally breaks things at month six was usually there in week two, just smaller: the vague plans that never firm up until you take over, the joke at your expense, the “you’re being sensitive” on the third date, the apology that somehow ends with you reassuring them. Each one is easy to excuse on its own, but together they offer a warning.

We notice these moments, then we talk ourselves out of them, with plenty of help from ordinary psychology.

  • The halo effect. One shiny trait gets generalized to the whole person. A great jawline gets read as a great guy, a phenomenon researchers have nicknamed “what is beautiful is good.” Intelligence passes for emotional maturity, success for reliability, charm for kindness. None of these come bundled.
  • Intermittent reinforcement. Affection that arrives unpredictably hooks us harder than affection that arrives reliably, which is the same principle that keeps people at slot machines. A great date after a cold week feels like a jackpot, and it buys the other person another month of benefit of the doubt.
  • Sunk cost. “We have already put in eight months.” Time already spent cannot be recovered by spending more of it.
  • Potential. Falling for the trailer and staying for a film that never gets made. Our post on mothering men with unrealized potential covers where that road tends to lead.

Anyone can fall into these. Humans are reliably bad at revising a story they like, and small disappointments rarely feel big enough to justify rewriting it. That is precisely how they grow.

You Are the Casting Director

Conventional dating advice tends to coach people, especially women, to be less picky, less demanding, and generally less, all in the hope of being chosen.

You don’t need to audition for a role in someone else’s life movie. Try the opposite. You are the one deciding whether someone gets the part to be supporting cast in your life movie. If his profile reads like a list of instructions for the woman he will tolerate, if his opening message is a compliment with a dig tucked inside, or if weeks of chatting never turn into an actual plan to meet, you can stop right there. You already have your answer.

This is the method’s most freeing idea: you do not have to wait for a big red flag. Incompatibility and disrespect usually announce themselves early and small, in a line of a profile, a passive-aggressive joke, or a message that tests you and then backpedals. Dr. Jennie Young’s book on BHDM names dozens of these patterns and offers a simple test: translate it to real life. If a stranger said this to you in person, would it be acceptable? If not, it is hay. Nobody has to be a villain, and you do not need to diagnose anyone with anything. Read the Rhetoric

BHDM’s signature tool is applied rhetoric: reading what language reveals about values and power. Anyone can say they value communication. Watch what happens when you actually communicate something uncomfortable.

Clinically, the word to hold onto is pattern. One awkward text is noise. Five consistent ones are data. Watch for:

  • Accountability. “I was wrong” and “I am sorry you feel that way” belong to entirely different species.
  • How “no” lands. Does it end the discussion, or open negotiations?
  • Follow-through. Do promises turn into plans, and apologies into change?
  • Who does the work. If one person carries all the planning, remembering, and repairing in month one, expect the same split in year three.

Insight Without Change

Some people can give a beautifully self-aware account of why they withdraw, get defensive, or go quiet for a week. The account may be completely sincere, and the behaviour may continue exactly as before.

Emotionally intelligent people are especially prone to this trap. Once you understand someone’s history, it feels as though you should be able to fix the present. Understanding why something happens is a fine starting point, but the question that tells you more is simpler: “Is anything actually changing?” Compassion does not require you to become someone’s therapist, parent, or rehabilitation programme.

Skip the Closing Argument

You know the message. Several paragraphs, redrafted four times, pre-empting every counterargument, so fair and so clear that the other person could not possibly misunderstand. At some point it stopped being a conversation and turned into a legal brief.

If your messages keep getting longer while the relationship keeps getting worse, something has gone badly wrong, and the relationship is probably well past its expiry date. Be honest with yourself and you can likely trace it back. Somewhere long before the legal briefs, there were small moments that called for what BHDM calls “block to burn,” and you drove right past them. The relationship already died a while ago. Every long message is another round of chest compressions on a decaying corpse... spooky!

You do not need someone’s agreement to decide a relationship is not working. A short, clear decision will do: “I don’t think we’re compatible, and I don’t want to keep dating. I wish you well.”

The same goes for blocking, one of the method’s more controversial habits. Blocking is simply a practical boundary, with no moral verdict attached. When someone reveals themselves to be hay instead of a needle, you owe them nothing further.

Monster, Mismatch, or Merely Mortal?

Burning the haystack does not mean torching everyone who is nervous on a first date. It helps to sort what you are seeing into three bins.

  • Merely mortal. Awkwardness, a forgotten detail, a clumsy joke. Calls for patience and context.
  • Mismatch. Different answers on children, finances, monogamy, or commitment. Calls for an early, respectful exit.
  • Monster movie. Repeated boundary violations, blame-shifting, dismissive behaviour, passive-aggressive communication, or worse: abusive tendencies with perhaps even a history of criminal allegations. Calls for a cold stop with a block. Our post on early dating red flags goes deeper here.

Stop Resurrecting the Ghost

Some relationships become hauntings. Instead of dating the person in front of you, you keep trying to summon the one from the first month: the attentive texts, the long talks, the feeling of being understood.

That beginning may have been entirely genuine. Yet a person’s true capacity to maintain consistently healthy behaviours reveals itself over time. So ask yourself plainly: is the relationship available to me now one I actually want?

If the answer is no, go easy on yourself for not seeing it sooner. You decided with the information and hope you had at the time. Replaying it on a loop tends to slide into rumination, which keeps you stuck without teaching you much. Becoming suspicious of everyone would be the wrong lesson. The better one is learning to let evidence change your mind sooner.

When Dating Starts Haunting Your Mental Health

Consider support if you keep being drawn to relationships that leave you anxious, struggle to hold boundaries, blame yourself for other people’s behaviour, or spend more energy analyzing a relationship than living your life. Therapy can help you understand attachment patterns, see how earlier experiences shape present choices, and build enough self-trust to stay open to connection without abandoning your own needs.

A Final Thought

You are allowed to leave long before a relationship becomes a nightmare, and you can decide someone is not your person without first proving they are a monster. The little things were telling you something all along. Your time and emotional energy are finite, so be choosy about where they go. This Halloween, skip the haunted hayride. Light a match.

Common Questions About the Burned Haystack Dating Method

What is the Burned Haystack Dating Method?

It is an approach to dating developed by rhetoric professor Jennie Young. Instead of sifting through every possible match, you filter early and firmly for incompatibility and problematic communication, using applied rhetoric to read what profiles and messages reveal. Her book Burn the Haystack expands on the method.

Does the method mean rejecting anyone who makes a mistake?

No. It helps to separate ordinary imperfections, genuine incompatibilities, and concerning patterns. A nervous first date deserves patience, while repeated disrespect for your boundaries deserves an exit.

Is blocking someone after dating immature?

Not necessarily. Blocking is a practical boundary and says nothing about anyone’s worth as a person. It is especially appropriate when someone ignores a request for no contact or makes you feel unsafe. You do not owe ongoing access to someone because you once dated them.

How do I know when to stop giving someone another chance?

Ask whether the issue is a core incompatibility, whether the same behaviour keeps recurring, and whether raising it leads to real change. You are allowed to leave a relationship that is not right for you even if the other person is not malicious.

Can therapy help me stop repeating unhealthy dating patterns?

Yes. Therapy can help you understand attachment patterns, strengthen boundaries, reduce rumination, and build confidence in your own judgment, so you can stay open to connection without abandoning your own needs.
