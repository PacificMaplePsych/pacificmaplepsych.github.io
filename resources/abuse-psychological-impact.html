Psychological
Impact of Abuse

Abuse can leave visible injuries, but the emotional and psychological impact of abuse often lasts much longer than what other people can see. Many survivors search for answers about trauma, anxiety, depression, trust issues, low self-worth, and why life can still feel hard even after the abuse has ended. This page is here to help explain those effects and to remind you that healing is possible.

How Abuse Affects Mental Health

The psychological effects of abuse can be immediate or long-term. In many cases, the longer someone remains in an abusive situation, the more deeply it can shape their sense of safety, identity, and relationships. Abuse can change the way people think about themselves, other people, and the world around them.

Some survivors feel constantly on edge. Others feel numb, ashamed, hopeless, disconnected, or confused. Many people struggle with self-doubt because abuse often teaches them to question their own reality. These reactions are common trauma responses. They do not mean you are weak, broken, or beyond help. If you are still unsure whether what you experienced was abuse, the signs of domestic abuse and signs of reproductive abuse pages describe common patterns, and the sexual assault page explains consent.

Important reminder: the psychological impact of abuse is real, even if the abuse was minimized, denied, normalized, or never left obvious physical injuries.

Psychological Impact of Abuse on Children

Children who are experiencing or who have experienced abuse can face unique emotional, behavioural, social, and academic challenges. For example, they may:

  • Feel ongoing fear, anger, or mistrust toward the abuser and people who remind them of that person
  • Develop negative beliefs about themselves, such as feeling unworthy, powerless, or unlovable
  • Have intense emotional or behavioural outbursts
  • Struggle at school or have lower educational attainment
  • Have difficulty with friendships and become easy targets for bullying or isolation
  • Find it hard to trust others or build healthy relationships
  • Become more vulnerable to future abusive relationships
  • Develop anxiety, depression, trauma symptoms, or other mental health difficulties
  • Engage in risky or harmful coping behaviours

Adverse Childhood Experiences, often called ACEs, are stressful or traumatic events that happen before age 18, including abuse, neglect, and household dysfunction. These experiences can create toxic stress, which may affect emotional development, physical health, and long-term functioning.

At the same time, difficult childhood experiences do not guarantee a tragic future. Children can be remarkably resilient, especially when they receive support, stability, protection, and trauma-informed care. Healing and recovery are possible.

Long-Term Effects of Abuse in Adulthood

The effects of abuse can continue into adulthood whether the abuse happened in childhood, adulthood, or both. Survivors may struggle with self-esteem, boundaries, guilt, shame, fear, intimacy, and trust. They may find themselves drawn to familiar unhealthy patterns even when they consciously want something different. Some also feel a strong attachment to the person who hurt them, which is explained in our article on trauma bonds.

Abusive experiences can increase the risk of mental health concerns such as:

  • Post-traumatic stress symptoms or complex trauma
  • Depression
  • Anxiety
  • Panic symptoms
  • Chronic feelings of emptiness, shame, or fear
  • Difficulty regulating emotions
  • Relationship instability or difficulty trusting others

For some people, abuse also affects sleep, concentration, work performance, physical health, and their ability to enjoy life. Survivors sometimes blame themselves for these difficulties, but the problem began with what happened to them, not with some defect in who they are.

Some survivors turn to alcohol, cannabis, or other substances to numb fear, fall asleep, or get through the day, and some were pressured into using by the person who abused them. This is an understandable way of coping, but over time it can deepen anxiety, low mood, and sleep problems. Our articles on substance use as a coping mechanism and relying on alcohol to unwind may help, and support is available without judgment.

Can Abuse Change the Way You See Yourself and the World?

Yes. Abuse often shapes core beliefs. A survivor may come to believe things like “I am not safe,” “I cannot trust anyone,” “My needs do not matter,” or “I deserved what happened.” These beliefs can feel deeply true, especially when they were reinforced over time by fear, manipulation, criticism, humiliation, or violence.

Part of recovery is learning to recognize that these beliefs were shaped by abuse, not by objective truth. Healing often involves rebuilding a more accurate, compassionate view of yourself and creating healthier expectations for relationships and safety.

Healing After Abuse

The psychological consequences of abuse can affect the direction of a person’s life, but they do not make recovery impossible. Many survivors build healthy relationships, find meaningful work, raise safe families, and create lives that feel peaceful and fulfilling.

Healing may involve:

  • Speaking with a doctor or mental health professional
  • Building practical safety through an escape and safety plan
  • Using trusted community supports and abuse resources
  • Learning trauma-informed coping skills
  • Working on boundaries, self-trust, and self-worth
  • Exploring evidence-based support for depression, anxiety, or trauma

Healing is rarely linear. Progress can be uneven. But setbacks do not erase growth, and struggling does not mean you are failing.

Need support now? If you are in immediate danger, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8 any time. For confidential support related to abuse, call or text VictimLinkBC at 1-800-563-0808, 24 hours a day.

Post-Traumatic Growth After Abuse

Hopelessness is common after abuse, especially when someone feels that too much has already been taken from them. But surviving abuse does not mean your life is ruined or that you are permanently defined by what happened.

Many survivors do more than simply survive. Over time, some develop greater clarity, empathy, strength, purpose, and self-respect. This is sometimes called post-traumatic growth. It does not mean the abuse was good. It means healing can produce real change, depth, and strength.

Some survivors become deeply committed to protecting others, ending cycles of harm, or building the kind of life they once believed was impossible. Many people who have experienced abuse go on to create safe homes, healthy love, and meaningful work. Some even turn their pain into advocacy, leadership, or service.

If you have made it this far, you have already shown resilience. What happened to you matters, and so does what happens next. Recovery is possible, and your future does not have to look like your past.

Common Questions About the Impact of Abuse

What are the long-term effects of emotional abuse?

Emotional abuse can lead to lasting anxiety, depression, low self-esteem, self-doubt, difficulty trusting others, and trouble setting boundaries. Many survivors also notice changes in sleep, concentration, and physical health. These effects are common responses to prolonged stress, and they can improve with support.

Can abuse cause PTSD or complex PTSD?

Yes. Abuse can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder, and repeated or prolonged abuse, especially in childhood or in a close relationship, is associated with complex PTSD. Complex PTSD includes trauma symptoms along with ongoing difficulties in emotional regulation, self-worth, and relationships. Only a qualified professional can diagnose these conditions.

Why do I still feel anxious after leaving an abusive relationship?

After living with danger or unpredictability, the nervous system can stay on high alert even once you are safe. Being easily startled, scanning for threats, and feeling uneasy when things are calm are common. With time, safety, and support, the body can learn that the danger has passed.

Why do I miss someone who hurt me?

Missing an abusive partner is very common and does not mean the abuse was acceptable or that you want to go back. Cycles of harm followed by affection can create a powerful emotional attachment known as a trauma bond. Our article Understanding Trauma Bonds explains how this happens and how people break free.

Why do I blame myself for the abuse?

Abusers often shift responsibility onto the person they are harming, and over time that message can start to feel true. Self-blame can also feel safer than accepting that you had little control over what happened. Abuse is always the responsibility of the person who chooses to abuse.

Can children recover from abuse?

Yes. Children can be remarkably resilient, especially when they are safe, have at least one caring and stable adult in their life, and receive trauma-informed support. Early support makes a meaningful difference.

How long does it take to heal from abuse?

There is no fixed timeline. Healing depends on how long the abuse lasted, whether you are now safe, the support around you, and what other stressors you are facing. Progress is often uneven, and difficult days do not mean you are going backwards.

What kind of therapy helps after abuse?

Trauma-focused approaches such as trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive processing therapy, and EMDR have strong research support for trauma symptoms. A good therapist will also work at your pace, focus on safety, and help with self-worth and relationships. Pacific Maple Psych Centre offers trauma-informed individual therapy in Vancouver and virtually across BC.

Where can I get support right now?

If you are in immediate danger, call 911. If you are having thoughts of suicide, call or text 9-8-8, the Suicide Crisis Helpline, any time. VictimLinkBC offers free, confidential support for people affected by abuse 24 hours a day by phone or text at 1-800-563-0808.
