Signs of Elder
& Child Abuse

If you are trying to understand whether you are in an abusive relationship, please see the Signs of Domestic Abuse page.

This page is specifically focused on warning signs that a third party may recognize in a vulnerable elder, child, or adolescent. Many people search for signs of child abuse, signs of elder abuse, or how to recognize abuse in someone else. These lists are meant to help identify concerning patterns that may warrant immediate attention.

Important: in British Columbia, everyone has a legal duty to report if they believe a child or youth under 19 may be at risk, even if they do not work with children. Call Provincial Centralized Screening at 1-800-663-9122, 24 hours a day. The rules for reporting abuse of older adults are different, but you can still report concerns about an older adult and get guidance.

These lists are not all-inclusive. Additional signs not seen on this page may also indicate abuse. At the same time, no single sign proves abuse. Some signs can come from illness, medication, dementia, or developmental differences, so look for patterns and let trained professionals assess the situation. You do not need proof to make a report.

If you suspect child abuse in BC, call 1-800-663-9122. For concerns about an older adult, contact the adult protection program at your local health authority, or call the Seniors Abuse and Information Line run by Seniors First BC at 1-866-437-1940. Outside BC, contact your local child protection or adult protection service, or call the police non-emergency line for direction.

Signs of Elder Abuse

  • Fearful, anxious, poor eye contact, or unusually childlike speech
  • Hesitant to speak openly, paranoid, or ashamed
  • Unusual or new anger, sadness, or low self-esteem
  • Appears underweight, malnourished, or dehydrated
  • Inadequate self-care, physical hygiene, or oral hygiene
  • Has unwillingly signed over power of attorney or financial control
  • Possessions are missing or there is unexplained lack of money
  • Caregiver appears indifferent, angry, hostile, or controlling
  • Caregiver does not visit the elder or offer basic assistance
  • Caregiver is always present and answers for the elder
  • Broken dentures, hearing aids, glasses, or other devices
  • Complaints of genital irritation, pain, or itching
  • Areas of baldness or damaged hair from hair pulling
  • Bruises, welts, bites, burns, bloodshot eyes, or bedsores
  • Explanations for injuries are insufficient or inconsistent
  • Exaggerated or absent emotional response from caregiver regarding injuries

Financial abuse is one of the most common forms of elder abuse and is often committed by someone the older adult knows and trusts, such as a family member, caregiver, or person holding power of attorney.

If you are in immediate danger, call 911. For free, confidential support in British Columbia 24 hours a day, call or text VictimLinkBC at 1-800-563-0808.

If you are a young person and someone is hurting you, you can talk to Kids Help Phone any time at 1-800-668-6868 or by texting CONNECT to 686868.

Signs of Child or Adolescent Abuse

Note: “child” may also mean “adolescent,” and “parent” may also mean “caretaker.”

  • Parent ignores, fails to comfort, or actively rejects the child
  • Little to no positive reinforcement is provided
  • Child’s social, educational, or physical needs are ignored
  • Harsh, cruel, negative, or belittling comments are directed at the child
  • Toddler does not protest the parent leaving the room
  • Child appears to prefer other adults over the parent
  • Anxious, fearful, poor sleep, or unusual emotional reactions
  • Self-destructive behaviours, social withdrawal, stealing, or cheating
  • Complaints of genital irritation, pain, or itching
  • A non-parent adult becomes unusually close or shows favoritism
  • Child is made to feel special, receives gifts, or is told to keep secrets
  • Child has been threatened not to share details of a “special friendship”
  • Boundary violations or no safe private space, such as a bedroom
  • Parent spends excessive time with child, especially in the child’s room or at night
  • Very attentive parent paired with an apprehensive or fearful child
  • Child shows unusual sexual behaviour, jokes, or body language
  • Low self-esteem, changes in mood, or sudden decline in school performance
  • Areas of baldness or damaged hair from hair pulling
  • Bruises, welts, bites, scratches, scalds, or cigarette burns
  • Explanations for injuries are insufficient or inconsistent
  • Parent appears angry at or blaming the child or sibling for the injury
  • Exaggerated or absent emotional response from parent regarding injury

If a child tells you they are being hurt, stay calm, listen, and tell them you believe them and that it is not their fault. Do not press for details or promise to keep it secret. Write down what they said in their own words and report it as soon as you can. Children who have been abused can heal, especially with safe, supportive adults around them. The psychological impact of abuse page explains more.

Common Questions About Elder and Child Abuse

Do I have to report child abuse in BC?

Yes. Everyone in BC has a legal duty to report if they believe a child or youth under 19 may be at risk, not only teachers or health care workers. If the child is in immediate danger, call 911 first. Otherwise, call Provincial Centralized Screening at 1-800-663-9122, 24 hours a day. Learn more from the Government of BC.

What if I am not sure it is abuse?

You do not need proof to make a report. If you have reason to believe a child may be at risk, report your concerns and let a child welfare worker assess the situation. Investigating on your own can put the child at greater risk.

Will the family know I made the report?

Child welfare workers generally keep the identity of the person who reported confidential, and BC law protects people who report in good faith. You can ask the screening worker about confidentiality when you call.

What should I do if a child tells me they are being abused?

Stay calm, listen, and tell them you believe them and that it is not their fault. Do not press for details, promise to keep it secret, or confront the person they named. Write down what they said in their own words and report it as soon as possible.

What are the signs a child is being groomed?

Warning signs include an adult giving a child special attention, gifts, or privileges; spending time alone with the child; testing physical boundaries; and asking the child to keep secrets. Grooming often targets the family’s trust as well, so the adult may seem especially helpful or generous.

What is elder financial abuse?

Elder financial abuse is the misuse of an older adult’s money or property, such as theft, pressure to change a will, misuse of a power of attorney, or using their bank cards without permission. It is one of the most common forms of elder abuse and is often committed by someone the person knows and trusts.

Who can I call about elder abuse in BC?

If an older adult is in immediate danger, call 911. For information and support, call the Seniors Abuse and Information Line (SAIL) run by Seniors First BC at 1-866-437-1940. You can also contact the adult protection program at your local health authority.

Could these signs have another explanation?

Yes. Some signs, such as confusion, weight loss, falls, or changes in behaviour, can come from illness, medication, dementia, or developmental differences. No single sign proves abuse. Look for patterns, and let trained professionals assess the situation.
